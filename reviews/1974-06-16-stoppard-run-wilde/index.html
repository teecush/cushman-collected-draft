Jun 16, 1974

Stoppard Run Wilde

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Stoppard run Wilde

TOM STOPPARD is the kindest of playwrights. He has written with compassion of Rosencrantz and Guildenstern, and even (in The Real Inspector Hound) of theatre critics. These are all characters who watch while others act; and in Mr Stoppard's world the on-stage spectator invariably misses most of the game.

Take Henry Carr, the hero of his new play, Travesties (Aldwych). Carr was, historically, employed in the British Consulate in Zurich during the latter part of the First World War; resident in the same city at the same time were such revolutionary figures as James Joyce, Lenin, and the daddy of Dada, Tristan Tzara. Though he briefly brushed with Joyce, there is no evidence that Carr ever met either of the other two: a waste, when he comes to think of it, of two splendid opportunities. But of course, as he insists in his reminiscent old age, he might have met them, no reason why not; and Travesties, which is a play-within-a-monologue, is largely devoted to his imaginative substantiation of this hypothesis.

Carr's acquaintance with Joyce was not happy. The novelist, on his accustomed uppers, inveigled Carr into appearing as Algernon in a production of The Importance of Being Earnest, for which he served as manager and then refused to reimburse him for the cost of a pair of trousers purchased for the performance. The matter ended in the courts. It caused great bitterness, but it did enable Carr to creep--by the seat of his pants--into the footnotes of Joyceana, thence into Mr Stoppard's ken and, so, into the centre of his play.

Making the most of the few facts at his disposal, Mr Stoppard has magnified Carr's known preoccupation with his trousers into such a passionate dress-sense as might actually befit a Wildean dandy. From here the obvious next step--given Mr Stoppard's taste and talent for pastiche--is to mould much of the play in the style, and even the shape, of The Importance. Looking back on his Zurich youth, Carr sees himself, even before Mr Joyce's fateful talent-scouting, as being a natural Algy, languidly inquiring of his manservant as to the state of the war, the wine-cellar and the Russian Revolution and attempting to protect his sister Gwendolen from the attentions of his anti-artistic friend, Mr Tzara, who drops in regularly and, as it might be earnestly, for tea. Joyce figures in this scheme as Lady Bracknell, though when he appears the dialogue temporarily abandons the epigram for the limerick. The pun remains a constant factor.

The surface of the play is one to revel in, and beneath it, ideas are shaping. The theme is self-justification; the play represents Carr's strategy to impart a retrospective dignity and glamour to his life, and the distinguished shades he conjures up are all embarked on their own ego-trips. Carr, being an English gentleman, extolling art while distrusting artists, serves them as an excellent sparring-partner; he has his author's facility for seeing and espousing any side of any question (an aspect of the Stoppard sensibility to which I, like many critics, am deeply sympathetic). When not arguing with Carr, Tzara, who draws fragments of Shakespeare sonnets at random from his hat, can clash directly with the literary purist Joyce (occasionally referred to as Doris).

Or again any one of them can argue with Lenin, though none of them actually does. He exists in his own corner of the play, sealed off as in the famous railway compartment in which he entered Russia. We are into Act II; Carr has sloped off to the city library to encounter a girl named, naturally, Cecily. There he is, in mid-Bunbury, when suddenly Lenin's wife commandeers the stage, introducing her husband in excerpts from his speeches on the responsibility of the artist to the State.

One sees the relevance to the previous pronouncements made by Joyce and Tzara, and notes with wry amusement that Vladimir and Tristan would each stigmatise the other as bourgeois. But Mrs Lenin's recollections can have no place within Henry Carr's, and the play's fantastic structure splits: a fatal mistake which Mr Stoppard has never, to my knowledge, made before. We may momentarily welcome the weightier texture of the Lenin speeches, but when the play begins to run Wilde again we have lost track of the farcical issues. The two girls' encounter, to the rollicking, infallible ragtime strains of Mr Gallagher and Mr Shean ("Oh, Gwendolen . . . oh, Cecilee") and their duet would be a delight, if only we could figure out what they were on about. By the end, Peter Wood's production is assuming more euphoria than it is generating.

It is hampered by performances that are generally undistinguished and occasionally clumsy. The exceptions are Frank Windsor, ambiguously massive as Lenin and, providentially if quite foreseeably, John Wood, who gives several brilliant performances as Carr. There is the young consul basking in Swiss neutrality ("the entente cordiality of it"); the blushing amateur actor, seduced by appeals to the memory of his brilliant Goneril at Eton into essaying the part of Algernon (though he looks, to be truthful, as though his name were Ernest); and, when his memories are done, the final unwilling return to old age, his hands and feet still obstinately going through the motions of a swaying, decelerating foxtrot, as the grin of youth fades from his face.
