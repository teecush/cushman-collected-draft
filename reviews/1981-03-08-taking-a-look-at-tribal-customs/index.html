Mar 8, 1981

Taking A Look At Tribal Customs

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Taking a look at tribal customs

ROBERT CUSHMAN on plays by Mike Leigh, Brian Friel, Nigel Gearing and Arthur Miller.

IN ALL Mike Leigh's improvised plays he gets his characters socialising: formally as in Abigail's Party, or in the dismal drinking sessions of Ecstasy and Babies Grow Old.

Now we have Goose-Pimples (Hampstead), which begins with a dinner-party, progresses to the entertaining of a rich Arab by a girl croupier, and then proceeds to bring the two strands together, in a long second act during which hardly a dry breath is drawn by anyone.

It is obviously useful to Mr Leigh that wine loosens tongues. It can also--and in his plays usually does--loosen behaviour, and it gives scope for the satiric observation of the tribal customs. This is what Goose-Pimples is at. Vernon, in whose flat the play is set, has the effrontery to be a car salesman; as if that weren't bad enough, he takes obvious pride in the possession of a music-centre and in the obsessively neat serving of a meal that begins with melon and would go on to hotter things if only the supermarket didn't let him down.

His guests are Irving, in the same trade, and Irving's plump and heavy-braceleted wife Frankie, with whom Vernon is having an affair. Jackie the croupier is his lodger, and very respectable; however, her Arab, who knows only two or three words of English, thinks she is a call-girl and dutifully peels off wads of fivers when she offers him a drink from behind Vernon's very professional-looking bar.

This is a very funny moment, and the misunderstandings proliferate. In time, of course, the game of teasing the foreign national becomes ugly and we wonder apprehensively how much violence Mr Leigh and his team have in store for us.

Underneath it all we have a study in morality, or at least in mores. The Arab assumes he can buy sex, and spends much time closing doors, so that he may be alone with Jackie. But the two Englishmen are just as predatory, and far less generous; Irving never stops feeling up Jackie, while Vernon treats Frankie abominably and is making a subtle play for Jackie as well.

In fact what is being delivered is another one in the eye for social-climbing British hypocrisy. Improvisation is supposed to lead actors deeper into their characters; but here they seem to have passed judgement on them before the start of rehearsals. This is a far sprightlier piece than Ecstasy, but the blend of humour and compassion seems to have escaped Mr Leigh's work since Babies Grow Old six years ago.

Even while silently protesting, I laughed a lot. The standard of performance is extremely high. Jim Broadbent's Vernon is a continuous ooze, like toothpaste over rotten teeth, set off by Paul Jesson's clotted, contorted Irving, surviving on bogus confidence. Marion Bailey's Jackie sports an amazing accent (which involves offering callers a drink called `whisk-oy') seldom heard outside Mike Leigh plays, but is entertaining. Jill Baker finds desperation in Frankie as well as hard-faced greed, and keeps both within a comic convention. As for Antony Sher's Arab, words should fail me as they do him. His dialogue is limited to `okay,' a vocal shrug of uncomprehending resignation constantly repeated with infinite variation, and assorted slurs. Through all this he conveys lust, pain and finally dignity. There is no better acting in London.

*

Depths cannot save a play if its surface is boring; nobody is going to bother to look. The surface of Brian Friel's Faith Healer (Royal Court) is forbidding: four monologues, delivered in order by Patrick Magee, Helen Mirren, Stephen Lewis, and Mr Magee again. Mr Magee plays the title role, Mr Lewis his manager, and Miss Mirren his wife--or, as Mr Magee would have it, mistress; they are always contradicting each other, the point, I think, being less the fallibility of memory than the constant need to protect the ego.

The faith healer, endlessly touring the Scottish and Welsh hinterlands in a succession of one-night stands may, in his corrosive self-doubt and inhuman selfishness, stand for all performers, maybe for all artists. That at least is how his manager talks about him. But his trade is too specialised to function persuasively on that level, and I suspect that if rolled out into an ordinary play, with action and dialogue and suchlike adornments, Mr Friel's story would not seem wonderfully interesting.

The acting is good. Mr Lewis's gaping cockney gets some laughs, and Miss Mirren has a remarkable moment in which she remembers her father, a judge, and her face sheds years. Reference to the text confirms that Mr Friel has furnished them all with graceful sentences to speak, but also that he has chosen a dull method of telling us not much.

*

Not as dull though as Nigel Gearing's Snap (New End), which takes us through the life-story of Eadweard Muybridge, nineteenth-century photographer operating in San Francisco, who by his own account invented the moving picture. We see him at work with a trio of nude models. The male seduces one of the females, who happens to be Mrs Muybridge. Muybridge shoots his rival; at the murder trial his wife delivers a tearful speech, claiming that her husband has ushered in a new era in voyeurism, and prophesying a long new degradation for woman as sex-object.

Muybridge, from the look of things, knows all about photography, nothing about people. The look of things here is jumpy and joky, short scenes heavily strobed and linked by parody piano music. On this level Roland Rees's production is quite slick, but it does nothing to pump blood into the piece. Oliver Ford-Davies has some authority as Muybridge, and Colette Hiller is a treat to look at, in or out of clothes.

*

The performances in the National Theatre production of The Crucible (now at the Comedy) have grown in authority--notably Dave Hill's as the slimy Reverend Parris--but the improvement only points up the misconceptions in Bill Bryden's production. To praise a director for cutting the melodrama out of The Crucible is like congratulating him for leaving the farce out of Feydeau. Arthur Miller's account of the Salem witch-hunt is not melodramatic in its presentation of good and evil (he subsequently wished that it were), but in its shape and tempo; it builds to a thrillingly organised trial scene in which life-and-death debate gives way to fraudulent hysteria, and then to genuine hysteria as all pretence of judicial reason is destroyed.

Since hysteria is the play's historical climate, the method is perfectly valid, maybe inevitable. Mr Bryden gives us a play for, rather than about, Puritans; very slow, very level, very downbeat. In domestic conflict with his wife (Lynn Farleigh, strangely ineffective) Mark McManus as the accused farmer John Proctor displays a terrific battered stoicism, but in open court his vocal resources give out on him; he sweats blood for--as always--diminishing returns. The last act--Proctor in prison, torn between capitulation and intransigence--fades away, but then it always does; here Mr Miller's method does betray him and he is left with nothing to say, with postures but no characters.

The photograph illustrating last week's review of The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas showed Fred Evans as The Governor, not Henderson Forsythe as the Sheriff. Apologies to both actors.
