Dec 30, 1979

False Starts--Big Moments

Robert Cushman · The Observer

False starts--big moments

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Theatre in 1979--a year in which 'few talents have been at their best and some were absent altogether.'

The year 1979 was a bad one for new plays. I remarked on this at half-time and spent the balance waiting to be contradicted by events. Events did not oblige.

My initial outburst was questioned by one leading playwright, who wrote to me saying in effect that I hadn't seen everything, and therefore couldn't possibly know. He had a point (I missed out on the year's offerings from such notables as Snoo Wilson and Stephen Poliakoff), but my pessimism seems to have become contagious. The terribleness of the past year's work has become the current critical orthodoxy.

It has, in fact, been overdone. There have been interesting, even meritorious, plays around on various levels: David Lan's 'Sergeant Ola,' David Mercer's 'Then and Now,' Alan Drury's 'An Empty Desk,' Barrie Keeffe's 'Sus,' Peter Nichols's 'Born in the Gardens,' Roger Hall's 'Middle Age Spread,' Jonathan Gems's 'The Tax Exile.' But they have all been modest pieces, in scale as well as achievement, and it is not surprising that--despite Mr Lan's wit and imagination and Mr Gems's unusual funniness--the world in general remained unaware not only of their quality, but of their existence.

Only two plays actually impinged; and one can be grateful for their presence without feeling particularly enthusiastic about them. The two were Martin Sherman's 'Bent' and Peter Shaffer's 'Amadeus,' each distinguished by sensational subject-matter (a good thing, by the way) and a matching intensity of treatment, each disfigured and finally distorted by sentimentality and special pleading.

Mr Shaffer, after an infinity of false starts, began to develop a riveting case history of artistic jealousy which he proceeded to throw away in what must have seemed higher interests: offering us, instead of Salieri v. Mozart, Salieri v. God, God refusing to put in a personal appearance and thus breaking the back of the play's second act in which endless recitals of Salieri's grievances alternate with chronicle-play enactments of Mozart's last years, these being necessary to the story-line, but irrelevant to the theme.

However, the announcement of that theme at the half-way mark is as thrilling a long speech as has been heard in the theatre since 'Kennedy's Children'; so at least it seems on the lips of Paul Scofield, whose performance--camp comedy rising to gnarled grandeur--is his most entertaining in years, strongly recalling (like the play itself) John Wood's nostalgic tour-de-force in 'Travesties.' Felicity Kendal gives her earthiest and most delightful performance to date as Mrs Mozart, and Simon Callow copes well with the Shaffer-Salieri idea of Mozart as a foul-mouthed infant: an idea which I found perfectly credible (if that matters), but somewhat monotonous. The big moments apart, the most apposite and attractive thing about the play is its operatic structure, with which Peter Hall's production is perfectly at home.

The most encouraging thing about 'Bent' is that it secured a transfer to the West End and stayed there, attracting the young audience which is supposed to have abandoned the commercial theatre ages ago. It suggested that there may yet be a future for producers in putting on plays in which they might conceivably believe--or at least that they may as well go down fighting. With these two plays I should really bracket a third, and a more substantial, piece than either of them, David Edgar's 'Mary Barnes'; it belongs for me to 1978, when it opened in Birmingham. But its arrival in London brought us a stunning performance from Patti Love, ranking with that other Midland import, Jane Lapotaire's Piaf.

The Roundhouse did London a favour by exhibiting three productions from the Manchester Royal Exchange, the best of which ('The Family Reunion') proved another surprise tonic for the West End. The Half Moon, outstanding among fringe theatres, brought us some healthy blasts from the past. I have already mentioned Court; the revivals of three shows at the Royal ('Happy Days' and 'The London Cuckolds') were other credits. The record was patchier than in the preceding year, but since some Sloane Square heroes of the distant past seem to have a vested interest in disparaging the theatre's present, I had better re-state the plain truth, which is that the regime of Stuart Burge and Max Stafford-Clark is the most productive the place has known since the death of George Devine.

The National Theatre, having begun with a brace of disappointments, had a strike which acted, quite by coincidence, as a buffer: nearly everything that happened after it was a distinct improvement on what had gone before. If 'Richard III' was possibly the year's worst Shakespeare production, 'As You Like It' was probably the best. (Nothing at Stratford was as searching or as consistent.) 'Undiscovered Country' was rich.

Michael Rudman's double directorial debut brought fine ensemble work in 'For Services Rendered' (Maugham was actually the playwright of the year) and a flawless solo from Warren Mitchell in 'Death of a Salesman.' John Standing and Anna Massey did comparable work in Simon Gray's fairish 'Close of Play.' The Cottesloe did nothing historic, but nothing bad either, and kept a recognisable company together.

The RSC was weakest at the Warehouse, where the new plays merged into one another, and on the main stage at Stratford, where nothing (except John Woodvine's Malvolio, and maybe the 'Othello,' which I missed) caught fire. Still, the 1978 season hadn't looked too grand in its time, and those shows were mostly splendid when shunted down to the Aldwych, where they were rounded out by the joyous splurge of 'Once in a Lifetime.' The equation was balanced by an excellent year's work at Stratford's Other Place.

Here's a thing: it was a good year for musicals. And not only the old American behemoths, though 'My Fair Lady,' with magic enhanced, was an evening to rank among the thrills of the year (along with 'Lifetime' and 'Salesman'--the last the only one not choreographed by Gillian Lynne). 'Ain't Misbehavin'' was a pleasure; I hated 'Chicago,' but was glad to see it, and even to see it survive; even 'Flowers for Algernon' had its points; 'Hello Dolly' boomeranged on me; I wouldn't have thought that my description of Carol Channing's act as 'one long curtain-call' could have been taken as a compliment, but there it is outside the theatre.

The cheering occasions, though, were small and native: 'Songbook' and 'A Night in the Ukraine' and 'Fearless Frank' and 'Talent' from Victoria Wood, whose own extends in so many directions it's unfair.

Once you start cataloguing particular joys the landscape brightens. But don't be fooled. Few talents have been at their best, and some were absent altogether. That could be chance. But it makes this the gloomiest time imaginable at which to contemplate further financial cuts. These will not result in inferior work being squeezed out. Bad plays will continue to be written and--choosing being the rarest skill of all--to be staged. The good ones will continue to be flukes, nestling in the margin. If a margin still exists.
