Jun 5, 1983

Light Blue Backdrop

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Light blue backdrop

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

FOOTLIGHTS! by Robert Hewison Methuen £8.95

IT must have been early in the 1950s that I heard a radio programme called The Cambridge Footlights Take the Mike: a spoof of all other radio programmes presented apparently by a bunch of undergraduates. It was delicious. I doubt if I knew at the time what a real footlight was, let alone a human one; but the fact that Cambridge University could not only win the Boat Race every year but make me laugh as well clinched my scholastic ambitions. I think I was in primary school at the time.

Robert Hewison makes no mention of Take the Mike but there is not much else he leaves out. (I knew he had been thorough when I found my own name in an appendix since my own eventual membership was fairly passive.) His history is subtitled A Hundred Years of Cambridge Comedy, the Footlights having mounted their first production in 1883. It was a burlesque called Bombastes Furioso, and yielded a published song-hit, The Cambridge Boating Song ('Bow, your back's out ever so far,/What a very consummated muff you are'). Boaters have remained part of the Footlights' image ever since, in the eyes of the world if not in those of its members, causing considerable embarrassment in recurring periods of austerity or social consciousness. Social snobbery was actually replaced in the 1950s by performing snobbery; audition procedures were fearful. But now, it seems, they have to tout for members.

Footlights themselves are a long outmoded form of illumination; and revue -- which the club did not adopt as its format until 1921, but to which it has stayed faithful ever since -- has generally been declared extinct as well. It survives only at universities, and pre-eminently at Cambridge. Mr Hewison (a broadminded Oxonian) concludes that 'the concentration of intelligence in one of the country's leading universities guarantees that a fresh supply of potential talent is available every year'; and he might have added that fledgeling writing talent is better employed on short sketches than on long plays. Funniness is something that burns out early in many people; what is often stigmatised as 'undergraduate humour' is frequently the funniest there is. It comes from people sharply aware of absurdity, and able either to attack or exploit it. If it can stay fresh at the post-graduate stage ('Beyond the Fringe,' 'Monty Python,' 'Not the Nine O'Clock News') it is priceless.

Mr Hewison reveals that the first real star Footlight, from 1910 to 1913, was Jack Hulbert, 'the model of the "Cambridge actor" long before the type was recognised.' If he was a theatrical all-rounder, his successors have been revue specialists: Peter Cook, Jonathan Miller (a Footlights star but, incredibly, never a member), John Cleese, Eric Idle, Griff Rhys-Jones.

Satire, it seems, has never really existed in the Footlights; the prevailing moods have been whimsy and craziness. (The funniest quoted material is by Cook and Cleese.) Music has been in and out. In the 1950s Michael Frayn wrote sentimental songs with misgivings; in the Sixties Cleese, having been penalised for his own inability to sing or dance, stamped them out.

The book traces the Footlights' social as well as cultural history. Its succession of clubrooms, with lunches and late licences, made it the centre of theatrical Cambridge, especially when the introduction of Associate Membership in the 1960s opened it to those with no personal revue ambitions. How the whole apparatus survived redevelopment and homelessness is a wonder. It has, apparently, suffered. But it is unlikely to die.
