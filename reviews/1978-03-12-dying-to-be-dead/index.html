Mar 12, 1978

Dying To Be Dead

Robert Cushman · The Observer

MOST OF US have made a theoretical decision about a point, either of pain or disability, at which we would consider life to be no longer worth living.

We have probably been aware that, in practice, a kind of sliding scale would operate and that, even were death to be had for the asking, we would keep postponing the request. We would hang on to life because, having got through one day, we might as well endure another. Habit itself would numb us. But that, too, is a terrifying proposition, enough to make suicide seem an enticing prospect: at least from afar off.

Nothing is likelier to involve an audience than the emotion I can best describe as there-but-for-the-grace-of-Goddishness; and I have never felt it operating more intensely than at Whose Life is it Anyway? (Mermaid). I have rarely been so gripped, my attention has never wandered less, and I have never felt so conscious that everyone in the house was reacting as I was.

We were all rooting for Brian Clark's hero, a sculptor whom a motor accident had paralysed from the waist down, and our sympathy took the unaccustomed form of wanting him to die. It was what he himself wanted, and in making this choice he was toting a load for all of us. Nor does the decision cost him, as far as we are shown, much of an effort; his struggles are all with the world beyond his bed. His chief antagonist is the consultant in charge of his case in whom a professional disinclination to sacrifice a patient's life combines with the general feeling of society that suicide must be essentially wrong and who can point in all honesty to similar victims who have survived into a kind of content.

This doctor, in the writing and in Richard Leech's performance, is allowed dignity and intelligence; Mr Clark is fair minded. But mind is not everything, and his heart, his guts and his wit are all on the other side. Ken, the sculptor, is both funny and attractive; to top it all he is played by Tom Conti, whose more extravagant mannerisms have of course been forcibly eliminated by the physical circumstances of a role which otherwise allows unlimited scope to his peculiar gift: that of making other people feel not merely sorry for his sufferings but guilty about them. He is at once abrasively self-pitying and damnably charming. The self-pity in this case is wholly justified, which should set up even more complicated vibrations but in fact results in a moral landslide.

There is a curious discontinuity here; long after Mr Clark's theme has been established, his plot goes on reiterating it. The consultant invokes the Mental Health Act to retain his control of Ken (a man who can logically argue that he wants to die must be insane is the Catch-22 argument) and Ken's solicitor responds by trying for a writ of Habeas Corpus. All is resolved in the judge's chambers (which for the nonce are located at Ken's bedside) and the decision remains in doubt till the very last moment.

The play works in fact as a thriller in addition to its effectiveness as polemic (and I believe it to be one of the very small body of plays which, because it articulates widely held feelings, might have a genuine social effect). It scores, too, in Ken's reactions to impending vegetation; particularly his bitter awareness that sexual desire does indeed outlive performance and that he is beginning to think and talk like a dirty old man.

Craft and dialogue are generally excellent. The analysis of the nature and function of hospital routine (institutional repressiveness at, in all senses, its purest) is fair enough, but Peter Nichols and Trevor Griffiths have done it better. There are too many lay figures among the supporting characters, notably the sympathetic lady doctor (Jane Asher) and the solicitor who, with surprising speed, takes Ken's part. The weaknesses can be felt at the time, but they do not interfere with the enjoyment.

*

A couple of years ago Mr Conti was involved in a singularly luckless attempt at Moliere's Don Juan. This play finally gets its English deserts at Greenwich in a production and translation by David Thompson.

Mr Thompson is probably the most underrated director in the British theatre; at least I have yet to hear anyone imperiously demand that he should be given his own theatre. He could make better use of it than most. No phrases flash out at you from his translation but the whole is singularly well-knit. There are no obvious visual coups either, but there is constant satisfaction.

Peter Rice's set is dominated by a great pair of doors and these, open or closed, are a continual source of satisfaction. A pair of carved saints at either side of the stage admirably set the religious background about which Moliere was so enticingly non-committal. There is plenty of orthodox sentiment in the play, most of it in the form of moral missiles aimed at the hero, but neither he nor his opponents are at all endearing.

The production's main merit is the obvious one that the actors are permitted to inhabit the play, rather than to wander uncomfortably through it. Frank Barrie's Juan, the height of cool, is matched with a dwarfish Sganarelle (Peter O'Farrell), common sense compromised by cowardice: the prototype comic servant, in fact. His famous cry for his wages, with his master dead, shows us Mr Thompson at his most tactful. It is not a moment; it does not crush us with Savage Irony; it has actually been pared down, but it haunts.

*

And so for a plunge into the cesspool. That is how Orwell introduced his essay on No Orchids for Miss Blandish and the Glasgow Citizens' company, as might be expected, take the plunge with enthusiasm. This is in fact their best production I have seen, piling on the tension, the sex, the cynicism, the violence, the madness and the drugs in a manner calculated, if the longed-for crunch ever comes, to drive Agatha Christie wincing from the West End.

And if the cast seem young and their accents well-intentioned but bizarre, then that does not crush us with fits too; James Hadley Chase, as I was reminded last week, never himself set foot in the States. His America is like the Jacobeans' Italy, a gloating fantasy; and the comparison certainly fits here. In this version all crosses are doubled and only one character survives the bloodbath: a private eye who closes the evening with Marlovian (Philip, not Kit) moralising.

If bits of Robert David Macdonald's adaptation are incongruously high-flown, his direction is exhilaratingly high-flying. The morality may be pernicious, but it is free of the hypocrisy that Moliere castigated more effectively in Juan than in Tartuffe. The sharks all eat each other; whose death is it anyway?
