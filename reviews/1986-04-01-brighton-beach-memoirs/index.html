Apr 1, 1986

Brighton Beach Memoirs

Robert Cushman · Plays International

Brighton Beach Memoirs

Brighton Beach Memoirs is, as far as I know, Neil Simon's best play. It is not his funniest, but that works to its advantage. When one breaks up at Mr Simon's wisecracks (as I nearly always do) some guilt usually goes along with the enjoyment. You have to ask yourself would people really come back as snappily as this?" Actually my own, possibly starry-eyed, belief is that in New York they would; sardonic attack, for those who can do it, is the best form of defence. It becomes less convincing, though, when the mannerism takes over the whole of the dialogue and becomes the author's defence rather than the characters'; as Pauline Kael once put it, 'it's not the one-liners that get you down, it's the two-liners.' The Sunshine Boys and The Prisoner of Second Avenue are depressing (at least in their film versions, which are the only way I know them not because their LONDON NOTICES flare Ins Atre 198C subject-matter is dark, but because the author never faces up to how dark it is, and tries to conceal it from himself with mechanical joyless brightness.

It is himself, I'm pretty sure, that he is trying to protect. He doesn't pander to audiences, he panders to himself. Little of this applies to Brighton Beach Memoirs. Most of the gags here are given to the central character, who represents Mr Simon's teenage self, and how else would a precocious gag-man speak? He tells us his story and the story of his family, scraping a living in New York as the depression ends and the war begins. His narrative is the autobiography he is writing; the convention only breaks down when he addresses us as if we were spectators rather than readers, (yes, I know we are, but I don't think he should; it makes him stickily ingratiating.

But it's only a minor irritant). Does he ever have problems. He is forced to eat liver and cabbage, his mother sends him on errands at ten-minute intervals, he has an unsatisfied curiosity about girls, and he worries about masturbating. His older brother, who gives him good but infrequent advice on the last two, is in danger of losing his job. His fatherless cousin wants to quit school and join a Broadway chorus. Her younger sister has asthma, and is a brat. Their mother has very bad eyes, worries at being a burden on the family, and is attrac ted to a drunken Irishman across the street. Her sister, our hero's mother, thinks she should stick to her own kind, or at least stay away from other kids.

("That's what they have gutters for.') That isn't all she worries about. As a matter of fact she worries about everything. Her husband, meanwhile, struggles to support this menage by working day and night: literally. When his night-time employer goes bust he turns to taxi-driving. It is not good for him. Each of these dramas rises to a confrontation, open or subdued; each subsides. The orchestration of all this is masterly, each character and incident in place at the right time, and Michael Rudman's production never misses a beat. Steven Mackintosh holds everything together as the young Simon, and Robert Glenister as the brother does some lovely modulations from fright to defiance. Harry Towb is an underplayed embodiment of paternal authority: authority unsought-for but conscientiously executed.

Mr Towb, by the way, must have played more cabbies than any other living actor, and he is, altogether, pretty obvious casting for this role. But one of Mr Rudman's great qualities is to take chances with actors he trusts; I remember how he extended Harold Innocent in For Services Rendered and Geraldine McEwan in The Browning Version. Here he casts Alison Fiske, usually a very English smoulderer, as an ex-beauty determined to be downtrodden, and is rewarded by a time biding performance that finally explodes. He also permits Frances de la Tour to break the mould of the lewish mother; it may be that she is too obviously intelligent for the role but in underpinning the nagging and the irrationality with nerves and intelligence she gives us a richer character and therefore — since the mother is of course the soul of the household - a richer play.

She has a moment of silent weeping on a staircase, the mask dropped when nobody can see her, that has haunted me for days. But she never betrays the play's comic mode: is actually happier in it — or at least made me happier _than in the heavy-breathing roles she has been playing recently. The big fight in the play is between the two sisters, a contest in self-abnegation simultaneously proclaimed and resented. It is patched up, like all the others, and we leave the already overstretched household preparing to take in any number of refugee relatives in Europe. They will cope. I don't know if this is sentimental, but it is probably true — and less of a jolt here than it was in the darker-toned New York production.

For all I know the real Simon family were more durably miserable than their stage surrogates here. But the fictional image is sturdy enough to survive in its own right; Brighton Beach Memoirs will probably work better for an audience that has never heard of Neil Simon than Long Day's Journey Into Night could ever hope to do for an audience that cared nothing about the real O'Neill. (Funny the chime of names: Neil, O'Neill. And the author's rep here is called Eugene.) The National were justified in presenting it; it has a better chance of survival on the South Bank than in the West End, and it is the most evenly satisfying play by a living author in their current repertoire.

That does not mean that they have to present the sequel Biloxi Blues, Mr Simon's military reminiscences and a play disfigured by self-consciously earnest writing. And the sequel to that, the yet-to-be-seen Broadway Bound, will have to meet sterner standards; the nearer we get to present reality, to Mr Simon the career playwright, the more uncompromisingly honest we will require him to be.
