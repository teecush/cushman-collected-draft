Dec 28, 1975

Danniella And The Dragon

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Danniella and the dragon

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the latest offerings from Danny La Rue and Charles Marowitz.

A YEAR ago the London Casino returned to live entertainment with a pantomime, Cinderella. Though excellent in most respects, this flunked the principal requirements of a modern Christmas show by failing to run until Easter. So this year no chances have been taken and the Casino has requisitioned the services of Danny La Rue, the surest draw in the West End. The show's title--though this is of the merest academic interest--is Queen Danniella.

For the past three Christmases I have been trying to explain the conventions of pantomime to my Canadian wife. I have had little success, and Queen Danniella makes the task harder than ever. It has virtually no story. Digression is of course the staple diet of pantomime, but this one has nothing to digress from. It has, to be sure, some stock situations: there is the usual quest in which all the good or funny characters venture into the castle of the bad or serious ones. Mr La Rue as Danniella, whose queendom is in hock, gets lost in a forest glade where she (I am running into trouble with personal pronouns) meets an admittedly sweet baby dragon. But the charm of irrelevant episodes like this is traditionally that of playing truant from the main action; when school has been closed up truancy loses much of its appeal.

Mr La Rue for the adults, fragments of narrative and slapstick for the kids, and some indeterminate pop for everyone or no one: that must have been the formula. It is, I suppose, only a logical culmination of the process which has been overtaking the cavernous West End panto for years. But at a matinee performance the calculations seemed upset; there were plenty of empty seats and those that were full were mainly full of children. Now whatever the advances of juvenile sophistication (signalised by a shrill cry of "Rape" from the back stalls when some villain made off with Sheila White's extremely tough Princess), Mr La Rue's characteristic double entendres are not likely to mean too much to the under-twelves. He went devoutly through his patter, and through Bryan Blackburn's lyrics (which contribute to theatrical history by rhyming "Gielgud" with "feel good" and don't ask me to explain the context or we'll never get home), but it was all done with subdued desperation.

In fact Mr La Rue was up against it; and in these circumstances he strangely seemed far more likeable than when playing to a full and sycophantic house at the Prince of Wales. His manner is always professional, and in this show his professionalism is manna. I have never seen a performer get through the community singing bit with more address or less embarrassment.

I even, when the panto-pretence was finally discarded for a prolonged star-turn, enjoyed his impersonations--done, remember, to an audience that can never have heard of Carmen Miranda or even of Marlene Dietrich. (He also does the Marilyn Monroe of Some Like It Hot, flanked by two other guys pretending to be Tony Curtis and Jack Lemmon pretending to be girls. Even Shakespeare never thought of that juxtaposition.) He has the legs for Grable and he wears the frocks as ever with superb panache--though the problem of how to occupy the stage while he is changing them has been solved even more miserably than usual. (By the way, could someone be hired before next Christmas to teach the pantomime chorus just one new step--or alternatively to drown them. Everybody knows these routines bore the kids; but whom are they supposed to entertain?)

The whole production stinks of compromise, but Mr La Rue--especially after you have spent an evening hearing Charles Pierce bitching at the ladies to whom he owes his livelihood--is very pleasant. He is, in fairness, less funny than Mr Pierce; next Christmas perhaps the two of them could do the Ugly Sisters.

*

Charles Marowitz, of all directors, respects his author. It takes only a workaday trust in Shakespeare to assume that his plays will make sense if the lines are delivered in the order their author set down, but to believe that they will still be effective with their running-order jumbled requires true fanaticism. It also requires of course that your audience, if they are to enjoy themselves, must bring prior knowledge to the theatre with them.

As this is true of Mr Marowitz's Shakespearean collages, so it is of his latest work. Artaud at Rodez (Open Space) is a phantasmagoric view of the French theatrical theorist and madman Antonin Artaud, Rodez being the asylum in which he spent some of the latest years of his life. That, and a deal else, I learned from Mr Marowitz's compilation but it was not enough. The production lacks flair, but it might succeed as an illustrated lecture if only we were told more; if, for example, some attempt were made to distinguish between Artaud's Theatre of Cruelty and its common interpretation as a theatre of sadism. The line is perhaps a thin one, and Artaud himself probably smudged it, but it ought at some stage of the evening to be drawn.

The show makes no bones, and a few good jokes, of the misinterpretation of Artaud's ideas by his disciples, but since those ideas are never presented to us he comes over as just another victim. As such he is well and feverishly played by Clive Merrison.

*

I missed Beetles and Buckman when they played the Mermaid this year, but I will do my best to catch them when they return to the boards in February at the Mayfair. Catch is the appropriate word; they are a pair of doctor-comedians, and for their current season at Quaglino's Beetles (Chris) has pulled the ultimate medical joke by going down with hepatitis, leaving Buckman (Robert) to carry the act on his own. This he does most appealingly, revealing himself as a unique commodity in Britain: an overtly literate stand-up clown, the first since Jonathan Miller.

His humour is far less surreal than was Mr Miller's; though it has a touch of what I can only call solid whimsy, in the most recent Cambridge tradition: hence a rousing sea-shanty about working in a public library. And though he does not actually cut anybody, he is sharp: he even creates an endearing critic, one who having left his concert tickets behind, nevertheless followed the pianist he was sent to review to the restaurant of his choice and there timidly admired the technique of a virtuoso "trained to be equally at home with spoon and fork." Geniality flows from Dr Buckman; go and see him, unless of course you are ill.
