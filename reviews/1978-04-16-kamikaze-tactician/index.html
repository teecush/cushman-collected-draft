Apr 16, 1978

Kamikaze Tactician

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Kamikaze tactician

ROBERT CUSHMAN profiles one of Britain's most influential theatre directors who is now turning his talents to television.

THE theatre of the decade for new work -- the Royal Court, if you like, of the 1970s -- has not been in London at all.

For the past five years Nottingham Playhouse has been a centre for writing and acting, brittle in style but wide in scope; that has influenced the Court itself, the National Theatre and such fringe companies as Joint Stock. It has also touched television; and the Playhouse director during this period, Richard Eyre, has now in fact moved on to the BBC as a producer of Play for Today.

Eyre, who is 35, began his professional life as an actor. Even that, he insists, was an accident; he had intended to become a journalist. Certainly he always wanted to write, and it had occurred to him that he might well at some point write for the theatre. When he went up to Cambridge in 1961 involvement in university theatre seemed a good method of preparation. So for three years he acted -- in, it must be added, extremely good company. So at least it seemed to me, viewing his generation through dazzled freshman eyes two years later. Robin Ellis, Michael Pennington, John Shrapnel are among his acting contemporaries who have made professionally good.

I have memories of standing around in some undefined and probably unnecessary capacity (in the professional theatre I could at least have made the tea) on a production of The Caretaker; Eyre was the tough brother, Shrapnel the lobotomised one, and the tramp was Jonathan Lynn, who now also runs a rep (at Cambridge, in fact). Neither Eyre nor Lynn directed anything much as students, though; Eyre actually directed a film, which he now describes as "a 16mm. piece of avant-garbage." Cambridge meanwhile was full, as it always is, of people who thought they were going to be directors. Hardly any of them have succeeded.

The turnabout for Eyre came soon afterwards at Leicester, where he was appearing in The Boy Friend, delivering "a collector's piece of coarse acting -- no, that's overrating it. Acting is the most depressing job in the world, if you realise you can't do it." To stave off depression, he organised some of his colleagues into a Sunday night production of The Knack, which was successful enough for Clive Perry, the boss of the theatre, to face him with the brusque and unexpected question, "Well, which are you going to be, an actor or a director?"

Put that way, there was really very little choice. Eyre went, prophetically, to Nottingham, assisting John Neville on a couple of plays that eventually toured the Far East. He did freelance productions for the Liverpool Everyman and the 7.84 company, and in slack times worked as drama editor for a publishing firm and even, I assure you, as a theatre critic for The Scotsman.

That figures because it was in Edinburgh that his directing reputation was really established. He worked at the Lyceum there for his old mentor Clive Perry, and as Perry turned more and more towards administration Eyre began doing the bulk of the productions. He ran through many of the standard classics (including all four major Chekhovs) and began, as well, to find himself as a director of new plays. It was in this role that he came into prominence when he took over in Nottingham: first (in January 1973) as deputy for the incumbent director, Stuart Burge, and then in his own right. "I've always been interested in writers. Why, some of my best friends. . . ."

One of them, David Hare, went with him to the Playhouse in the first instance as literary manager; and Brassneck, written by Hare and Howard Brenton, was the first world premiere of Eyre's regime, a social comedy built to the capacious scale of the Playhouse stage and auditorium. (It is one rep without a studio for new work to be banished to.) The success of this play, followed by Brenton's Churchill Play and Trevor Griffiths's Comedians (which Eyre, with reason, counts as "a contemporary classic"), established Nottingham as the home theatre of the new generation of public playwrights: a generation too young for the Royal Court and too ambitious for the fringe.

They are also writers in demand elsewhere, and to lure them to Nottingham Eyre pursued what he calls "a kamikaze policy." I said, "Write a play and I guarantee I'll put it on." I could offer them a definite commitment and, with hindsight, I'd defend having done every one of them." The list also includes Stephen Lowe's Touched, which I disliked but which Eyre regards "with great pride: a play about Nottingham, by a Nottingham author, but ambitious, not parochial."

The play is set at the close of World War Two, which reflects not only the author's but the director's preoccupation with the last 30 years. "Brassneck" covered the period from the Labour victory of 1945 to the end of the Poulson era -- the crucible out of which our present state has crystallised." Eyre grew up in Devon, sheltered, he insists, with little knowledge of the theatre or of real life. Going north, he says, made him realise there really were two nations -- and I don't mean England and Scotland." At Nottingham he had to function virtually as a liaison official between theatre and city.

As well as writers Eyre has developed some tough, quirky actors ("Comedians" was a terrific ensemble production). "It's a real pleasure going to work with a group of people, friends." His wife, Sue Birtwistle, runs the Playhouse's Theatre-in-Education company, and there are times when she's had as large a company as me. I'll miss keeping open house for them all." He may even mean this. The Eyres' Nottingham home is extremely pleasant, and the move to London has made house-hunting a far greater headache than script-hunting.

He has in fact a number of scripts lined up for his television series. He got the BBC offer last June; James Cellan Jones, the Head of Plays, was looking for scripts from contemporary writers, and though no one, least of all the man concerned, ever knows precisely how these things work, it seems reasonable to assume that Eyre has been commandeered to continue his Nottingham work for a wider audience.

Certainly some writing links are being maintained. Trevor Griffiths is writing a film. (Eyre has been contracted to do 10 plays, two films and eight in the studio.) Scripts have been commissioned from other theatre writers -- Robert Holman, Barrie Keeffe, John McGrath -- and also from novelist Tom Clarke. Clarke's piece is the first to go into production: a play about apartheid, but set here, and dealing with our responsibility for it. If the series has a unity, it should lie in Eyre's concern with the way public behaviour hinges on the private and vice versa.

He alternates between relief and regret at ceasing to run a theatre, though after five years relief seems uppermost. He's glad not to be directing for a bit, though he will handle one TV play himself, probably next year. When I talked to him he had just made the common discovery that "watching other people's filming is very boring," but, like most people who have got within yards of all the fascinating hardware of TV, he has not done badly with hardware on the stage and there seems no reason why his Nottingham style ("highly wrought detail alongside the spectacular") should not transfer from one medium to another.
