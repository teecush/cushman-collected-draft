Aug 14, 1977

The Three Irish Maries

Robert Cushman · The Observer

The three Irish Maries

THE AUTHOR'S name is Mary O'Malley, the theatre the Royal Court and the play is called Once a Catholic, so you approach expecting another glum patch from the Irish litterati, drenched in Catholicism and philosophy. Not a bit of it; on offer is an insouciantly funny piece about the growing pains of three 17-year-old girls at a convent school in the neighbourhood of Willesden Junction.

The three girls are all called Mary - like every other girl in their class, apart from a token Maria. Mary McGinty is going out with a local Ted. (The date is 1956.) Mary Gallagher is having a pretty intense relationship with a Catholic boy who fancies himself for the priesthood; and so it is piquant that it is Mary G who has lost her virginity by the end of the play while Mary McG has picked up a proposal of marriage.

The third girl, Mary Mooney - who seems to represent the author - has one brief encounter which leaves her in a state of shuddering surprise, but otherwise the chief man in her life is the school music-teacher, who wants her to become a singer. He too makes her shudder. She is the innocent of the trio, and also the fall-girl: a string of unfortunate coincidences leaves the staff with the impression that she, of all of them, is the incipient scarlet woman.

Miss O'Malley's play is put together rather amateurishly; in the first scene the girls' form-mistress (a performance of ripe simplicity by Pat Heywood) warns them of the GCE perils ahead, promising 'a very heavy burden of homework' and advises that 'no one ever passed any exam of their own accord - only prayer can help you.' Exam results prove crucial to the play's resolution, but in the stretch between the subject is entirely dropped. The author, one feels, might pay more attention to her lessons and less to sex. It is, of course, a more easily stageable subject, and Miss O'Malley, taking the situation as it comes, treats it with great good humour, some invention, and considerable tact. We see nothing of the girls' parents but still get a full picture of their homelives. If Derek the Ted is conventional, Cuthbert the would-be cleric is an original, given by Mike Grady his full measure of preening insecurity. And if the situations are predictable the jokes (or two-thirds of them) are not.

For non-Catholics the play has of course a considerable voyeuristic appeal, but one which produces smiles rather than tears. Nostalgia is similarly handled: fed but not pampered. Mike Ockrent's production is physically awkward but it has the authentic smell of repression and school gravy; and though it is time Jane Carr (who has more or less cornered the market in plump, prim adolescence) was allowed to grow up she makes the mooniest of Maries.

The second half of the title we can of course fill in for ourselves; not so the action to which it corresponds, and one would like to know how these girls - rebellious but still indoctrinated - coped after they had left school. It might make a more challenging play.

*

The Roundhouse, as currently occupied by Joint Stock, lives up to its name; the audience surround at any rate, three-quarters of the acting area. For Howard Brenton's Epsom Downs this consists of a dazzling green slope; up and down it move some 50 characters. Not, unfortunately, all at the same time; nine actors play all the roles, and though this is clever of them it does result in the audience getting the worst of both worlds: no massed scenes and no characters to hold on to. The moment you begin to take an interest in someone, he is off to change into someone else.

So the play is afflicted by a monotony of rhythm and by the surprisingly low pressure of the writing. This new Derby Day canvas may be Mr Brenton's most genial play, but it is also his weakest.

Critics, noting the coexistence of knights, shopkeepers and beggars in Jacobean comedies, like to refer to them as 'pictures of a hierarchical society.' Epsom Downs is open to the same indulgent misrepresentation, presenting as it does the Aga Khan, a Socialist peer, bookies, punters, trainers, horses (fetchingly impersonated by nude harnessed actors, one of whom brings the house down with the bashful admission that he's an outside chance), a gypsy and a stable-boy: these last two for that dear old thing, the love interest. But it only presents them; and the ghost of Emily Davison, the suffragette who once threw herself under the royal horse, stalks the scene to little effect leaving us only with the vague feeling that it is a good idea to throw yourself under racing hooves on days of celebration. Cf. Mr Brenton's Magnificence: 'Disrupt the spectacle. The obscene parade.'

But the nearest to obscenity in his depiction of the Derby is the amount of litter dropped in an excellently-staged spectators' galop while the race is on, to be picked up at the end by inmates of the local mental home. (Good of the actors not to leave it to the stage-staff.) Otherwise the best scenes involve a poor family risking their all on a desperate flutter. They include Gillian Barge as a splendidly horrible three-year-old (girl not horse) and Simon Callow, a polished comedian seen in a new and homelier light.

*

Otherwise it has been a week of old favourites. Edward Bond's Bingo (Warehouse) has been rescued from the mumbling mummer-set which has previously shrouded its minor characters and much of its action in unfathomable mystery. The RSC is rich at the moment in striking young actors named Paul (this is as far as I am prepared to go in spotting trends); the Messrs Brooke, Shelley and Moriarty all illumine their roles and the play. Patrick Stewart, than whom Saturn himself could hardly be more saturnine, plays Mr Bond's suicidal Shakespeare; strong while establishing his despair, monotonous when reiterating it.

This is largely the play's fault, but it distributes its guilt with more complexity than I had suspected; the Puritan parricide who in leading the anti-enclosure men fulfils the Shakespearean prophecy of chaos ('the rude son shall strike the father dead') is a fascinating study in self-righteousness. So is Judith Shakespeare, not malicious but lethally unimaginative; Meg Davies's performance matches her Regan and I doubt if the connection is fortuitous.

For Barry Collins's Judgement (Cottesloe) too I now have more respect, though there is more of literature and less of humanity in Ben Kingsley's rendition of this marathon monologue than there was in Colin Blakely's. To suggest so persuasively the actions and reactions of seven men, abandoned and reduced to cannibalism, is a considerable imaginative feat, though Mr Collins cannot leave his ironies well alone; after using the word 'delicacies' he has to draw our attention to it.

Tales from the Vienna Woods (Olivier) remains the National's best show, though the replacement of Kate Nelligan by the perky Brenda Blethyn leaves it more schematic, less tragically turbulent, than before. All the same, to see the bathers plunge daintily into the Danube or the stage revolve twice over, apparently through sheer joie de vivre, is to realise how much the theatre customarily denies us, and to see the death-dealing grandmother leave the stage proclaiming her own immortality is to feel the dead hand of the ritualised past on your spine. Always a Catholic.
