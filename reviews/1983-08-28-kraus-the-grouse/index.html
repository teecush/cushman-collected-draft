Aug 28, 1983

Kraus The Grouse

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Kraus the Grouse

ROBERT CUSHMAN on official and Fringe productions.

AS far as the theatre is concerned, the Edinburgh Festival's theme of `Vienna 1900' has so far meant a brace of plays in which the glories of the Austro-Hungarian Empire have crumbled before our eyes. The stages have shimmered with old gilt; and also of course with old guilt.

In the Assembly Hall, the Glasgow Citizens' Company have, with typical generosity, been offering The Last Days of Mankind. This piece has defied even their unflappability; their version, weighing in at four hours, is a mere sliver of an 800-page original. That original, a chronicle of the First World War, was written more or less contemporaneously, and published complete in 1922.

Its author, Karl Kraus, was an Austrian journalist who hated journalism. The main purpose of his play was to expose the lies and distortions of the patriotic papers, along with the fatuities of the politicians and soldiers they served. Much of the play consists of direct quotation, many of the characters are animated topical cartoons. I apologise if I sound as if these are things I have known all my life. Like all the other reviewers, I learnt them only last week from the Festival brochure.

That same brochure says of the piece, `It has half of Europe as its stage.' That sounds exciting, doesn't it? It also sounds tricky, and the solution adopted by Robert David Macdonald (who as usual directs his own translation) is to set all the scenes where Kraus set some of his: in a restaurant. My recurring impression of Austrian drama as being just one long meal is given spectacular confirmation.

Of course the characters do not just eat and drink; they also read the news, and they discuss it. From time to time the scene is transported, gentles, to the battlefield, with no change of decor. The transitions are seamless: so smooth that it took me some time to realise what was going on. Admittedly, I had a bad seat.

It is impressive. What else would you expect of a four-hour epic from a company to whom flamboyant gesture, satirically honed, is second nature? But it is not, despite an abundance of picturesque sex and violence, very enjoyable. Kraus, though obviously a pungent pamphleteer, had no observable gift for story or character; and the production concept sacrifices whatever interest might have accrued from constant changes of locale or personnel.

The people are identified as `a Patriot and Optimist' or `a Reader of Newspapers.' The only one allowed a name is a figure representing the author himself, called in the cast-list Kraus the Grouse, and played by Giles Havergal, who--since he is kept sitting throughout--it is tempting to re-christen Giles the Piles.

He observes the follies and cruelties of humanity and comments on them. He is always right. The play goes on (and on and on and on) making the same point. It is a good point, and Kraus makes a better fist of symbolising and lampooning 1914-18 military lunacy than did O'Casey in The Silver Tassie, though he falls far short of Joan Littlewood in Oh What a Lovely War. But it does not amount to drama.

At the end--and after more loud bangs than I have ever heard before in a theatre, some of them dislodging unnameable substances from the rafters of the Assembly Hall--the world is destroyed, i.e., the restaurant closes forever. God, who apparently resides on Mars, declares himself unwilling to go on picking up the tab--at least he, coming over a celestial Tannoy, is audible; most of the cast are defeated by the hall's notorious acoustics.

*

Also living in Vienna in 1900, and dying there by his own hand in 1903, was Otto Weininger, an apparently brilliant Jew who hated Judaism. He was 23 when he killed himself. Hitler approved of him. The Soul of a Jew (a less grandiose title than The Last Days of Mankind, but not much) is his story, dramatised by an Israeli playwright, Yehoshua Sobol.

One of the Jewish things that Weininger hated was nascent Zionism, and Mr Sobol has understandably given this great prominence. The play raised a storm in Israel. But it distracts from what should be the play's main theme. Otto's self-hatred is a common neurosis carried to fascinating--and historically ominous--extremes. But neuroses are difficult subjects.

The temptation is to cut the protagonist's psyche into neat gobbets and then distribute them about the stage: one supporting character for each influence. Mr Sobol does this, and throws in some other jaded devices. The play is a flashback just before suicide; Otto encounters his father in a brothel; he also--since he is anti-woman as well as anti-semitic--has a feared but fascinating female Double.

I may have been soured by the very poor English translation circulated in Edinburgh; the Haifa Municipal Theatre, playing in Hebrew on a mirrored stage in the Assembly Rooms, give the play more theatrical life than I had expected. Doron Tavory's Otto is a mixed-up kid in the best tousled tradition; Leora Rivlin as his mother is chicken-plucking good, this being what she does while simultaneously soothing and upbraiding her boy in the play's best scene.

*

The Fringe grows ever more unruly. I read in the Festival Times that my colleagues, dismayed by the impossibility of reviewing it all, are saying that it should be put a stop to (how?) and transplanted somewhere else. This is rubbish: since (a) drama does not exist to keep critics at work, and (b) the point of the Fringe is its accessibility; over 37 years people have learned where Edinburgh is, and when it has its festival.

One of its charms is that it breaks down barriers. Far from home, critics can afford to fraternise with performers. This can be embarrassing. I seemed to have a friend in every show I have seen. My chief problem--as I am performing on the fringe myself--is a musical, written and composed by Colin Sell, who also happens to be my accompanist.

By the time we meet tomorrow afternoon for our final performance together he will have learnt that, though I liked many of the songs in his Iron Harvest--they sound like a concert-party scored by Noel Coward--and thought it well performed, I found his post-Falklands message heavy, and his use of recitative excessive. I must speak to him about his habit of rhyming `fruit juice' with `root loose' and `Bracknell' with `shrapnel'.

Iron Harvest lives in the same segment of the Assembly Rooms as Andy Armitage's Oil Strikes Back, yet another one-act play about depressed youth doing dead-end work, decently but quite unexceptionally written. Set in Manchester in 1968, it does at least enable Phil Daniels and Tilly Vosburgh, talented players habitually cast as London punks, to extend themselves backwards in time and northwards in accent.

There are even two better performances--by Stephen Boxer and Sarah-Heliane Elliott--in Muzzle, at Buster Brown's, but the play, by Stuart Delves, who has won Fringe firsts--belongs to an even deader genre: whimsical-spiritual. I do believe it could only happen in Edinburgh.
