Apr 8, 1979

Hands At The Opera

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Hands at the opera

ROBERT CUSHMAN profiles the director of Covent Garden's new 'Parsifal,' which opens on Wednesday.

SURROUNDED by the massed choral heavies of the Royal Opera House, some of whom carried rubber swords while others were equipped with dustbin-lid shields, Terry Hands, slight in his black track-suit, looked distinctly vulnerable.

Directors of the Royal Shakespeare Company invariably look boyish; Peter Hall did in his time at Stratford, and so do Trevor Nunn who succeeded him, and Hands who last year was promoted in equal rank with Nunn. On leave from the RSC, he directs Parsifal at Covent Garden.

This is his second opera; the first was Otello, which he staged in Paris in 1976 with Placido Domingo singing the Moor. Georg Solti conducted and the association was a happy one. "I only accept one opera for every five I turn down," Hands says, but two years ago, when Solti telephoned him and asked whether he would by any chance be free for Parsifal in 1979, he agreed.

As might have been foreseen, he is not as free as all that. Apart from sharing the burden of RSC planning at a particularly tricky point in the company's history, he has to supervise the European tour of his highly successful production of Coriolanus, which played last year at the Aldwych and the year before at Stratford. In fact he will have to fly into the first night of Parsifal directly after seeing to Coriolanus's arrival in Vienna.

Of all the RSC's directors--apart, perhaps, from the special case of Peter Brook--Hands has been the most actively international. He has a production of As You Like It in the repertory of the Vienna Burgtheatre and he is a consultant director of the Comedie Francaise. It was at the Comedie that he had one of his greatest successes with a production of Richard III in 1972. Two years before he had come unstuck with the play at Stratford, but doing it in Paris turned out to be one of the key experiences of his career.

As his leading man, he had the most unconventional of French classical actors, the comedian Robert Hirsch, and he found that the French rhetorical acting tradition gave him a new insight into Shakespeare. He learned the value of "playing a line right through to the end" rather than breaking it up into anxiously-analysed sections; and this led the way to his most fruitful period of work at Stratford: the Henrys (IV, V and VI) and Coriolanus. These were all productions of a new muscularity and simplicity and, like the French Richard, they took much of their impact from the director's rapport with his leading actor, in this case Alan Howard. "Alan has no directing ambitions, he always speaks for the actor."

Opera, for all its fearsome logistics (all the problems of a play even before you start on the music), can be a comparative relaxation. Since the conductor is the boss, "if it doesn't work, you can only get half the blame. The problem with opera is that everyone is working in a different style; I feel sorry for opera-singers, subjected to one influence after another. The director is always hired after the singers have been approached."

Hands's father was a musician; he himself "learned the violin at eight, gave it up, took up the cello at 10, gave it up. I can read a piano score." He has, as it happens, a reputation as a somewhat operatic director of drama. The choreographer Gillian Lynne is assisting him on Parsifal and during the rehearsal I attended she congratulated him on the staging of Coriolanus, which she had caught up with the night before: "Very well choreographed."

The irony here is that Hands, by his own account, does not go in for strictly organised movement and certainly never works any out in advance. (In drama, that is; in opera, with a hundred-strong chorus to manoeuvre in short order, the situation is obviously different.) But it is certainly true that he has always leaned towards ceremonial, or anyway non-naturalistic drama. Naturalism he sees as an overrated passing phase that television has now thankfully taken over, much to the theatre's benefit.

Hands is 38. He grew up in Woking, Surrey, and as a teenager used to come into London by the early train to do the art galleries by day and the Old Vic by night. He went to Birmingham University and flourished there in the Guild Theatre Group "for purely social and ambitious reasons: I didn't have enough money for the Union, which was the only alternative." He wanted to do research, but his tutor John Russell Brown (now head of scripts at the National Theatre) doubted if he had the academic temperament. So he went to RADA and while there laid the plans for his first theatre.

In association with Peter James (a Birmingham contemporary, now director of the Sheffield Crucible) and Martin Jenkins (now with the BBC), he started the Liverpool Everyman Theatre. In their day it was a classical theatre; it has now changed its identity completely and is devoted to new and mainly local plays. After two years in Liverpool, Hands "ran out of everything I knew" and wrote two letters, "one to the National, one to the RSC." The second paid off: Peter Hall wanted someone to run the newly emergent touring group, Theatre Go Round. Hands was told to go away and make it work. "Within three months we had nine shows on the road. But I'd done all this kind of thing before, working with kids in Liverpool."

In 1967 he had his first solo flight at the Aldwych: a three-character Cuban play called The Criminals, done in a boldly extrovert style that confused the critics and had the audience booing and heckling--"most unusual, they usually just leave." Hall told Hands that the production was "very brilliant--I didn't follow the play for a moment," and Hands survived to direct a resoundingly successful Merry Wives of Windsor at Stratford the next year.

This supposedly intractable comedy became virtually his property for years (it was revived as late as 1975), though he says the only play about which he really feels proprietorial is Pericles, which he staged in 1969. These two plays, as it happens, have opened this year's Stratford season in other directors' production, so Hands is using his new authority magnanimously.

In some ways his post as Joint Artistic Director is a rationalisation of RSC policy over the past few years. He and Nunn have run alternate seasons at Stratford and the Aldwych, and each has developed his own team of actors. (Common opinion has it that Hands's bunch is the more roughneck of the two, and he doesn't deny it.)

The new season in some ways breaks the pattern; the company contains actors from both groups, and both Nunn and Hands are directing. Hands's play is Twelfth Night, which I said I thought unlikely for him; I don't associate him with comedy. He said nonsense, he's spent most of his life directing comedy. Are there any Shakespeares he yearns to direct in the future? "Yes, 22 of them. I've done 15. I want to do them all."
