Robert Cushman · Radio Times

Reviewing the '70s

BBC television drama entered the 70s with its eye firmly fixed on the past. I don't know if nostalgia can be prophetic but I'd like to believe that it was a charmed Entering this time. For New Year's Day universe 1970 brought us episode one of The Six Wives of Henry VIII, the series whose immense success pointed the way to custume drama at home and prestige sales (to American educational networks mostly) abroad. For those who have forgotten what outstanding they learned either at school exposi- or on BBC2, Henry's first was Catherine of Aragon, played by of Annette Crosbie. And just to show you how the wind was blowing, playing on BBC1 was a re-run of The Forsyte Saga Series were rampant: Z Cars and Softly Softly of course (I never could get used to the idea of them co-existing) and Doomwatch, the one about technology getting out of > 117 115 < «k hand and killing people.

Ian McKellen's twin kings - Shakespeare's Richard II and Marlowe's Edward II - were brought to the screen, after their phenomenal success in the theatre. But the new single play, whose supremacy had been taken for granted during the socially-conscious 60s, was already entering its fight-forsurvival stage. Tony Parker's Chariot of Fire tried to main- Radio Times Making War & Peace WAR AND PEACE SEPTEMBER 1972 tain the tradition, and Tom Clarke's Mad Jack (directed by Jack Gold) to extend it. But probably the most impressive dramatic achievement of the year was the serialisation of Sartre's The Roads to Freedom, with its fine performances from Michael Bryant, Daniel Massey and Georgia Brown. Television has never served a modern classic better.

After a re-run of Six Wives came Elizabeth R, chiefly memorable for establishing the bankability of Glenda Jackson. It was a Scandinavian period for ace director Alan Bridges first Bergman's The Lie (for which he won the STTA award) then in 1971 Ibsen's The Wild Duck with Denholm Elliott and Derek Godfrey. Bridges also directed Dennis Potter's Traitor; an untoppable partnership. Edna the Inebriate Woman won applause for its leading actress Patricia Hayes and its author Jeremy Sandford; it made his Cathy Come Home of the 60s seem like child's play. Dennis Potter aroused puritan wrath - and critical surpriseas writer of the serialised Casanova. It may have been the costume - and it launched Frank Finlay as a sex-symbolbut nobody called it escapist.

CHRISTMAS AND THE NEW YEAR 1972's modern classic serial was Man of Straw with Derek Jacobi whom RADIO TIMES tipped to become the next stage name to achieve television fame. There was a Merchant of Venice with Maggie Smith and Frank Finlay. There was no keeping the Tudors down - even the uncharismatic Henry VII was allowed his fling in Shadow of the Tower. And there was a production of Hedda Gabler with Janet Suzman in a role she would play more successfully in the theatre. Ian McKellen brought his greatly underrated Hamlet to the screen, Coral Browne flourished in Lady Windermere's Fan and, if any actress could play Shaw's Millionairess, it was Maggie Smith.

The BBC embarked on Tolstoy's War and Peace. Peter Terson's The Fishing Party inaugurated an affectionate cycle of plays about small masculine pleasures, and Alan Bridges directed Dennis Potter's pivotal Follow the Yellow Brick Road a play about death drawn from the author's own experiences in hospital. Peter Ustinov narrating and Galton and Simpson writing failed to make Clochemerle funny. Brecht's The Gangster Show: the Resistible Rise of Arturo Ui, directed by Jack Gold with Nicol Williamson playing Hitler-as-Capone took us into 1973 in high style - as television filmeraft it's still unsurpassed. Fine craftsperson Elaine Morgan dramatised the Bywaters-Thompson murder case in A Pin to See the Peepshow, and meanwhile Barlow and Watt had graduated (or been demoted) to investigating Jack the Ripper.

Kisses at Fifty was Colin Welland's best play and in the leading role Bill Maynard came in from the cold. While Colditz had caught on in 1972, 1974's The Pallisers didn't. Colin Welland wrote television's definitive strike drama in Leeds United!, given the semi-documentary film treatment by director Roy Battersby and producer Ken Trodd. The unlikeliest historical series of all was launched - Churchill's People, virtually a blank cheque for its writers. In Shaw's The Apple Cart, shown in 1975, Peter Barkworth played the Prime Minister; today he'd be the King. Our appetite for the well- / costumed past led to the Pre- Raphaelites being given their own series; they called it The Love School.

The admirable Miriam Mar golyes (self-described as fat and intellectually glamorous ) was one of Muriel Spark's The Girls of Slender Means. That had style. Jim Allen's marathon Days of Hope had integrity, and never allowed you to forget it. Poldark arrived And 1975's most memorable play was Through the Night, Trevor Griffiths's account of a woman with breast cancer. When the Boat Comes In came in in 1976, and stayed; even Tyneside depression could provide nostalgia-fodder. So could 1950s Cambridge and its graduates' subsequent careers; there was more than nostalgia to The Glittering Prizes but Frederic Raphael's six-parter never really came together. Jack Rosenthal's Bar Mitzvah Boy was the nearest thing I can remember to a really funny TV play. 1976 had two Dennis Potter plays: the best, most personal Double Dare and the most celebrated Where Adam Stood.

Jack Rosenthal's Spend, Spend, Spend (1977) was the ice-cold telefilm at its best, with a hard, stunning performance from Susan Littler as the girl who won the pools and ended as she started, broke. John Hopkins, who in the 60s had almost created the play-cycle with the tetralogy Talking to a Stranger, went to six with Fathers and Families, as if determined to nag or bully his viewers into a response; the series established Dinsdale Landen as number one choice for desperate intelligent RadioTimes Returnto Coldi COLDITZ JANUARY 1974 middle-class hero. Licking Hitler was David Hare's only play to date to take a subject and follow it through.

Dennis Potter carved Hardy's The Mayor of Casterbridge into seven episodes and the 30s of his own song-infested mythology into six: Piers Haggard's direction of Pennies from Heaven reminded us that he is the only man who has ever managed to put musicals on television. A few years had passed since the last royal serial so the Plantagenets were engaged for The Devil's Crown with the trickiest sets you ever saw. The level of jokiness varied disconcertingly. Shakespeare, who had been contributing one-offs to the BBC for years, was engaged for a series called The Complete Works.

He got, on the whole, bad notices. The drama event of the year was Holocaust; the objections raised to it were for the most part extraordinarily frivolous. 1979 is too close to be nostalgic, or even retrospective, about. At the close of the decade the original single play seems even more beleaguered than at its start. Fashion and economy both conspire against it, and the rise of the TV novel' - the cycle of plays by a single writer - is another threat. During the 60s the theatre envied television its writers; now the traffic is the other way.
