Oct 3, 1982

Musical Cartoons

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Musical cartoons

MUSIC

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Andy Capp,' 'The Paranormalist' and 'Mass Appeal.'

MOST British musicals of the past few years have been imitation Broadway. There are worse things to be. But there is a native tradition, and Andy Capp (Aldwych) revives it.

Everyone knows that the Andy of Reg Smythe's comic-strip is a drunken, pigeon-fancying, football-playing layabout who treats women abominably and whom his creator never even upbraids for his misdeeds.

Critics in what I would call the new Salad Days tradition ('oh look at me, oh look at me, oh look at me, I'm progressive') rushed last week to condemn him as atypical of the chivalrous, temperate, politically aware north-countrymen they presumably know at first hand. I hope they will be consistent about this, and never say a word in favour of Sir John Falstaff.

The behaviour of both derides the sober, responsible virtues to which we are all supposed to aspire. Falstaff charms and insults Mistress Quickly just as Andy does his Flo, though Andy, being married, does it more aggressively.

Mr Smythe's Andy always wore his cap right down over his eyes, while Tom Courtenay on stage wears his pulled right up. There are sound practical reasons for this: a musical-comedy hero has to see where he is going, or he might trip over the massed ensemble, and an audience that pays to see Mr Courtenay doesn't want vital bits obscured.

Besides being less blinkered than we might have expected, he is softer-centred. He has to cope with unprecedented problems: criticism and (though not quite on the Falstaffian scale) rejection. Flo leaves him, and he has to go for days without changing his socks or -- as far as we can tell -- eating. He does try cooking toast (in a saucepan), but it's beyond him.

All this is only symptomatic of the one great new factor in Andy's life: plot. When funnies turn into musicals the protagonists lose their mythic immobility. They have to act and be acted upon; they develop. Andy Capp delays this process as long as he decently can. The story doesn't start until the last line of the first act.

Before that we have been taken round Andy's milieu: home, pub, streets between. We have watched, in instructive counterpoint, the ladies (an admirably grim and un-uniform chorus line) at work in the factory and the gents getting changed for soccer. A lot of our time has been spent on, or with, Alan Price.

Mr Price is a problem, twice. He wrote the music, and collaborated on the lyrics with Trevor Peacock, who wrote the book. He also narrates and comments, mainly from the piano, though there are attempts -- vain and wasteful, since he isn't an actor -- to make him part of the action. These merely give the show more framework than it needs, like Amadeus.

Then there is the music. It's good to have Mr Price's own singing; he's the most talented musician in British pop, and has been for nearly 20 years. His composing is best here when it is least like itself: in a Formbyish item, 'I Ought to Be Ashamed of Myself,' for Andy to demonstrate how he gets everyone eating out of his hand while he drinks out of their pockets, or in a couple of extended nursery rhymes, one about rolling home at night plastered, the other about waking up next morning. But his production numbers, in jazz-tinged rock, get in the way of the words while supplying little impact of their own.

Andy has a neighbour who, under his wife's thumb, never says a word. He then acquires some money, finds his voice (he actually says 'bollocks to my wife,' which amuses the ladies in the audience no end), buys drinks all round and bursts into song. The actor, Robert Vahey, has pleased us so much that we want him to stop the show, but the material Mr Price gives him is so fussy he doesn't have a chance.

So, structurally and musically, the piece falters. But there is still plenty to enjoy. Mr Courtenay proves himself a consummate singing actor, never losing continuity as he passes from one mode to another and full of shy cunning. Val McLane as Flo heads the bold, beautiful and wholly convincing female brigade.

Michael Mueller is charmingly gormless as a young bridegroom who goes everywhere in a hesitant rush. There are some knockdown comic lines, not all of which can be Mr Smythe's. Braham Murray's staging, with Johanna Bryant's tabloid sets and Sue Lefton's jauntily utilitarian choreography, is the best he has done for a musical.

*

In the title-role of The Paranormalist (Greenwich) Denholm Elliott levitates, which is something the Living Theatre tried and failed for years to accomplish. Later on he performs an exorcism on his middle-aged daughter, who is played by Angela Thorne. She, and two admirers, then join in singing 'The Song is Ended,' though according to Miss Thorne's daughter, played by Catherine Hall, they're singing it wrong.

Thus corrected they try again, and the melody lingers on as the lights fade. It's an effective close, and stirring too; if a 1982 teenager can enlighten her elders on the harmonies of a 1927 Irving Berlin tune there is hope for the world. Compare the pessimism of Howard Barker's 'The Hang of the Gaol,' in which 'Moonlight Becomes You' was mis-titled and attributed to a non-existent composer.

For an exorcism you need devils; and Jonathan Gems fills his characters with them. Miss Thorne is a late-starting psychiatrist who keeps threatening to put Mr Elliott into an old-people's home. (For those of us who grew up on Mr Elliott as a juvenile this is very disturbing.) Her lovers are a television producer (Christopher Godwin) and an American academic (Stephen Creit). Her daughter is a tease who inveighs against education and society until she learns she's got her A-levels.

Mr Gems makes the erotic and professional torments credible (most erotic torments are) and all the actors are good, though Mr Elliott, being comparatively on the side of the angels, has less to work with than the others. He gets fine writing where the others get wit. Moment to moment Mr Gems seems in command -- he even writes good old-fashioned curtain-lines -- but this play, more elegant than his 'Naked Robots,' but less lively, has also to be written down as promising.

*

It's better on religion, though, than Bill C. Davis's Mass Appeal (Lyric, Hammersmith), which is the standard young rebel and nice old conformist play given Catholic trappings and an ameliorative ending (also standard). It shows that what Broadway takes for serious remains unchanged (though this piece, sadly, originated off-Broadway at the well-thought-of Manhattan Theatre Club), despite the fashionable focus having changed from disability to faith. Our unbelief is not helped by the casting of Gordon Jackson; though we keep telling ourselves there's no reason why a New York priest shouldn't be a Scots immigrant, he makes the colloquialisms seem very self-conscious.

The Tom Allen Centre (round the corner from the Theatre Royal, Stratford East) has commenced operations with Fuente Ovejuna, by Lope de Vega, a play much written of, but seldom seen. It depicts a Spanish village defeating a local tyrant by collective action and -- though the performance and Steve Gooch's demotic adaptation are a bit sheepish about this -- royal intervention. Otherwise both are very lively; I doubt, indeed, that Mr Gooch has ever written better. Last performance is tonight.
