Oct 2, 1977

Nights Of Opera And Telly

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Nights of opera and telly

ROBERT CUSHMAN, our theatre critic, steps outside his usual territory.

NO EXPERT, I have the impression that opera production is on the move; and moving--as nearly always happens with a stylised form--in the direction of naturalism.

Just as we traditionally applaud directors for the amount of 'lyricism' they can inject into realistic texts, so we cling in the lyric theatre to anything approaching recognisable behaviour.

When this behaviour is as intelligently organised as in Peter Hall's mordantly moral production of Don Giovanni, which fitted--like other visiting shows before it--quite beautifully into the Lyttelton, we acclaim a triumph.

Even finer--maybe the best production of anything I have seen this year--was the Edinburgh Festival Carmen, which framed the story within the memory of the imprisoned Don Jose. Effective in itself, the device was even more impressive as evidence that the director, Piero Faggioni, had cared.

The crowd scenes--one thought of a crowd rather than a chorus, which I regard as a compliment--were the best controlled, both for meaning and spectacle, that I have encountered for a very long time, and the single set was a powerful influence for unity. Grey and parched, it left the performers plenty of room and yet--with its Andalusian undulations--it dazzled.

By comparison with these, the Coliseum Ring, whose third cycle was completed last night, is uncertainly directed. Ralph Koltai's sets suggest there are limits to what can be done with steel poles: left lying in neat horizontal bunches they make the place look like a building-site. At moments these associations are transcended and we do seem to be, as I think was intended, in a mythological wasteland, outside our own experience of time and space but still conscious of it pressing upon us.

I happen to be a sucker for huge impossible works--I can hardly wait for the National to get round to Emperor and Galilean, maybe even The Dynasts--but I am sure that the more outrageous the author's demands, the more literally they have to be met.

I have no patience with critics or directors who try to dispense with the wonders of Peer Gynt, say, by claiming that they exist only in the hero's mind and so need not be represented. I am pretty sure that Wagner wanted his Freya in The Rhinegold really to be obscured by heaps of gold, not just to be measured up against a pile of greyish slabs by a couple of outsize tailors.

And I am positive that when one giant kills another he should do more than bop him gently on the head, while his victim totters obligingly in the wings.

These baldnesses in the staging stand out against some magnificent acting, particularly from the unheroic tenors: Emile Belcourt's cankered Loge, with the wit and intelligence of Lucifer, and John Dobson, whose cringings and fawnings as Mime were a revelatory demonstration of the personality to be squeezed from a vocal line.

You cannot, I think, ease your way gradually into an operatic performance; it has to be there from the first note and--apart from earning my layman's gratitude for his unwavering audibility--Mr Dobson's blacksmith-dwarf was (and for once the word seems thoroughly appropriate) riveting.

*

Still poaching, an afterword on Tom Stoppard's television play Professional Foul, not I think the departure that has been claimed, since Mr Stoppard's humane view of politics was already plainly evident in Travesties and in Jumpers: the latter is particularly relevant, since it also juxtaposed philosophers and athletes, and since a single pun in it--'I'm not quite happy about your Ethics'--is almost the key to the new piece, with its football-fancying English professor forced in Czechoslovakia to reappraise a code of conduct that happens also to be a professional standard.

Not all the footballing parallels work, but no other playwright could have found so apt an image for a calculated breaking of rules and then have devised so many reflections of it within the action of the play. Just about everyone in the play commits a professional foul at some point, and we are made to judge means and end in every case.

There is one scene--the hero's encounter with his imprisoned student's wife and son--that goes deeper emotionally than anything he has written before. It was faultlessly acted by Peter Barkworth, and delicately directed by Michael Lindsay-Hogg, who--provided he chooses something more tractable than The White Devil--is welcome back in the theatre anytime.

*

I must get back myself. Bubbling Brown Sugar (Royalty) returns me to business, though it should have been pleasure. But this musical tour of legendary (i.e., inter-war) Harlem raises awkward questions which the level of performance here is not high enough to subdue. Billy Daniels does little more than smile; Elaine Delmar loses all her vocal subtlety in a theatre. Lon Satton has more of a spark, and Helen Genzler, attacking Sweet Georgia Brown, can do physical and vocal high-kicks simultaneously.

Otherwise things are slapdash. Nobody expected, or wanted, a documentary expose of the ghetto but even musically the show is incomplete (no reefer songs, no poverty songs) and usually anachronistic. Impersonations of Billy Eckstine and Louis Armstrong are thrown in because the performers can do them; then, perversely, Billie Holiday's God Bless the Child is (after a hushed but seriously folks introduction) taken, musically speaking, as gospel.

Miss Holiday, by the way, once said of Armstrong's crowd-pleasing propensities, 'Sure, Pops [Toms], but he [Toms] with class.' This show [Toms] without class; it has energy but, except in the finale, that is not enough.

*

The billing of Tufff by Billie Brown presents pronunciation problems (is the u silent and what do you do with 'fff' except shout?), but the play itself (at the Theatre Upstairs) is simple. It pleases, however. Three Australian 12-year-olds interact on a beach; two fancy themselves as hard men, the third is in a wheelchair. The fight is rigged in his favour, but this is a play in which reconciliation is more important than conflict. On the way, shifting allegiances hold the interest. The play belongs to the small, rare genre of the stage lyric, and Diane Cilento's direction (more tender than tufff) is properly geared to it.
