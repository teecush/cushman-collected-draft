Sep 13, 1981

In Stalin's Labour Camp

Robert Cushman · The Observer

THEATRE

In Stalin's labour camp

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a play by Alexander Solzhenitsyn.

ERIC BENTLEY once wrote of Russian realism that "everything -- every single thing -- that we call either comic or romantic has to be there on stage." You could say the same of Shakespeare; no wonder the RSC does so many Russians.

Alexander Solzhenitsyn's The Love-Girl and the Innocent (Aldwych) is a panoramic picture of life in a Stalinist labour-camp. Our natural impulse is to soft-pedal the horror; and it happens that the play makes that process rather easy. It is concerned with survival rather than suffering; it is set just after the war, when the camp is said to be "a health resort" in comparison with previous conditions. And this camp ranks low on the current scale; one of the worst fears hanging over the prisoners is that of being sent on somewhere worse.

We don't see much overt brutality; the ruling forces are graft and bribery. The most powerful man in the camp appears to be the doctor, himself a prisoner. This is an enclosed society, obeying the same rules as any other. The audience leaves the theatre thinking that if that's a labour camp, it's really not so bad. That cannot have been the intention.

The most impressive aspects of Clifford Williams's production are mechanical. Ralph Koltai's set incorporates a foundry and a railroad track, whose purpose is to transport prisoners; a new consignment arrives at the end, scared and shivering. This is the first time in the evening anyone has looked cold.

Hunger and fatigue are elements mentioned in the text, but never dramatised; perhaps because Solzhenitsyn lacks theatrical skill, perhaps because he takes them for granted. But a production has to supply them somehow; without that centre, acting becomes a business merely of getting through the lines. As usual the more experienced players survive best; the younger, left to fend for themselves, die of exposure: an apt reflection of the play's world, perhaps, but not a helpful one. The play holds attention, but it jumps confusingly about from one character to another, and when it finally settles on the two in the title the result is bathos.

The love-girl lives by leasing her body; the innocent is an ex-officer who tries to bring some fairness and reason to the job of labour supervisor, only to be dislodged by someone with a talent for greasing up to the authorities. Together they are our old Hollywood friends, the well-bred young idealist and the girl from the wrong side of the tracks who ain't no good for him. They illumine nothing that the rest of the play does not make clear; here Solzhenitsyn himself falls short of the standards of Russian realism.

*

In C. P. Taylor's Good (Warehouse) another idealist walks through a concentration camp, this time as an official. A nice man who joins the Nazis will inevitably end up supervising gas-chambers; or, alternatively, even the men of Auschwitz must at some time have been conventionally decent people: it would be interesting to know from which end Mr Taylor started.

Alan Howard, when not playing trumpeting heroes, has a fine line in moony myopia; he plays an academic with problems: a slatternly wife, a blind and troublesome mother, an attractive girl-pupil, a Jewish best friend. He writes a novel which seems to advocate euthanasia; the Nazis spot him; he embarks on a steep self-justifying road: the burning of books, the night of broken glass, gassing of mental cases, gassing of Jews.

It sounds schematic, but Mr Taylor, aided by Mr Howard's nervous solitariness, keeps it fresh: juggling time-schemes, varying the hero's excuses, and equipping him with a marvellously useful neurosis. At the crises of his life he hears music, music he knows to be imaginary (at one point he is treated to Kurt Weill's September Song a full five years before its composition, though here of course it's the production that's having hallucinations); when he enters Auschwitz there is finally real music in his ears: the prisoners' orchestra. No recent play has had so macabrely elegant an ending.

Sometimes Mr Taylor's dialogue contorts itself for too long at a time; the hero's girl (Felicity Dean) says once too often that all they can do is love one another -- though her deterioration is nicely sketched too: Love indeed is what our man seeks, with fatal lonely promiscuity, from both his old Jewish friend and his new Nazi friend. These two roles are played by Joe Melia and Pip Miller; Domini Blythe is the wife; Howard Davies directed: congratulations to all, not least to Mr Taylor for somehow combining Holocaust with Pennies from Heaven.

*

A Waste of Time is a dramatisation of Proust; the cheek of both enterprise and title identifies it as the work of the Glasgow Citizens' crowd, whom it represents both at their best and worst. The worst resides in a streak of self-congratulatory cleverness, admittedly less evident in Robert David Macdonald's adaptations than in his original plays. This extends to casting middle-aged mondaines (this being the stratum to which the company is most attracted) with young actresses who proceed, in self-defence, to turn in impersonations of Hermione Gingold.

There is also a cavalier disregard for the audience's mental comfort; neither text nor programme offers much clue as to who is whom in the first act, as Marcel's memories go whizzing by. But even these faults have their virtues; the acting imposes itself, and you begin to respond, not to individuals, but to a collection of delicate monsters. Giles Havergal, the theatre's director, turns actor to deliver a choice piece of the highest camp: a towering falsetto Baron de Charlus, terrifying and terrified.

Steven Dartnall delivers a dry, precise Marcel; the second and best act concerns his relationship with Albertine, who is cast in double focus as girl and boy; we see both the heterosexual affair of the book, and the homosexual affair it masks. Finally there is the usual splendour of Philip Prowse's sets, lit by Gerry Jenkinson as a series of decaying old masters. Mr Prowse also directs; and his capacity for squeezing his actors, to maximum effect, into the interstices of his decor, is pretty extraordinary.

*

A literary week: Solzhenitsyn, Proust and Dusty Hughes, he standing in for Johnson and Boswell. He has brought their double-act to the stage in Heaven and Hell (Royal Court), which title may prefigure the distance between Edinburgh and London (a journey undertaken by both Boswell and the play), but probably has more to do with sexual pleasure, as embraced with regrets by Boswell and abandoned with regrets by Johnson.

Their struggles are brought delectably before us, in designs by John Byrne and in dialogue full of quotations; Mr Hughes's own linking bits don't really measure up. But he has given the material shape, especially in its first half (the second rambles), and has set up, in Richard Wilson's production, two fine performances. Jimmy Chisholm makes a prim bouncing Bozzy, the fashionable world his banana-skin, and Jonathan Adams is a vast growl and slouch of a Doctor.
