Jul 15, 1979

Glenda And Cleopatra

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Glenda and Cleopatra

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Peter Brook's production at the Aldwych.

IN THE last scene of Antony and Cleopatra (Aldwych) the heroine has a speech describing the dead Antony. "His delights were dolphin-like; they showed his back above the element they lived in."

It is magnificent writing, and Glenda Jackson delivers it beautifully. But, making full allowance for the fact that we never value anything properly until we have lost it, the words in this context do not make sense. They speak less of Cleopatra's love for her deceased Roman than of her admiration for him.

Love may take many forms, and the fact that Miss Jackson's reading hardly permits her to say a civil word to Alan Howard's Antony from one end of the play to the other may not preclude a real affection between them. She never seems, though, to have any kind of respect for him: she is the kind who would find it uncool to respect or admire anyone. So maybe, when she remembers Antony in those extravagant terms, she is just fooling herself. That, too, could be a defensible interpretation, though it impoverishes the play's last movement and does not seem to accord with what Miss Jackson does elsewhere in this scene, which is to present a woman finally stripped of illusions.

In fact she gets to that stage rather early. Trying to cheer Antony up after one of their military defeats, she cries, "Since my lord is Antony again I will be Cleopatra," and goes into a brief whirling dervish routine, presumably meant to represent the conventional idea of eastern promise. But after a couple of twirls, she gives it up; she really cannot play the Cleopatra game any more.

But Miss Jackson has never seemed a woman who would play that game anyway; she has other ways of keeping her man and they are cleverly counterpointed in this production against the more conventional seductiveness of her maids. I had better come clean and admit that I actually preferred the more conventional seductiveness. Everybody has his own idea of the kind of woman who remains irresistible despite behaving unforgivably, and Miss Jackson is not mine.

Which is not to deny her considerable qualities. She can jump instantly from one emotion to another, and though this may not quite equal the infinite variety needed to ensnare Antony, it enables her to do a thorough scare-job on the messenger who brings her news of his marriage. She gives us most of the strengths of Cleopatra but few of the weaknesses. She never looks likely to lose a kingdom.

Mr Howard, on the other hand, looks more than likely to lose an empire. It does not matter too much that he and Miss Jackson are playing, as the jargon unhelpfully has it, in different styles; they are after all playing different people. But except in the first scene (where Shakespeare more or less disposes of the love-interest and all the players have to do is go along with him) there is no rapprochement between them.

Hovering somewhere in the wings is an icy view of the play in which the protagonists only need each other to camouflage their personal and political weaknesses; but Mr Howard's is too swirling and choppy a performance (forceful, yes; there's always another bellow left in him) and never lets us get a steady look.

Peter Brook's production is frustratingly uneven. Scene after scene has been dredged for meaning which is then offered to us crisply and clearly. But overall that rhythm is lacking; Rome melts seamlessly into Egypt, with Cleopatra crying out for Charmian before Octavius and Lepidus have left the stage. Then all is spoiled by having everyone enter at a giggly run: a monumental Shakespearean cliche.

Jonathan Pryce's maturing Octavius retains its Stratford fascination, but has also acquired a London gabble. Two others stand out: Paola Dionisotti as Charmian and David Suchet, whose Pompey is the most fully Shakespearean performance of the evening -- which is to say that his is a solidly realistic portrait of a main-chancer dashed by his own impressionable weakness, who can also -- in his moments of contemplation or prevision -- capture the quality that makes the play leap out of time.

Leigh Jackson's Reggae Britannia (Royal Court) is an artless title that accurately reflects an unfortunate blatancy in the play itself. It should also serve as fair warning to the tender-eared. I find reggae unbearably monotonous, but as the numbers here ground each into its tenth minute I could recognise a force in the bellicose vocals and pounding guitar work that must appeal to those who like their music relentless. The question at issue in the play is how relentless?

The band in question, known as Pitch Black, is playing a school-gig, invited there by a Jamaican kid who hopes to find in the music a reflection of his own racial consciousness. This puts him slightly to the left of the group's leader, who wants reggae to have a cultural identity but doesn't mind proclaiming that identity to whites; who is himself more radical than the group's singer, who doesn't care much who hears her.

These positions are laid out on the line, but nothing much is done to flesh them out. Subsidiary white characters are strictly from stock. May be the blacks are more subtle, or written than I realised; it was difficult for me to tell from the performances on hand. It is a compliment to Mr Jackson, who is white, that he has written dialogue that slips so easily from the lips of these black performers but, though Debby Bishop is appealingly gritty and Ram John Holder artfully laid-back, they are pretty sloppy at communicating it to the audience.

Ten years ago, the Living Theatre of New York had the trend-followers of London crowding into the Roundhouse. Returning now with their Prometheus, they played on Thursday to rows of empty seats.

Well, their paranoia level has commendably in these aggrieving circumstances dropped considerably but then so has their level of presentation, which was hardly shattering before.

Imagery consists mainly of having a naked actor bound to a wheel, hands and feet splayed apart. The myth unfolds so confusingly that it has to be spelled out in the programme, which is scarcely more enlightening. What we used to call the underground has rarely considered that its audiences may be less deeply into its concerns than it is itself. As the actors wander round the hall spouting fragments, or just lie there and writhe, they end up with a very private form of theatre: what they, I think, would call elitist in others.

Anyway, Prometheus brings man the gift of fire, but man abuses it, or at least his rulers do, and Judith Malina, co-director of the troupe, complains that she is cold and cannot pay her gas-bills, and suggests, by an association of words if not ideas, the storming of the Winter Palace. Which means that Act Two is the Russian Revolution, though I didn't stay for it.

I don't know if Miss Malina is aware she is being disarming at such a moment or when, as the god-tormented Io in a Valkyrie's headpiece, she moans that she is unhappy and wants to go home, but disarming is what she is. It is difficult, now, to be angry with the Living Theatre. Their idolaters are, or were, another matter.
