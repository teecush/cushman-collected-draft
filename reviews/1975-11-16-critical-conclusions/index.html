Nov 16, 1975

Critical Conclusions

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Critical conclusions

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

I WOULD like to save the English stage. It would make me feel good and after two-and-a-half years in this space I cannot help but feel that I owe the theatre a living.

The trouble is I'm bashful. Of course the trade of critic is a presumptuous one, but I reckon that I have reached the justified limits of presumption when I inform a dramatist that I do not want what he is offering me. It is not for me to tell him what I do want; in the first place he would then find me even more insufferable than he does now, and in the second I don't know.

I am not sure that I would recognise a piece of constructive criticism if I wrote it, but I do know, and dislike, prescriptive criticism -- criticism which instructs writers or theatres precisely what they should be producing. It occurs at a minor level, annoying but probably harmless, when we complain, as one reviewer did, that if the National Theatre wanted to do an Irish play something by O'Casey would have been much more relevant (I hate that word) than `The Playboy of the Western World.' Maybe they didn't want to do `an Irish play'; maybe they wanted to do `The Playboy.'

Prescriptive criticism on a larger scale has won its good name through its mistaken association with the two most brilliant of modern drama critics: Bernard Shaw and Kenneth Tynan, both of whom were spectacularly good at dismissing what they didn't want. As for what they did want: inveighing against `our drama's prevalent genre, the Loamshire play' (which, by the way, has made a triumphant return this year with `Murder at the Vicarage'), Mr Tynan proclaimed `we need plays about cabmen and demi-gods . . . warriors politicians and grocers.' That is the properly vague note of the theatrical prophet. It is true that when `Look Back in Anger' arrived two years later he had the wit and the taste to announce that this was what he had hoped for all along. Helpful journalism consists in being wise as soon after the event as possible. But being wise before it is the prerogative of the practitioners -- who themselves often only blunder into discovery.

Recently as in a time-warp, Mr Tynan has been at it again, this time on television and with the addition of a demagogically dictatorial note. Pinter and Simon Gray are castigated for concentrating on small middle-class agonies while the world around them crumbles. But unless you have a new cure for cancer (which is unlikely) you are not necessarily doing anything useful by expatiating on it and ignoring your own chilblains. (The terms are Mr Tynan's own.) I am not advocating escapism; civic problems can actually be an escape for a man immersed in his own. If Messrs Pinter and Gray were commanded to write on obviously public themes they might write very bad plays or no plays at all. That would be a loss. And unless they are actually hiding such plays -- in their heads or in their bottom drawers -- or are stopping other people from writing them, criticism should accept them as they are. If you hate them say so, but don't lecture them.

I feel the inadequacies of criticism with particular acuteness this week, having discovered a theatrical creator who can obviously do without all of us. Oh What! (Bush), an improvised show presented by the Hull Truck Theatre Company under the direction of Mike Bradwell, has defeated the negative critic, since there is so very little wrong with it, and as for explaining the play, Mr Bradwell has done that himself with perfect clarity.

In an interview handed to me with my programme he says that his object is to present characters living in Hull and confused by fashionable ideas. Thus Celia is `a rich university graduate who has discovered the drug culture eight years too late.' (It was always too late to discover the drug culture.) She lives in a country cottage and dreams of ecological self sufficiency. Yes, that's Celia; and if you detect affection as well as mockery in that dry summary you will be well prepared for Rachel Bell's performance.

One of the things she has acquired in her retreat is an appalling cold; or perhaps she was born with nasal constriction. Everything she says is on one note; when she is earnest (as she frequently is) she must rely on intensity rather than variations of pitch to make her points. When she protests that `Christmas is no excuse for eating dead animals,' you realise that there can be beauty even in catarrh. Miss Bell, protesting that just because she's a vegetarian people think she's fair game, is dumpy, solid and hilarious.

The timing of Miss Bell, David Hatton and Joseph Peters (like that of Matthew Guinness in Mike Leigh's `Babies Grow Old') is from a comic realm to which it seems only improvised performances have access. And though the evening, being mainly a series of conversations, lacks an obvious shape, a burst of anger from Mr Peters as a bullying soldier back from Belfast, clinging like the others to a threadbare code, rounds it off with peculiar conviction. Sometimes the dialogue slips from authentic to forced intimacy (like a radio phone-in); the relationship between Celia and her sister, a fashion-writer, only becomes believable when it is stylised by having the two of them step out of context to sing the Irving Berlin song `Sisters.' The show is musically very strong.

*

Its songs gave me more pleasure than those Carl Davis has composed for Cranford (Theatre Royal, Stratford), though these, like the rest of the show, maintain strict decorum. They have been composed, agreeably if too copiously, as pastiche; I did wonder why the absence of men in this Victorian village should be lamented to the tune, more or less, of `When Johnny Comes Marching Home.' If he did return he would be done for; the masculine mortality rate in Cranford is something shocking. One man is mown down by an emergent railway train; another returns from Paris fatally diseased; even a male cat cops it.

The village is mainly inhabited by spinsters making the show all of a-twitter and something of a-flutter. Penelope Lee is most tender and forbearing as the elderly heroine, and as her best friend (she says, though they hardly exchange a word) Jeanne Watts is contrastingly trim and tart. Much of the original novel's sharpness has been lost and some episodes in the second half are dull and dewy, but the evening (like Sandy Wilson's `Valmouth,' unsexed and decamped) has charm.
