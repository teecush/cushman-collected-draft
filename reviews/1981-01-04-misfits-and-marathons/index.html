Jan 4, 1981

Misfits--And Marathons

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Misfits--and marathons

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN sums up the achievements of 1980.

IN New York a couple of years ago, at the time of Sweeney Todd and The Elephant Man, I remarked that it was a great season for shows about Victorian misfits.

Nineteen-eighty was the year in which both these productions reached central London stages, along with Nicholas Nickleby and The Streets of London. With The Ticket-of-Leave Man, a nineteenth-century melodrama spiced with social comment announced for production by the National Theatre, it is obvious that the trend still has some distance to run.

There is escapism at work, all right. The word has acquired murky connotations; youngish playwrights use it to mean taking the bread out of their mouths. What is being escaped from, though, is not contemporary life, but the general run of contemporary theatre. What Sweeney Todd and Nicholas Nickleby -- the undoubted peaks of the London year -- had to offer were size and intensity, together with the sure-footed craftsmanship of men who knew what they were doing, even though the ground was fresh to them, and who were extending themselves because their material demanded it.

They also capped the two most important streams of work in the English-speaking theatre of the past decade. Sweeney Todd was the fifth of the Harold Prince-Stephen Sondheim musicals; not quite the best (that, I think, was Pacific Overtures), but, in its narrative force and psychological power, the most expressive of its composer. Nicholas Nickleby gathered up everything that has been achieved by Trevor Nunn and his collaborators since the RSC turnabout of 1976.

In fact the line goes farther back than that. The Theatre Workshop actress Toni Palmer has recently speculated on the production's debt to Joan Littlewood; and it's true that the last show I can remember whose cast seemed so thoroughly committed to what they were presenting and how they were presenting it -- to be so much a part of the play -- was Oh What a Lovely War. In both cases the commitment stemmed from responsibility; they had worked up the material themselves.

They had not, however, written it; and it is here that the RSC's literary bias works in its favour. (The contrary animus practically killed Theatre Workshop.) It's amusing now to remember the beginnings of the fringe, the cries of the Sixties and early Seventies. No more masterpieces. We don't want art, we want events.

There was a certain force to them. Art, as the best critics -- Kenneth Tynan, Pauline Kael -- have realised, is a useless term, signifying whatever one approves of (and can hit other judges over the head with). A great production of a classic is a uniquely energising experience, for audience and participants, but most productions go from ordinary to painful. Who wouldn't rather have an event?

The trouble is you don't achieve one simply by dispensing with a script: the point has been proved, miserably and often. Nor do you get it by 'restructuring' an existing play; it has its own values which are likely to be stronger than those of any director. He may spoil it, but it will murder him, making nobody happy.

With Nicholas Nickleby, by luck or cunning and maybe unrepeatably, the RSC has found the perfect solution: a great text which, not being written in theatrical form, had to be restructured by its interpreters. The fact that our second greatest dramatic genius, the second source of energy in English literature, did not write plays has turned out a theatrical blessing. And, form apart, Dickens is the friendlier and more amenable of the two. (You don't really collaborate with Shakespeare. Ralph Richardson once said he found him too great a genius to be chummy.)

All that really stands in the way is length; most dramatisations squeeze out everything except plot, and a few catch-phrases. That marathon theatre disposes of the alleged incompatibility of the two forms was proved by Ken Campbell's version of Illuminatus. I said as much when reviewing it, apparently inspiring Mike Alfreds of Shared Experience to have a go at Bleak House, a production which I imagine influenced the RSC. (Why put a novel on the stage? To share the experience. The pleasure.) If the RSC's achievement exceeds Mr Alfreds's, that is partly because it offers a theatrical statement rather than a virtuoso demonstration that the thing can be done, and partly because of superior resources; in the end a show with 42 actors, a beehive set and lots of music is more fun.

There have been other RSC epics: The Wars of the Roses, the Terry Hands histories, and, also last year (a staggering thought in itself), The Greeks. We came out of them all happily, punchy, impressed and excited, and we applauded the actors. But we never felt it was their show; the concept came first. But then none of them was essentially comic.

To keep the comic spirit alive and at full pressure over eight and a half hours is an extraordinary achievement, and it induces a special exhilaration in the audience. The experience of Nicholas Nickleby is literally unique; though it was prefigured in Trevor Nunn's productions of The Comedy of Errors, The Alchemist and Once in a Lifetime. These were not just funny, but joyous; as his Romeo and Juliet, Macbeth, King Lear, Three Sisters, Juno and the Paycock were not just moving, but searing. What links them is an apparent concern for the human components: actors and audiences. (The range may explain why Nunn has never been accorded the cult-status of the puritan directors -- Lindsay Anderson, William Gaskill, Peter Gill.)

With Sweeney and Nickleby as beacons, we can survey the year for other shared experiences. Shared Experience's own five-man production of Cymbeline was certainly one; the year's best Shakespeare, and proof that the fringe can tackle the classics on its own terms but without resorting to grubby iconoclasm. Juno and The Greeks I've already mentioned. Adrian Noble's Duchess of Malfi was the best production yet at the Royal Exchange, Barry Kyle's Maid's Tragedy one of the best at the Other Place, and The Wild Duck an absorbing return to form for Michael Blakemore.

After which we get to individual performances; to Jonathan Pryce's Hamlet (I've cooled on the production itself, maybe in reaction to the ignorant claims made on its behalf by Christopher Logue, who was in it), to Julie Walters's exuberance in Educating Rita and Julia McKenzie's high notes in On the Twentieth Century, to the riveting calm of James Grout in Make and Break and Lindsay Duncan in Julius Caesar, and to Alec McCowen's breakdown in The Browning Version; the only performance that really turned me on at the National Theatre, whose year was seldom less than respectable, but hardly ever more.

That assessment includes The Romans in Britain, one of the better new plays of the year. Ron Hutchinson's The Irish Play was an ebullient advance with a stark ending, a child dancing in debris. (Some took it to be hopeful; if they're right, the play is as sentimental as its enemies make out.) Paul Kember's Not Quite Jerusalem was an arrival; other promising playwrights were critics (Dusty Hughes, Peter Jenkins, Francis King), which is charming. The best new play, Michael Frayn's Make and Break, was brave, sad and funny, but not quite a knock-out.

Nineteen-eighty was proof that it's possible to have a great year in the theatre without great new plays. If that weren't true, we'd all have cut our throats years ago.
