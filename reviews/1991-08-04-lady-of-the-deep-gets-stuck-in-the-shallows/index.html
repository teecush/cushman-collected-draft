Aug 4, 1991

Lady Of The Deep Gets Stuck In The Shallows

Robert Cushman · The Independent

Lady of the deep gets stuck in the shallows

THEATRE ROBERT CUSHMAN

LET no one say that Ibsen's later plays are invariably dark and obscure. The Lady from the Sea has a happy ending that can be seen coming a nautical mile off. A minute into the show a painter, working on a picture of a mermaid, says that "the lady who lives here inspired me to paint it". Enter Ellida, the lady of the painting and of the title. She has been for a dip ("she swims every morning, whatever the weather") and surfaces in this production cropped and shiny in a green towel. She looks healthy but asked if she feels refreshed she replies: "I don't; I never do."

She is pining for the open sea beside which she grew up and which she associates with a sailor to whom she once plighted her troth and who then disappeared on suspicion of murder. Now he arrives to reclaim her from her husband, an amiable if incurably land-locked doctor who would be many women's ideal son-in-law. A dab hand at a diagnosis he picks up immediately on his wife's complaint that if she stays with him it will be "not of my own free will". He gives her her freedom - you half-expect him to write a prescription for it - and she exercises it by electing to stay with him. That is what the play has promised to show, that is what it does show, and there is not a note of irony to be heard.

Heidi Thomas's adaptation, from which I have been quoting, is brisk and generally colloquial and it enables director Jules Wright to deliver the play in what may be record time. There are no significant pauses, and few insignificant ones. The principal emotion transmitted is a terror of being thought pretentious.

In the last major British production, Vanessa Redgrave gave a visual and verbal resonance to Ellida's marine compulsion that transcended polemics. The one before that, also with Redgrave though in a different role, found its texture in the life of the household, this being one of the few Ibsen plays to have one. Ellida attracts men as ineluctably as a Chekhov chatelaine, and they sublimate their feelings by hovering around her two stepdaughters. The elder of these is courted by her ex-tutor who wants to take her away and show her the world, which is fine with her, but also wants to marry her, which isn't. She looks like repeating her stepmother's mistake with a far less sympathetic partner. Or maybe they'll be all right.

But in either case we have here two people at a crisis of their lives, and it passes belief that they would get through it at an even pace, in unvarying profile, without once taking a moment to sit and think. But that is how this production goes. People face one another and deliver their lines, as conversationally as possible. When passion seems unavoidable they stomp about a bit and switch to a tone of petulant self-pity.

Kathryn Pogson as Ellida has a still, troubled presence that persuades you something is going on beneath, but it never breaks surface. "I can't see him any more" - referring, at the height of her disarray, to the husband she cares for - is not a line that can plausibly be muttered. And with the central performance so battened-down, the others sink automatically lower; for a man in imminent danger of losing the wife he loves Hugh Fraser's doctor is amazingly unruffled. Alister Cameron as the local jack-of-all-trades is nice, but his is the smallest role. We learn that mature Ibsen played purely at dialogue level seems impossibly glib.

After The Lady from the Sea, The Boys from Syracuse. (Next week The Girl from Nantucket, The Man from U.N.C.L.E., and The Creature from the Black Lagoon.) This is the 1938 Broadway musical based, with considerable resource and fidelity, on The Comedy of Errors. The opening number, "I Had Twins", handles the cumbrous exposition a lot more adroitly than did Shakespeare. Otherwise Lorenz Hart's lyrical wit shines less in the obvious comedy songs than in the bitter-sweet spin he puts on the love ballads. In fact the best-known lines are the best: "falling in love with love is falling for make believe", "this can't be love because I feel so well" - the latter sung by the visiting Antipholus to (I think I've got this right) his brother's sister-in-law. Meanwhile the prodigal grace of Richard Rodgers' music extends to what, for any other composer, would be throw-away numbers; mark the tripping verse to "What Can You Do with a Man?"

Grace is what George Abbott's book rather lacks, but it keeps the jokes coming, and some of them are funny, and Judi Dench's good-natured production enjoys them all. It reaffirms that, barring the occasional Anything Goes, musicals of this vintage are more fun when done on a modest scale, and it plays wittily with its own pastoral setting.

The action revolves around a parkland bandstand, and James Merrifield's bold, gorgeous costumes make the ladies look like animated bell-tents. Accents are emphatically American, with Jenny Galloway's frustrated wench a Brooklynese triumph. Louise Gold, one of our best soubrettes, makes a valiant prima donna. Gillian Bevan sings beautifully. Musical direction is excellent.

As long as wars leave human debris Euripides' Women of Troy will be the world's most topical play: so pertinent as almost to defy revival. The women awaiting deportation and enslavement are eternal. The murder of the boy-child Astyanax, slaughtered for policy, is the greatest atrocity in world drama, a breach in nature. Millennia later, Edward Bond in The Woman presented it as the way of the world. By so much have we fallen.

The temptation to cheapen the play with facile parallels must be enormous, but Katie Mitchell's production never succumbs. Nothing is vague; every line means something, either as fierce lament or - where the gods or Helen are involved - bitter comedy. The style, which involves a lot of perfectly controlled chanting and dancing by a large ensemble, is extraordinarily strong.

I didn't like all the choices (Kathryn Hunter's crazy Cassandra, a Jewish-American rather than a Greek princess, I found especially hard to take) but the point is that they are choices, intelligently made and uncompromisingly carried through. Paola Dionisotti's flinty Hecuba is too young to suggest the mother of them all, Barbara Flynn's bereaved Andromache almost too selflessly stoical to be moving, but they are fine performances and great figureheads.

The Lady from the Sea, Riverside Studios (081-748 3354); The Boys from Syracuse, Open Air, Regent's Park (071-486-2431); Women of Troy, Gate (071-229 0706).

Irving Wardle is on holiday.
