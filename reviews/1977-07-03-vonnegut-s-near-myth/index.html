Jul 3, 1977

Vonnegut's Near-Myth

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Vonnegut's near-myth

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the new plays in London.

THE HERO of Happy Birthday, Wanda June (Bush) is called Harold Ryan. He is a hero in the antiquated sense that he goes out and kills things, staying away from home for years on end.

Home, in this case, is a city in the United States; Tanya McCallin has designed a remarkably convincing apartment (the double doors are particularly good), which happens to have animal-cries for doorbells, skins hung all over the walls, and antlers sticking out most other places. Harold's game is big game; presumed dead in the jungle, he returns to the consternation of his wife Penelope. Kurt Vonnegut is hardly a realist; his play is, if you will pardon my lisp, a near-myth.

Harold tries, as he always has, to frighten everyone: his wife's suitors into flight, she and her son into love, honour and especially obedience. He can scare them, yes; but he cannot impress them. In the decade or more of his absence, there have been changes. His comrade-in-arms, Looseleaf Harper, who once dropped the bomb on Nagasaki and is beginning to feel sorry about it, catches on quicker than he does. Looseleaf registers that there are nudes on the news-stands and profanity just everywhere, and his diagnosis ("something very important about sex must have happened while we were out of the country") is both accurate and endearing.

That really is the trouble with the play; Harold comes back to a world whose inhabitants, on stage and in the auditorium, can see right through him. There is no need to cut him down to size; the play keeps reiterating something we already know. This makes, it is true, for audience empathy, and there were stifled shrieks of approval when Penelope turned on Harold and asserted her independence. I approved of her, too, but I don't necessarily go to the theatre to be agreed with.

The play is weak on action and on character, Harold being both boorish and boring, and Penelope the mere skeletal idea of a liberated woman. The actors cannot make more of them, and Dusty Hughes's production is understandably wooden. There are, however, many good lines; Looseleaf acquires enough of them to pass, in Manning Redwood's shiningly bashful performance, for a person. There is also a superstructure which, since it subsists on language alone, is very pleasant. It is set in Heaven where, apparently, everybody goes, from an SS war criminal to Wanda June, who got run over on her ninth birthday.

The implication -- that macho big-gamesmen and peace workers will all end up together playing shuffleboard (which, according to Mr Vonnegut, is what they do in Heaven) -- is piquant, though I doubt if it qualifies as an irony. The children hired to play Wanda June and the younger Ryan are magnificent.

*

Some time before the beginning of Much Ado About Nothing (Aldwych) there seems to have been a passage of love between Beatrice and Benedick which she took seriously and he forgot. He responds to her raillery in kind, since he feels he owes it to himself and to his masculinity, but she is the one who starts the fights. John Barton, who directed this superb RSC production (now restaged to advantage by Peter Stevenson), has noted that Benedick repents in prose, and Beatrice in verse. So Donald Sinden ("Love me! Why, it must be requited") greets his new dawn with gratified surprise. While he strives to keep his defences in good order, Judi Dench drops hers; she walks slowly from her hiding-place and makes of 10 patterned lines the most magical two minutes in London. You can see anger departing, chased by happiness. Underlying this performance, as with all this actress's best work, is a feeling of joyous surprise, shared by the audience. She is a mistress of mixed emotions, delineating each component with absolute clarity. Her double-takes, vocal and visual, match Mr Sinden's.

His eyes are not fully opened till the church scene; still one of the boys, he is about to follow his friends and it is almost by chance that he stays for Beatrice. When he makes his declaration of love he has his back to her. It takes time to detach him from the officers' mess; a point the British-Raj setting (Shakespeare obviously thought of everything) makes brilliantly clear.

It helps too for the rustics; their jokes get far more careful treatment in Indian accents than they usually receive in Mummerset. John Woodvine's Dogberry, exquisitely and exhaustively punctilious, walks as delicately as Agag, speaks in a voice as monstrous little as Bottom's. The middling males are dull, but the girls are excellent; Cherie Lunghi is the only Hero I have seen with a sense of humour.

*

Tom Stoppard's Every Good Boy Deserves Favour (an elaborate one night stand at the Royal Festival Hall) concerns two men with the same name sharing a cell in a Russian mental hospital through some unexplained whim of the colonel-in-charge; perhaps he just has a tidy mind. Since this is a Stoppard play, everything rhymes and the colonel's bright idea is his undoing: a moment of gleeful satisfaction -- social and aesthetic -- for the audience.

The cell-mates are a political dissident, played by Ian McKellen, and a genuine madman, fooled by John Wood to the top of his bent. His mania is music; he believes himself the agonised possessor of a large orchestra, represented on stage by Andre Previn and the LSO.

This terrifying companion is only a part of Mr McKellen's ordeal; in depicting the rest of it, Mr Stoppard certainly extends his range, but at the price of including some scenes that anyone else might have written. Others are uniquely him; puns underpin not only his dialogue, but his plot. But the live presence of the orchestra, though it may have sparked the play off, seems incidental; the piece's future lies, I imagine, in humbler circumstances, with recorded music off.
