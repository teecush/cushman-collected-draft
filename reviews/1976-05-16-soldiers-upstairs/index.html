May 16, 1976

Soldiers Upstairs

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Soldiers Upstairs

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

THE Royal Court's Theatre Upstairs is in business again, and the reopening production is worth the climb. Yesterday's News deals with the contribution of British mercenaries to the civil war in Angola. It is a show for talking heads and, in one or two cases, for some revealingly fidgety hands. Seven actors sit in a row, facing the audience, and deliver monologues which they themselves have culled (with the assistance of Jeremy Seabrook) from documentary material and interviews. Four of them represent actual mercenaries, of varying degrees of professionalism; the other three have more distant interests in Angola.

Only five of the cast are on stage to begin with. The two toughest soldiers arrive late, their looks uncompromising enough to suggest momentarily that this is a raid. They take their places at the end of the line and the central position accordingly shifts. Before it was occupied by a voluble lady journalist; now it belongs to Keith Jones, a 17-year-old recruit at first believed dead in Colonel Callan's massacre but later discovered to have survived.

He has, it seems, less to say than anyone; he sits hunched forward, and his ears, protruding before his cropped hair, seem to be propelling him further to the front. If he is the man in the middle, the others have all to some extent shaped his destiny: at one end his more hardened colleagues from the SAS and the paras, at the other, Roche, the man who recruited him. Nearer to him sit more domestic influences: a girlfriend, the reporter who got him into the papers having commandeered the rights to his story by moving in on his parents, and a stockbroker concerned with British investments in Africa.

These last two confuse the piece a bit. The girl has one bitter outburst about Keith's misguided ideas of playing at soldiers, but she seems too slight and too particular. The stockbroker, by contrast, seems too generalised; there is more to be said about economic interests in Angola than the play has time for, and his streaks of individuality--he was once an actor and may be one again--do not compensate. He never quite makes it as either a person or a type.

It is none the less an informative, scary and riveting evening: not about Angola but about Britain. The tone of the production (by William Gaskill and Max Stafford-Clark, for Joint Stock) is unremittingly level. Will Knightley's bewilderment as Keith becomes recognisable as native violence: a state which the real soldiers (Paul Kember and David Rintoul) have by now stonily transcended, their frustrations thickened into a way of life. Less reachable is Philip McGough's Roche, whose departure, in scowling fury, is as disconcerting as the arrival of the other two. Perhaps because these three characters are the least familiar, their performances are the most persuasively real. The others are actors' distillations, not larger but more insistently revealing than life; Gillian Barge, who excels in playing harried professional women, has her best and best-taken opportunity since The National Health as the journalist, alternately brandishing and tripping over her own nervousness.

*

This has been a good week for rooms above. The Bush (over a pub) offers Franz Xaver Kroetz's Geisterbahn, a sequel to his Stallerhof. Spastic Beppi, whom we saw impregnated by a farm-hand in the first play, has now had her baby, and the birth seems to have mellowed everyone considerably. This may partly be due to changes of cast: only Penelope Lee as Beppi's mother remains from before, even more creased and more desperately tired but with more sorrow than anger in her eyes. Her husband (Mike Pratt) looks positively benign, while Sepp (Peter Sproule), the discharged farm-worker, previously played as a man in whom gentleness occasionally surfaced, has been transformed by fatherhood, and dies a kindly martyr's death (to cancer and Social Security).

Beppi herself has more than doubled her output of words; she can not only talk to her baby but can deliver a poem over Sepp's body. This moment, and a couple of others, are unwontedly sentimental for Kroetz; he may be trying to get more out of these characters than they will render him, and some strain shows too in David Samourai's production, which is occasionally mannered in its stillness. Pictorially, though, it improves even on its predecessor: a series of bleak interiors takes shape, the actors harshly posed against walls that may once have been white. Harshly but not pitilessly: even when Kroetz appears to be wallowing in desolation, past the making of his points, there is some surprising human action at the end of it. He does not deal in automata.

Beppi's slow gyrations, as she fights against putting her child in a home, are touchingly executed by Jenny Twigge; hers is less of a virtuoso display than her predecessor's (if only because Beppi is now less of a case), but her decision to give the baby up--and her concern lest it should suffer for her negligence--is acted, as it is written, with stunning humanity. It is not the end of the play; what is, it would be worth your while to discover.

*

In the fifties, when they spoke of American playwrights, it was Tennessee Williams, Arthur Miller and William Inge. Inge's plays never reached London, except as films, and he is dead now; but his Bus Stop has finally arrived at the Phoenix as (appropriately) a vehicle ridden by Lee Remick and Keir Dullea: she as a Kansas City bar-room singer, he as the cowboy who fell for her at first glance and is abducting her to Montana on that basis (not to mention on that bus) but is temporarily halted, first by snow, and then by the suggestion that she may not actually like him.

They both do well, but the conventions of the play demand that we should will them to come together at the end, and I could not oblige. The film craftily began with the cowboy, and so got us on his side. The play starts with Cherie, stating her case, plaintively but firmly, against her boorish pursuer, and nothing that happened later persuaded me she was wrong. Other couples dispose themselves around Saul Radomsky's roadside diner (which looks as if it ought to be authentic) and Vivian Matalon's production takes good care of the counterpoint. The play really centres on a young waitress, watching it all as part of growing up; Jenny Quayle, who was promising but wrong in The Gingerbread Lady, is here fulfilling and right. The play is not badly contrived; but it is contrived.

*

At the Palladium last week Joel Grey did his MC numbers from Cabaret; their chill, their zest and their authority were undiminished. What was diminished was the rest of his act, which was compact of just the kind of showbiz bonhomie that Cabaret undercuts. Mr Grey does not need this kind of charm (just as well, since he hasn't actually got it); he has colossal energy, and is the fleetest performer I have ever seen, electric even when changing his shoes: one pair with taps and one without. He was supported by Johnny Hackett, a better than average Northern comic, and the Brotherhood of Man, a shiny gang of Eurovision song contestants for whom "slickening" would be the right portmanteau word.
