Jan 19, 1975

Tragedy Of The Hungry Tribe

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Tragedy of the hungry tribe

I WOULD like to proclaim Peter Brook's Théâtre des Bouffes-du-Nord as the most exciting playhouse in Britain. The fact that it is actually in Paris hinders me somewhat; but I cannot repress my delight that the greatest of living British directors should now, for the first time in his career, have his own theatre. His troupe may contain only one British actor (the origins of the others stretching from Africa to Japan) and it may perform in French; but chauvinism will out.

In this scooped-out shell of a theatre, formerly disused for 20 years, contact between actors and audience is unusually (though not, of course, unprecedentedly) close; Mr Brook exploited it in Timon of Athens, a play about hospitality and its collapse. Timon has now been followed by Les Iks, a more extreme treatment of the same theme.

Timon's disillusion caused him to pray for the destruction of every social bond, and this is the situation depicted in Les Iks. The title refers to a Ugandan mountain tribe, whom a governmental decree forced into an almost overnight change of life-style. Originally hunters, they were compelled to become farmers, a pursuit suited neither to their temperament nor their terrain. Drought compounded their plight; and they began to starve.

In the 1960s an English-born, American-based anthropologist, Colin Turnbull, spent two years among the Ik, subsequently describing his experiences in a book, The Mountain People. He tells of a world in which survival has become the only value and food the only good; a good man is in fact defined, not as one who gives food, but as one who has eaten it. The family has hardly any meaning; the young and the old are victims of the few who have somehow managed to attain a short-lived prime. Mr Turnbull devotes a final chapter to prophecy; his thesis is not merely that in similar circumstances we could become like the Ik (which few would dare dispute), but that in many ways we already have. Here he strains a bit; sending one's children to a day-school is not invariably the same as banishing them permanently from the household at the age of three, so that their only hope of survival lies in combining into predatory packs.

The tone of the book is surprisingly jocund; Mr Turnbull writes with cool detachment, not merely because this may be held to be scientifically appropriate, but because he realises that nothing is more shocking than unshockability--the characteristic of the Ik and eventually, he implies, of anyone who shares their life. He does not mock the Ik; indeed, he scrupulously records the occasions on which they mocked him, misleading outsiders being one of their few remaining pleasures. The anthropologist as fall-guy haunts the book; and comes to solid life in the play. The adaptation (by Colin Higgins and Denis Cannan) is remarkably faithful; but the fact of dramatisation inevitably alters the emphases. When we see Turnbull enacted, conscientiously capturing suffering for posterity with camera and cassette-recorder, he becomes ludicrously irrelevant in a way that may be hinted at, but is never embodied, in his own lucid prose.

I have mentioned suffering, a word sparingly employed by Mr Turnbull, but inescapably by Mr Brook and his actors. They have sacrificed the book's didacticism, reproduced its humour, and intensified its pain. A couple of paragraphs about the visit of two missionaries become a great tragi-comic set-piece; cracked voices desperately sing out "Rock of Ages," having been promised food in return for volume. The production never begs for pity; a woman announces that she has not eaten for three days and laughs; but not nobly--contemptuously. The Ik attitude is catching; when some of the lucky ones vomit up the food they have just swallowed, many of the audience laugh. I could not join in but I suspect that they were right. Probably the most powerful sequence shows a woman picking up a piece of orange peel, whimpering for a second, and beginning to eat; a child snatches the peel from her and is joined by two other children; the three of them conclude by jumping on the woman.

The cosmopolitan cast make no attempt to impersonate Africans; with superb cohesion they show us instead irreducible, nerveless, shuffling humanity. (The contrast to the gross and fumbling portrayal of Vietnamese in Mr Brook's US could hardly be more marked.) The barren theatre is the perfect environment: rungs fixed into the exposed back wall enable two of the actors to undertake a breathtaking mountain ascent; there is a recurring image of the Ik sitting with their backs to us, their legs dipping into the void that was once a stage. Brook wants to restage this production in London, in English; finding him the right venue may not be easy, but there is no more urgent quest confronting our theatre.

*

Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz And Guildenstern Are Dead has returned to the Young Vic, from which indeed it has seldom been absent in two years. Mr Stoppard has written more engaging plays, but none so satisfyingly shaped; it dovetails and resolves every one of its preoccupations, philosophic, dramaturgic, or merely exuberantly verbal. (Mr Stoppard is an incurable semantic.) On the tongues of Christopher Timothy and Richard O'Callaghan not all of the wit emerges as trippingly as Lord Hamlet (or I) would desire; but their awareness of the characters' plight is admirable. As a bonus Graham Crowden has slipped across the road from the National to re-create his definitive First Player, resonant with remembered bravado, electric with contempt.
