Aug 4, 1974

Turgenev's Duets

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Turgenev's duets

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

TURGENEV'S A Month in the Country begins with a tableau: on one side of the stage three old people playing cards; on the other two young ones, a man reading and a woman embroidering.

In fact, the age distinction is not that clear-cut; two of the card players might fairly claim to be still only middle-aged, while their opposite numbers--he 30 and she 29--are already resigned to having lost their youth. This is a world where--psychologically, at any rate--age comes early and youth suffers. As the play proceeds we are introduced to two characters who are unequivocally young, and it becomes plain that this country-house society has no place for them. They are cast out.

Since the Chichester Festival Theatre has an open stage, the director, Toby Robertson, is unable to have his opening group discovered; instead he brings them on, formally, to music, so striking a satiric note which dominates the entire production. For the play revolves around Natalya Petrovna, the self-consciously languid lady of the house. She is more than something of a poseuse; so much the dialogue makes clear, but clarity becomes transparency when we see Dorothy Tutin, radiantly bored, walk on stage and scrupulously arrange herself in a becoming position.

A glass of water is proffered: she graciously sips it. The profferer is Rakitin, for four years her admirer, but no more than that: as he puts it, "I'm no Don Juan, I'm sorry to say." Miss Tutin knows exactly how to administer the degree of mild torment that will keep him in leading-strings. When she mentions that she has engaged a new tutor, and that he is young, she makes it sound like a comment on their own premature decay. She can render affection infinitely condescending. Taking Rakitin's hand, and addressing him as "My old friend," she reassures him that "it's only the good things we can have too much of."

In fact Natalya ends up losing most of the good things. Rakitin, not altogether of his own volition, finally forsakes her; she persuades herself that she is in love--at last--with the young tutor and he too has to leave; though not before Natalya has jealously and calculatingly manoeuvred her ward--aged 17 and a potential rival--into marriage with a fat, stupid landowner of 48.

This is a play largely composed of duologues, and Miss Tutin, presenting a narcissist desperate for contact but incapable of it, excels in them. She advances, feints, withdraws with consummate skill. And yet, somewhere, somehow, monotony creeps in. The fault, I think, is Turgenev's. He eventually gave up the theatre for the novel, convinced that he could not construct a play. In some respects he was too hard on himself; the play tells its story subtly, the scenes have their own momentum. But three-quarters of the way through the evening the abundance of two-handed scenes creates a log-jam. The characters--and the cast is large--queue up for their moments; some simply vanish. (Compare Chekhov, in some respects Turgenev's pupil, who knew how to keep his minor figures active and relevant.) These are hardly insufferable faults, but they glare quite ferociously on a frameless stage. Events at Chichester simply happen; a director can hardly hope to impose a shape.

He can of course impose a tone. There are occasions in this play when soliloquy sets in; these are heralded at Chichester by a sharpening of the lights and the distant sound of a horn, a near relation of the clarion employed at this theatre to summon the audience back from the bars. The monologue then proceeds, backed by mood music, which is likely--given the cynical mind of your contemporary playgoer--less to reinforce the speech than to undercut it. We are in fact invited to smile at the characters' self-dramatisation. This fits Miss Tutin's characterisation perfectly, but it seems ludicrously inappropriate when applied to John Turner as her honest unimaginative husband and a grave handicap to Derek Jacobi as Rakitin. Being short Mr Jacobi is apt to forfeit attention when not actually doing anything, and in his early scenes Rakitin does virtually nothing. One wonders why Natalya keeps him on, even as a butt.

Later, when suffering drives him to unclench his teeth and loose his accumulated bitterness, Mr Jacobi is very good. Natalya--in that charming way she has of delivering a compliment as if it were a rebuke--calls him "Mr Observer," but that title truly belongs (and the critic of this journal should surely be allowed to bestow it) to Timothy West's doctor, despising the household on which he battens and grimly aware that the feeling is returned. Narrowing his eyes he surveys the pack of them while ostensibly gazing into the distance; asked if he is staying to dinner he replies "If I may" with the respectful confidence of the accustomed freeloader. He is also extremely funny.

*

The Two Noble Kinsmen (Regent's Park), believed to be the joint work of Shakespeare and Fletcher, treats, like A Midsummer Night's Dream, of the delayed nuptials of Theseus and Hippolyta. While they hang about waiting for wedlock, two gallants, Palamon and Arcite, fall for the same girl and descend from courtly sentiment to mere squabbling. At length, after many vicissitudes--notably the death of Arcite--Palamon's pre-eminence ("I saw her first") is established and rewarded.

This, considering the omens, is in the nature of a surprise ending: on the eve of their last combat Arcite, praying to Mars, had been rewarded by a most impressive thunderclap; Palamon, taking Venus as his patroness, had been granted only a swarm of balloons. (The text actually calls for doves, but the director presumably felt the need of a laugh at this point; that at any rate is what he got.) The play calls out for a high fantastic style, as of the Royal Shakespeare Company at their most ceremonious; the approach in the Park oscillates between the raucous and the artlessly conversational. There is a bouncy jailer's daughter who, as has been the way of jailers' daughters down the ages, arranges the escape of her daddy's handsomest prisoner; she skips around a good deal, is condemned to deliver her best speech from impossibly far upstage and eventually goes mad, but were she to quieten down a bit she might be nice to know.
