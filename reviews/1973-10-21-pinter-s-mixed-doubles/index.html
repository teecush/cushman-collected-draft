Oct 21, 1973

Pinter's Mixed Doubles

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Pinter's mixed doubles

THEATRE

Robert Cushman on a week of duologues.

NEXT week the Royal Shakespeare Company is finally permitting the critics to get their hands on its productions of Shakespeare's four Roman plays. But we must starve before we are stuffed, and the RSC has this week offered three duologues, one of them fleshed out by a mute third presence.

I refer to the mysterious Matchseller of A Slight Ache, which now forms the first half of a double bill of Harold Pinter plays at the Aldwych. Originally written for radio, it was first staged in 1961 along with one-acters by John Mortimer and N. F. Simpson. (That was our idea of an avant-garde evening back in 1961.) It has survived rather better than I would have expected.

There is a school of thought, examined and partially endorsed by Simon Trussler in his recent excellent study*, that considers Mr Pinter's plays mainly as so many actors' exercises, composed of arbitrary tensions that spring to life when they are well performed, and sink back into meaninglessness thereafter. It is interesting to approach the Aldwych production from this point of view, and to note that if actors bring out the best in Pinter, he can do the same for them.

I referred last Sunday to David Waller as a steamrollering actor; he has always seemed best when most monstrously complacent--as, for example, a practically definitive Dogberry. The protagonist of A Slight Ache is also unhealthily prone to self-satisfaction, but--being a man of letters--he vents it in more refined, arcane terms than we usually expect of Mr Waller. He rises to the occasion magnificently, however. His jowls are as heavy as ever, but a trim grey moustache prickles above them; in his dealings with his wife, with the wasp that strays with fatal unwariness into his breakfast marmalade, and even, before panic sets in, with the intrusive decrepit matchseller (to whom he confides 'I was in commerce too') he contrives to be skittish, even dainty.

In the confrontation of Mr Waller (who finishes up a gibbering idiot) and his silent, victorious opposite (whom the author himself is alleged to have played in a radio production) is it fanciful--well, yes it is, but what the hell--to see Civilisation engulfed by its Discontents? Among the trophies which Mr Waller's Edward must yield to the invader is his wife Flora (a line about his biological interests suggests that he has somewhere a mistress called Fauna), who is beautifully played by Peggy Ashcroft. Flora is the prototype of those faithless females who have lolled or sauntered their way, usually in the person of Vivien Merchant, through Mr Pinter's later plays. Dame Peggy defies convention by giving her a housewifely practicality which melts into erotic surrender without a hint of tease.

Peter James's direction, refreshingly light-handed, ensures that we follow this study in capitulation with fascinated attention but, as far as I am concerned, without much belief. Why should Flora give herself, and why should Edward give himself away, the one so freely, the other so crassly? What's in a matchseller? Unless we can credit their reactions to him as a human being, he can only be an author's convenience, the laziest of symbols.

Landscape, which completes the evening, is less immediately compelling than A Slight Ache, but lingers more acceptably in the mind. Here are the same actors as another married couple with no need of a matchseller to prise them apart. Time has done the job. He rambles and reminisces; she merely remembers. He tries fumblingly to re-establish contact, but she is beyond him. It is the play's singular merit (which I failed to recognise at its first appearance four years ago) that it makes no attempt to generalise from this bleak vignette; here are merely two people, fastidiously particularised. Its weakness is the roseate sentimentality that clings to the character of the wife, even in the lovely simplicity of Dame Peggy's performance. We are on the side of her memories, and resent the clumsy intrusions of the man whose last speech, in Mr Waller's powerful delivery, amounts to verbal rape. She, however, is able to turn the assault aside, to ignore or to transcend it--choose your interpretation--by immersing herself in the seductive, see-saw rhythms of nostalgia.

Nostalgia plays a large, bitter part in the RSC's other duologue, Athol Fugard's Hello and Goodbye at The Place; here the son and daughter of an old Afrikaner railwayman chew up their past and spit it out at each other. We are confronted with inbred situations that need some exceptional dramatic spark to ignite them. In this early play, Mr Fugard gives us pages of earnest effortful writing, which establish beyond doubt that his characters are wretched, but fail to provide any motive for the audience--a notoriously unfeeling animal--to care. So Johnnie Smit is going out of his skull; so Hester has come in off the streets and will probably return there. So what?

That question can be briefly, affirmatively answered when Hester, ransacking the place for her father's money, comes upon a dress belonging to her dead mother and joyfully exclaims that it still carries her smell; or in some moments of graveyard wit, unsurprisingly akin to Jewish ghetto humour. Much of this seeps through Ben Kingsley's hopelessly defeated Johnnie; he seems at home in the Port Elizabeth shanty, stumbling around in his pyjamas. But Janet Suzman, though her tirades give the play a welcome lift, looks and sounds too smart to be credible.

If Frank Hauser's Meadow Players have to end their 17-year tenure of the Oxford Playhouse one would have liked them to go out in triumph with their production of The Wolf; their current version of Doctor Knock is a miserable pendant. There is no reason to assume that a satire on French medicine in the early 1920s is fatally dated; in the age of Need Your Doctor Be So Useless it is plainly still very topical. In conscience, though, Jules Romains, having conceived the masterful joke of a doctor who propagandised the healthy (and more particularly the wealthy) villagers of his district into sickbeds, took a long time to set it up. The greater the necessity, therefore, for the comic chances to be firmly seized when they occur. But Minos Volanakis's direction has no grip on them; gags sputter up out of nowhere, misfire, and are gone. Nor is the occasion redeemed by a dazzling central performance; Alfred Burke is a competent sharper where the text demands a great manic rogue, the intoxicated victim of his own deception. Only in the last act, when Knock has visions of hundreds of thermometers simultaneously taking rectal temperatures, does the mercury show any sign of rising on stage. James Cairncross gives a good anonymous performance as the town pharmacist, and there is a nice moment when an hotel chambermaid is metamorphosed into a nurse at the drop of a mob cap.

All in all, things were better in Salisbury, where the Playhouse offered, as its contribution to a mysterious event now deceased called the Festival of British Theatre, a competent reduction of What Maisie Knew. Denis Constanduros could hardly dramatise the novel's perceptions, but he hinted at their existence. A certain amount of vulgarity might have perplexed Henry James; when he spoke of the dramatic method as throwing the cargo overboard to save the ship I doubt if running gags about sea-sickness were quite what he had in mind.

* The Plays of Harold Pinter (Gollancz, £2.50).
