Aug 3, 1980

The Showman And The Ideologue

Robert Cushman · The Observer

The showman and the ideologue

ROBERT CUSHMAN assesses the achievement of his outstanding predecessor as theatre critic of The Observer

IT IS difficult to write theatre criticism in English without feeling, and maybe fearing, that Kenneth Tynan is hovering somewhere around the typewriter. If you write in THE OBSERVER it is probably impossible.

A great deal of theatre criticism in the Sixties and Seventies consists, more or less consciously, of imitations of Tynan. Three factors, beyond his actual writing, contributed. One, most practitioners had trodden the grammar-school and university trail that Tynan had so spectacularly blazed just after the war. Two, he himself had given the job up and retired to the National Theatre and points beyond, so sparing his juniors the harrowing trial of direct competition. Three, beginning in 1961, his criticism became readily available in stout but elegant anthologies: what a friend once referred to as `Ken Tynan's Bumper Review Books.'

It was all there to be consulted and to be rifled: Tynan jokes and Tynan theories have now been steadily--and often thoughtlessly--recycled for 20 years. It is quite possible that the jokes are more important than the theories. Occasionally they were decorative, generally they were structural, and precisely evocative. (Of Michael Redgrave's Shylock: `Mr Redgrave cringes and crumples every time Antonio opens his mouth--you would think he had never seen a Christian before.')

Once, considering an American polemic against criticism in general, he justified his own tribe with the words `what counts is not the opinion, but the art with which it is expressed.' This struck me when I first read it in 1956 (an earnest schoolboy, I was beginning to follow the master into the rigorous climate of committed criticism for a committed theatre) as evasive; and he contradicted it in later utterances (`If a man tells me something I believe to be an untruth, am I forbidden to do more than congratulate him on the brilliance of his lying?'). But he was right the first time; it is the art of his expression that made him a great critic.

This does not mean that he was a stylist in vacuo. The test of a critic is his ability to make you believe, while you are reading him, that his is the only possible view of the subject. (You are free to start disagreeing the moment you finish the last sentence.) Tynan achieved this more often than any theatre writer, and he managed it, of course, by virtue of the intensity of his reaction. You rarely feel that he willed an epigram into life: rather, that it was the inevitable reflection of the precision with which his mind had registered the performance he was describing.

I went through a period of thinking that his phrases were superb and his opinions absurd. Wrong: his judgments of particular plays and performances are, more often than not, unassailable. He was no great theorist; merely the great chronicler and evaluator of the passing moment. But the theatre is a collection of passing moments, and Tynan usually got them resoundingly right.

He was not, temperamentally or philosophically, much in sympathy with Beckett. But nobody in all the 25 years since the London premiere has got closer to the heart of `Waiting for Godot': `His two tramps pass the time of day just as we, the audience, are passing the time of night. Were we not in the theatre, we should like them be clowning and quarrelling . . . all, as one of them says, "to give us the impression that we exist."'

That review was written before he had fully discovered Brecht and before `Look Back in Anger' gave him the opportunity to become the journalistic spokesman for a new theatre movement. I do not believe that he, in any sense, created that movement (that would be beyond the power of any critic), or even that he procreated its audience, but he probably speeded up the process of recognition and codification. The latter, I think, he may have over-encouraged. Indiscriminate generalisation was his least endearing critical trait. His espousal of socialism came--as far as one can tell from his writing--comparatively late, and he displayed the proverbial zeal of the late convert.

Having decided on the kind of theatre he wanted, he sometimes distorted (with old plays) or rejected (with new ones) works that didn't fit into his scheme. Tynan, though, often left himself generous loopholes. What really attracted him to Brecht was the spectacular stagecraft and the breadth of the dramatic canvases; as much the director's craft as the writer's. He claimed that he began by believing in `drama about great men' and changed to believing in plays about society. In fact, what he always believed in, whatever his disclaimers, was showmanship. Or, to put it more bluntly, show-biz.

He was brought up, he once wrote, on the Birmingham Hippodrome, and the comedians he saw there may have been his greatest influences. He possessed the extremely rare gift of writing funnily--and illuminatingly--about great clowns, and he developed the gift in writing about great actors. Those he most adored--pre-eminently Olivier, of whom he wrote incomparably--had always something of the stand-up comic in them. (I think that by comparison he underrated Gielgud. I think he thought so too.)

When he moved on to become Olivier's Dramaturg at the National Theatre, it was the showman rather than the ideologue in him that was most influential. Puritans and academics chafed, the rest of us rejoiced in a subsidised company whose repertoire reflected, of all things, style and humour. Gestures towards social conscience were comparatively infrequent and not too successful. He described himself as a congenital enthusiast, and a look at the files reveals that he was less of a butcher than he was reputed to be.

I have cause to be grateful to him for personal help and encouragement, and most of all for the inspiration of his work. He did the job--word by word, sentence by sentence, week by week--supremely and intimidatingly well.
