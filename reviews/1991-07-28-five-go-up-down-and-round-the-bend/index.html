Jul 28, 1991

Five Go Up, Down And Round The Bend

Robert Cushman · The Independent

There is real grass on the stage of the Bush, and a real play on top of it. Catherine Johnson's Dead Sheep is about five variously troubled people who set out to climb a Brecon Beacon, represented on Geoff Rose's set by a large boulder that rises and falls with majestic ease according to whether the characters are huddled at its foot or perched on its peak. They do have their ups and downs.

This is hardly surprising. Three of them are ex-addicts. Middle-aged Eve was an alcoholic whose children were taken into care. Zoe and Holly were both junkies. All three are inmates of a rehab centre presided over by Philip, a nannyish religious fanatic who, like many evangelists, identifies God's will with his own. He has brought his flock on this adventure weekend as a treat. Jon, their hired guide, is ex-SAS. A self-made macho man, he could well be more disturbed than any of his charges.

All have their decent moments, but there is too much envy, frustration, resentment simmering inside each of them for the party to go by without disaster. The petty strains of five people in two tents are nearly enough to get the outing cancelled. The play chronicles them in hilarious detail and Mike Bradwell's production revels in it. Bradwell, who founded the Hull Truck company, has over the years perfected a farcical style that goes effortlessly into higher gears, and this play is ideal for him, becoming progressively bleak without ever losing its salty comic texture.

One subject he has always been good on is religion: both its pain and its absurdity. Philip, always ready with a prayer or with a fulsome word of rebuke or encouragement, is seen by the other characters as an embarrassment. Partly this is because he is a dwarf; both his enthusiasms and his malevolence seem out of scale. He embarrasses the audience too, but that doesn't mean that the author dismisses him. By the end he has lost one of his flock (see title) and is ranting uncontrollably, addressing his principal antagonist as Satan, desperately denying facts that he cannot face. But he has at least - strikingly so in Jon Key's performance - the dignity of his own fury.

What does him in is the women. Ex-pop singer Zoe (Kate Hardie) is supposedly his success, having forsaken dope, chosen Jesus and decided to set up as a carer in her own right. Holly (Katrin Cartlidge) is at the other extreme, practically catatonic. In her few lucid moments she sounds like the most intelligent person around and Jon (Gary Love) brings her right out of herself, by what you might call the traditional route: direct but, as it turns out, extremely dangerous. The other girls are both curious and furious, and Gwen Taylor's Eve - the most strikingly fake of a quintet of fine performances - especially takes fire. One moment she is raucously asking Holly how it felt since in her own case it's been so long; the next she is an avenging angel. She exemplifies the most imposing thing about the evening; its seamless mastery of tone. I have not seen Catherine Johnson's previous plays but on this evidence she is a treasurable talent.

Johnson creates stage-size characters, something notably lacking in The Manchurian Candidate. This may be because it is based on a novel, though one best known as a film. Here too the personnel go through all kinds of hoops, and though some have the excuse of having been brainwashed, others do not.

Richard Condon's original fable concerned a US marine sergeant in the Korean War, turned by communist manipulation into a killing machine and used, in a sublime game of double-bluff, as a weapon to advance the cause of McCarthyism. You want to destroy America, was the caustic message, what difference if you do it from the left or from the right? John Lahr's new version advances the story to 1996; the rabble-rousing senator now takes for his theme not the number of commies in the administration but the proportion of America owned by the Japanese. Lahr may be prescient; this could be the theme of US stump politics in elections to come. But the new wrinkle does not provide the frisson of the Cold War original which lampooned a very recognisable demagogue.

Lahr is an adept adaptor, with some nifty lines and a competent way of unfolding a complicated story. The production only shudders to an embarrassing halt when the cast sing a xenophobic anthem obviously intended to chill our blood and discourage anyone who might be considering voting Republican. But the more techniques the show uses, the harder work it all seems; and the occasional use of video only reminds us of how much more we would like to see in close-up. These people need to be brought close for us to believe in them or to be gripped by the story they serve. Gerard Murphy, even from the middle distance, has the right sickly grin for the homicidal hero; Sian Phillips is the monster's suitably monstrous mother; and Manning Redwood is a boyish senator to the podium born. Clive Carter, as the officer who drags the truth to light, looks craggily and appropriately like a junior George C Scott; and Connie Booth lends him a sympathetic ear, which is all that is asked of her. A fella needs a girl if he's in a novel or movie, but in a play, with time at a premium, he should be able to do without.

Tovarich is a 1930s success by Jacques Deval disturbed from its long slumber to provide a vehicle for Natalia Makarova. When it wakes up, which takes time, it allows her to exude a ravishing hauteur while twirling about in a maid's uniform and to fill the theatre with romantic longings for a White Russian past. She and her Grand Duke husband (Robert Powell) are forced into domestic service with the bourgeoisie through poverty and loyalty, and some of their encounters with their dazzling Parisien employers are very funny. The evening then starts leaning on sentiment and charm, but the two stars supply both.

Alan Ayckbourn's Scarborough Theatre has moved into its restaurant to accommodate the American Tom Dulack's Breaking Legs, which is set in one: a New England Italian establishment whose proprietor (Jeffrey Chiswick, all olive oil and unction) ropes in two business associates (Glyn Grain, a master of slow-burning comic menace, and Robert Austin, a credible hulk) as backers for a play written by a nervous but increasingly confident academic (Peter Bourke, priceless) who happens to be loved by his daughter (Susan Denaker, making energetically like Angelica Houston). Ayckbourn wonderfully orchestrates a stagefull of flawless cartoon performances. The production outshines the play which is a collection of mild gangster and showbiz jokes, though one speech does begin "I can only speak for myself but I know I speak for all of us." Now that's eloquence.
