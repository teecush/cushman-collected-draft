Jul 20, 1975

Double Death

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Double death

THEATRE by ROBERT CUSHMAN

JOE ORTON'S last play, What the Butler Saw, was first staged in the West End in 1969, soon after its author's death. It flopped; and last week at the Royal Court it died its second London death. And yet I doubt if it will lie down. Of course every failed play has its bedraggled adherents; but in this case they--or rather we--have solid evidence to point to. In the years between its two metropolitan appearances audiences in reps all over the country have seen What the Butler Saw and have laughed even louder than he presumably did.

They were lucky; neither they nor the productions they enjoyed were intimidated by the play. Orton invented his own genre--epigrammatic, subversive farce--and this piece is its finest if not its firmest flower. The first production foundered in disordered extravagance; the new version is equally disordered, but far from extravagant. Like its two predecessors in the Court's Orton season, and worse than either of them, it tramples its author's wit into the ground.

Since Orton was never a Sloane Square playwright (he found his sponsors, to tell the awful truth, in the commercial theatre), there have been suggestions that in mounting the season at all the Court has betrayed its own traditions. This is to get the matter the wrong way round; as a theatre dedicated to the best new English writing the Court should have been presenting Orton in the sixties (very little of what it did present was of comparable interest) and it is decent of it to offer reparation now.

But though the will to recognise Orton as a serious, provocative writer may now be there, the spirit is still lacking. In the Court's formative days you knew when a writer was anti-Establishment because he told you so. Arnold Wesker preached, John Osborne grumbled, John Arden folkwove; they all tried hard. But Orton, homosexual and jailbird, never needed to raise his voice; cutting and insouciant, he effortlessly presented the complete outsider's view of society.

In What the Butler Saw it takes the form of a madhouse with a social conscience. "We've no privileged class here. It's democratic lunacy we practise." Those lines are the hook on which Orton hangs a gleeful indictment of Britain, and a device for embroiling the psychiatrists in the play's farcical action as inextricably as their patients. It is doubtful if Orton had any great interest in satirising psychiatry for its own sake; it served him as a metaphor for a system which operates by manipulating minds, and rarely doubts its own right to do so.

Dr Rance, the play's senior shrink, is as much civil servant as physician: "I represent Her Majesty's Government, your immediate superiors in madness" is the formula with which he introduces himself to his colleague Dr Prentice.

Prentice, a distinguished careerist, inaugurates the action by attempting to seduce his prospective secretary; surprised first by his wife, then by his superior, he spends the play rushing through doors, inventing plots, inveigling other characters into drag. He might, at any early stage, be willing to confess, but an account of the facts would disturb Dr Rance's own highly personal interpretation of them, and authority would not dream of allowing itself to be thus deflected.

Rance, by the way, is no stuffed shirt; faced by a situation that appears to involve fetishism, murder and incest, he dreams of turning it into a best-seller. He's cool ("someone should really write a book on these folk-myths," he remarks when presented with a standard view of heterosexuality) and desperately prurient. At one point Rance takes off into a denunciation of the avant-garde ("an instrument for inciting decent citizens to commit bizarre crimes against humanity and the State"); never afraid to underline the obvious, the director, Lindsay Anderson, has this speech delivered straight at the audience.

A running gag about a statue of Churchill with its private parts missing (the most blatant, dated thing in the play) is also given fearless prominence. Mr Anderson never stops reminding us that he knows what the play means, but he gives few signs of realising how it works. The pressure of farce is entirely absent; the pace is low and level, there is no energy in the exits and entrances. The sight-gags are clumsily handled, while most of the verbal humour is annihilated.

The play, like The Importance of Being Earnest, has too many funny lines; and a production must decide which it will capitalise on, which it will throw away. Here they are all left to take their chance; and since misreadings are rife that chance is a thin one. (On the first night a nervous cast displayed a tendency--unhelpful in a script crammed with mistaken identity--to get the names wrong.) Prentice is a role demanding great physical and verbal dexterity; after 10 years away from acting Michael Medwin was understandably floored. Betty Marsden as his wife came nearest to style and nearest to reality. The production as a whole, joyless and therefore toothless, is an impressive testimony to the continuing ravages of Royal Court blight. Other theatres suffer from other diseases.

*

At Chichester the audience's delight in simple trick effects must tempt any director to smother a play in whimsy. That is the course Wendy Toye has taken with Andrew Sachs's Made in Heaven, a play richly deserving to be smothered; sweets to the sweet, but why pour honey on sugar? Mr Sachs's comedy, a dissection of a marriage that had no dramatic existence to begin with, cannot even smirk with conviction. Chichester-kind cannot bear very much reality, though Patricia Routledge throws some in against the odds.

Attendance at Made in Heaven might prime you, though, for a visit to the Hampstead Theatre Club, now equipped with excellent air-conditioning, but still very hot on comic exposure of the bourgeoisie. Chichester, of course, goes in for comic reassurance. Hampstead's special subjects are those young bourgeois who kid themselves that they have escaped into Bohemia; Wolfgang Bauer's Ghost is interesting testimony that Hampstead plays are now being written in Austria. It offers amusing, cross-talking monologues, a mad monk who squeaks like a Monty Python housewife, a grand slam ending in which the characters degenerate to type and torment the maid; and good acting by a cast of the usual Hampstead weight. More than promising, less than stellar.

Trevor Nunn has prepared a sparkling translation of Hedda Gabler (a play by Ibsen, unlike Ghosts) and his production at the Aldwych is clear if, after its protracted world travels, a bit jaded. Particularly good is the evocation of the play's masculine world; Timothy West's solid, mischievous Judge Brack holds the ring for Patrick Stewart's fiery Lovborg and Peter Eyre's excellent Tesman. Mr Eyre, tall, young, and stooping, has the exact measure of Tesman's scholarly passion, of his myopia, and of his noble awareness of his own limitations; momentarily jealous, he finds lasting satisfaction in acknowledging his rival's genius. Glenda Jackson's Hedda is masculine too; if ladies rode to hounds in Norway her problems would be solved. She copes silently and well with the burning of Lovborg's manuscript and with suicide; but otherwise she is monotonous, conveying no belief in herself as a thwarted tragic heroine. This charmless Hedda is an unlikely lure for three men and a sad companion for the audience.
