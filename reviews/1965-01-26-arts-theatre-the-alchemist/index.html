Jan 26, 1965

Arts Theatre: The Alchemist

Robert Cushman · Broadsheet

Publication and date follow the handwritten annotation on the preserved clipping.

Arts Theatre: The Alchemist

I am frankly at a loss for a critical approach to this competent, agreeable, workmanlike, desperately average production. In no mood, and with no right, to submit a coherently-reasoned critique, I offer instead a series of notes.

1) The Play. I was seeing it, it dawned on me as the intrigues piled relentlessly up, for the fourth time. I still laughed, so I suppose it must be good. And yet, the great comic masterpiece of received opinion? Almost invariably I have lost the will to cope with the increasingly convulsive plot. It becomes less a play than a music-hall bill; Subtle and Face in the chair presiding over a succession of gulled guests. The acts vary considerably in quality and has to stand on its own, so there are bound to be longueurs. It is a plot-necessity that the various dupes should never meet; hence, we do not see them played off against each other until the action is two-thirds over. When they do collide, all hell should break loose, but it doesn't quite. The sum of the entertainment-value is considerable, but it is not cumulative, as farce ideally should be.

2) Production. A very even pace—not a good idea. I heard more of Jonson than ever before—some throw-away lines caught the audience laughing off their guard, but the big obvious ones ("Thou look'st like Antichrist in that lewd hat") came through muffled, and there was very little climactic sense. Ananias' entry at the height of confusion with "Peace to this house" should raise the roof. I smiled. Surely the long reels of alchemical jargon were meant to be gabbled. The set was pleasant, atmospheric, cluttered and reduced blocking to a series of brisk walks round a small room down right centre.

3) Acting. Subtle (Alan MacNaughtan) rather heavy-handed. Belief in him as a con-man hindered by the fact that he used his real voice for his assumed persona, and vice versa; strain in the wrong places the natural result. In general, too mellow, not sharp enough. All this goes too for Face (John Turner) whose best moments are at the end when he reverts to his butler-identity, (for which he has another funny voice—sustained over all that time?) Both are reasonably genial company; both lack the force of personality to carry the audience with them. Here they are in marked contrast to Dol (Judi Dench) who aroused all the sympathetic enthusiasm normally generated by transparently nice young actresses trying roguishly to pretend they're not. Before I am wrathfully struck down I will admit that Miss Dench is still the most adorable actress in the world and that I duly adored her. Furthermore that when her overrated, under-written part gave her an opportunity (in her Queen of Fairy scene) to display the talents of a real comedienne, as opposed to a girl it's fun to have around, she took it. So much for the tricksters. The tricked:—Mammon (David Swift) a casualty. What is initially a pleasingly un-emphatic approach to the big speeches proves not to conceal a supply of power-in-reserve. The glorious character fades into gruff oblivion. Surly (Zia Mohyeddin) my big disappointment—not perhaps the ideal vehicle for his mercurial talents, but still what has happened to him? Drugger (Simon Ward) my big surprise in the other direction; a gauche, stolid, foursquare, thickly-spoken, slow-witted joy. My favourite character (in terms of laughter, not sympathy) came out tops again; Ananias (Julian Curry) has brought blinkered fanaticism to unwaveringly funny heights.

4) Why the hell doesn't someone do VOLPONE instead?

Robert Cushman
