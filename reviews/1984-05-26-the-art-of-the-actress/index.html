May 26, 1984

The Art of the Actress

Robert Cushman · Radio Times

The art of the actress

Journey into the Shadows, Sunday 9.10 BBC2

Anna Massey has recently discovered a new talent - that of painter. It's part of her preparation for her dramatic portrayal of the artist Gwen John, as she explains to Robert Cushman.

A year or so ago there appeared in The Sunday Times colour magazine a portrait of the painter Gwen John. Her face had reminded somebody of another face: superimposed on it was a photograph of actress Anna Massey.

If ever a hint were dropped to a casting department, that was it. When television director Anna Benson Gyles suggested a dramatised documentary on Gwen John, Anna Massey was the actress she wanted for the lead.

Anna Massey's favourite word for acting is "journey", and Elaine Morgan's script appealed to her as a more extensive trip than most. It follows Gwen's life from 1903 when, in her late 20s, she was part of the London menage of her more famous brother Augustus John, to 1926, the year of her first exhibition in London, with a glance forward to her lonely death in 1939 in Dieppe.

She spent most of her adult life in France, much of it in Paris, living in small, poor rooms. Paris was also the scene of the one great love affair of her life. She was the mistress - one of many - of the sculptor Auguste Rodin. Their affair lasted on and off for ten years, but after that she went on writing him letters. At the height of their relationship she was writing to him twice a day, and it was to these letters - nearly 2,000 of them, all preserved in the Rodin Museum - that Anna Massey turned to research her role.

"People are like shadows to me," wrote Gwen John, "and I am like a shadow." The letters demonstrate an extraordinary mixture of passion and reserve. "She was," says Miss Massey, "extraordinarily shy to people she didn't know. But I was once told by a friend that shyness is actually a selfish, attention-getting thing; you walk into a room and you expect that people will notice you. Gwen was renownedly shy; she became a sort of recluse. And yet she was, in her own way, assertive. She walked in a speedily determined way. You can see from her photo that she has a wonderful, upright stance. She was indomitably there."

Anna Massey could take for granted a physical and facial resemblance. The next thing was to get her own painting right; or at least the appearance of painting. "I couldn't paint a pin-person for love or money, but I hate plays where actors are supposed to be painting and they're obviously not doing it. So I said I've got to learn to paint."

She turned for help to Mary Taubman, a painter herself as well as an expert on Gwen John and one of the programme's accredited advisers (the others are Ben and Sara John, Augustus's grandchildren, and Michael Holroyd, probably the finest living biographer, whose books include one on Augustus John). "Mary was incredible. Every time on the set I picked up a paintbrush or a palette or an easel I knew, technically, what I was doing." She also worked with a painter friend. "We forged some wonderful Gwen Johns. One of them she now has on her wall."

It was, she says, riveting research. Gwen John revealed a lot of herself in the letters. She wrote with a childlike innocence, and in a childish hand. Rodin, it seems, was sexually the only man in her life. "She had a lot of passionate relationships with women; I've never been sure whether they were consummated or not. I think probably not. There's an obsessive quality in the Rodin letters; they're very flowery, very free. She had been obsessed with another man before she met him, but I think she was probably a virgin. There's a virgin quality in her writing; at the same time she makes the Rodin relationship seem very luscious, very free."

All this is quite apart from her relationship with her brother, complicated by her friendships with both his wife Ida and his mistress Dorelia McNeill (with whom Gwen set out to walk from London to Rome). Augustus emerges, in Miss Massey's words, as "quite monstrous, certainly in his later years". But she can see resemblances between him and his sister. "They both drew with incredible speed; I could see in their work that they were from the same world." All the same, as they grew older their styles, especially in portraiture, diverged. "She was more agonised in her work; he suffered pain, too, but he escaped from it in a tempestuous way. Augustus said of her work: 'The little pictures to me are almost painfully charged with feeling'."

The mixture of shyness and spirit in Gwen John is something that should suit Anna Massey very well. At 46, she is one of the most respected and versatile actresses in the country, with a range extending from the luckless spinster of Angela Huth's TV play Virginia Fly is Drowning to the imperiously witty Ariadne Utterword of Shaw's Heartbreak House. What remains constant in her work is a firm backbone (mental and physical) and a fastidiousness enabling her to rescue the dowdiest characters from dullness.

She too has a successful brother, Daniel Massey, in the same profession, though their careers have grown pretty much side by side. But she has, like Gwen John, in some sense had to break away from her family in order to find herself. The daughter of two famous actors, Raymond Massey and Adrianne Allen, she grew up in the atmosphere of the West End, and began her career - without drama-school - in the title role of an enormously successful boulevard comedy, William Douglas Home's The Reluctant Debutante. It was 1955, and she was 17. She was actually offered a smaller role in the play, and turned it down. The management said, "Well, would you mind coming and just rehearsing the lead for a week," and at the end of that time she had the part, almost literally for keeps.

She stayed with it for years ("It ran for so long I felt imprisoned"), in London and on Broadway. By the end of it she was a star, or at any rate a starlet, who was not taken quite seriously and, in her own words, "was using the West End as a repertory", since she had no other way of practising. Another fluffy comedy followed, and then a T. S. Eliot play in which she admits to being hopelessly out of her depth. The turnaround came in 1961 when she won ("after endless auditions - well, no, there were only two, but it seemed endless") the role of Annie Sullivan, the Irishwoman who turned Helen Keller from a vegetable to a person, in The Miracle Worker: a role that had already made Anne Bancroft a star in New York.

Anna's own life was fairly traumatic at the time - her marriage to actor Jeremy Brett was breaking up, and she was decorating her flat for the first time in her life - but the pressure may have helped. Certainly she delivered an unsparing performance that took her out of the debutante class for ever. She had, in fact, been in on the last stages of the West End's period as centre of the British theatre: since then her stage career has leaned increasingly towards the subsidised sector, especially the National Theatre and the Royal Court, together with an enormous amount of work on radio and television.

Two especial problems in playing Gwen John. One, minor, was nudity, since Gwen first earned her living in Paris as a model. That was how she met Rodin. "I thought," said Miss Massey, bemused at finding herself faced with this for the first time at this stage in her career, "that I'd faint; but I didn't." (She adds that the script of Alfred Hitchcock's 1972 film Frenzy called for her to walk naked across a bedroom in long-shot, and she was rather annoyed that Hitchcock didn't actually ask her to do it.)

The other, larger problem is the perennial one in playing the famous: people expect them to be more elevated than they really are. As Anna Massey says: "Even Michelangelo must, occasionally, have said 'Pass the salt'."
