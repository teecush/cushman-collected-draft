Jun 24, 1979

Wenching In Old Austria

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Wenching in old Austria

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Schnitzler at the National Theatre.

THE title of Undiscovered Country (Olivier) is a three-way pun: not altogether surprising when the translator is Tom Stoppard.

Arthur Schnitzler's play is certainly unknown terrain to us. Within the play itself the Shakespearean words are used to describe the depths of the human heart. But we are unlikely to forget that in their original context they refer to death. Death, in this piece, is a very fashionable concept; almost the latest thing. Sort of like love.

We are in Austria, in the years just before the First World War. The Romantic Agony has been assimilated, and reduced to an agreeably titillating ache. The leading character, Hofreiter, is a successful manufacturer of light-bulbs, though his professional activities in the play are largely limited to telling his wife that he has been detained at the factory. An indefatigable wencher, he passes more or less unimpeded from one friend's wife to another, and is finally brought up short by a friend's daughter. She, being young, makes emotional demands on him that he would like to fulfil but cannot; at the height of his ardour his sense of self-interest still keeps ticking away.

Self-interest is not the same as conscience. Hofreiter is contrasted with another successful man, a hotelier this time, who can still reproach himself with his conduct to his wife: 'I loved her so much and was still capable of deceiving her.' (Not that this stops him from stocking his establishment with nubile domestic help and taking full advantage of the supply--to, be it said, the dazzled admiration of all his friends.)

The precise emotional state of the Hofreiter marriage is murky. Socially it is a great success--they keep lavish open house--but both partners are uncertain as to the desirability of Frau Hofreiter's continued fidelity. During the play she causes the deaths of two men: one she has rejected, and one she has accepted. As a woman of feeling, she suffers. Her husband has a highly developed flair for cerebral self-torment, but he is definitely a survivor.

Schnitzler himself was a doctor by profession, and also an amorist. He seems to have split himself between two of the characters in this play. There is a family-friend and physician; and there is Hofreiter, who seems also to have absorbed something of Mr Stoppard. At least I doubt if in the original German Hofreiter spoke quite so lightly and knowingly of his son's English education with 'all that fagging and cricket.'

The familiar crackle in lines like these is enhanced by their turning up on the lips of John Wood, the Stoppardian actor par excellence. Messrs Stoppard and Wood between them have probably bestowed on this part, and therefore on the whole play, a lighter and more febrile texture than it had in the original. That, however, is no complaint: Mr Wood's corkscrew calisthenics have seldom been put to better use, his wit shines and his trapped disgust steadily deepens.

In the last act the man fights a duel, which seems improbable, but Mr Wood makes us believe not in any particular motive, but in a voluminous weariness that can take on anything. (His opponent is young, and Hofreiter--with a teenage affair on his hands--has some desperate, biting things to say about youth.) Dorothy Tutin matches him exquisitely as his wife and there are performances of ebullient assurance by Michael Bryant and of startling promise by Emma Piper.

Peter Wood's production, too, is a fine piece of showmanship, down to the changes of William Dudley's ravishing green sets. (This, following on his designs for Dispatches, is some double.) The emblem of the evening is John Harding as an engaging young bore who, alone in the play, seems to have no familial or carnal relations with anyone else but is wedded to his tennis-racket. Tennis, in various formations, becomes a metaphor for the whole play: summery, playful, combative and capable of inflicting ignominious defeats. It is the most substantial piece of theatre we have seen this year.

*

Sensualists receive a similar wry come-uppance in Fearless Frank (King's Head), a musical based on the dubious memoirs of Frank Harris. The glory here is Bill Stewart's performance as Harris himself, dictating his autobiography to a secretary just the vital bit less impressionable than he hopes, and realising as he does so that half his claims are lies and that half of the other half are disreputable.

Mr Stewart, at once seedy and crisp, turns the character into a self-puncturing, self-repairing mechanism, still gamely lusting (with a repeated murmur of 'legs, legs, legs') at the final curtain. It is too arhythmic a performance that takes to song and dance with noticeable awkwardness. Andrew Davies has written him a witty, if slightly repetitive, script; his lyrics, and Dave Brown's music, are good enough to make you wish they were better. One number, actually, is superb: a preening, lascivious account by Guy de Maupassant ('maitre de la conte') of his innumerable conquests, surrounded by crooning couples with umbrellas: Fred Peters's choreography is a dazzling piece of Parisian parody, and the other routines take fair advantage of Harris's peripatetic career.

We get London songs, New York songs, Wild West songs, all routine and performed with immaculate precision. Technically, this is probably the best of the burgeoning small musicals; credit to the director, Robert Gillespie, and to the cast, which includes in the rolling-voiced Nichola McAuliffe and the wicked-eyed Mandy More such at least two snow-white musical hopes.

*

A belated but commendatory word for Tom McGrath's The Innocent (Warehouse), which certainly rambles enough, but whose dialogue is mostly sweet and funny enough to compensate. It follows a group of Glaswegians from the jazz-cellar of 1960 to the hippie pads of London in the ensuing decade; the drugs getting harder all the time so that we finally find them back home in a mental hospital, trying to kick their various habits. Some succeed, some fail: it all seems arbitrary, and that may pass as being lifelike.

A more serious fault is that Mr McGrath can only characterise them at second hand; he has to have them describe each other as his only means of sailing them apart. The actors help here too, of course; my favourite was smiling Paul Moriarty, but there is stiff competition from Jan Chappell, David Bradley, David Lyon and Anthony Higgins, and from a ferocious Hilary McRae. Mr McRae does not get to go to London; he stays in Glasgow 'getting crazier' and it is borne upon us at the end that he is the real victim. His mates were just enjoying themselves, because they could afford to. That isn't a bad point, but it is made so late you wonder Mr McGrath didn't sit down and start the play over.

*

Charley's Aunt (Adelphi) is fairly amusing. I share the general feeling that it ought to be more, and that the way to make it so is to cast the leading role as a believable embarrassed hearty rather than as an accomplished, eccentric drag-queen. John Inman's performance is certainly expert, but he cannot disguise the miscasting. Helen Cherry and Allan Cuthbertson are stalwart, and the sets are handsome; commercial theatre's Oxford looks indeed as sumptuous as subsidised Vienna, and why not?
