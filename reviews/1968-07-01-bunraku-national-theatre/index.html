Jul 1, 1968

Bunraku National Theatre

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

Bunraku National Theatre

Well, they are a civil company, I like them for that. They offer not to fleer, nor jeer, nor break jests, as the great players do; and then, there goes not so much charge to the feasting of them, or making them drunk, as to the other by reason of their littleness. Do they use to play perfect? Are they never flustered?

So Bartholomew Cokes, esquire of Harrow, to the puppet master of Bartholomew Fair. And the Bankside of his day, not having a Peter Daubeny to bring the wonders of the Orient to its friendly neighbourhood playhouse, he hadn't seen nothing. A civiller company of puppets than those of the Japanese Bunraku Theatre there can never have been; I work on the assumption that the essence of civility is restraint, and that of restraint, control. The Bunraku cross new frontiers of control, new for us, anyway. They use to play perfect, and they are never flustered. As to their propensity for fleering, jeering and breaking jests, I would not like to swear; they have a trick of mountainous eyebrow-raising before which the Aldwych audience, myself among them, had a tendency to cave in. But subsequent recourse to the programme synopses revealed that nothing particularly funny was going on at the time.

For long stretches the Bunraku—to a far greater degree than anything else seen in this year's World Theatre season—is a richly incomprehensible experience, that defies you to identify with its intended audience. That audience, however, has deserted it. Bunraku is dying in Japan, though acclaimed over here. It looks as if ignorance is bliss or do the Japanese find each other inscrutable?

I would not want to make too much of this point; I enjoyed the Bunraku's second programme more than the first, and this must in part have been due to growing familiarity. But I still remained a spiritual tourist; held, even hypnotised, but never involved, never fundamentally interested.

There is much to wonder at. Puppetry as we know it tends to be ingratiating, designed more often than not for children. Here, on the whole, it is endistancing, due to an unexpected eruption of the human element. The puppeteers are visible, one maestro and two hooded assistants to each four-foot doll; in prospect, this struck me as an impossibly cluttering device. In retrospect, I still cannot see how it failed to be. At the time, I just gasped at the spaciousness of it all. Nothing could have been less cluttered.

These puppets, though, operate on the grand scale, moving over most of the Aldwych stage (a segment stage left is reserved for the impassive musicians and storytellers) in front of grandiose Pollock-print sets. A moment at which the stage was occupied by only two dolls, both flopped face-down on the deck, reminded me irresistibly of Punch and Judy; this, I decided, was the Cinemascope version. But a second's awareness of the puppeteers' presence was enough to banish that idea; were ventriloquists perhaps a more fruitful parallel? But there was none of the chumminess of manipulator and dummy; there was, though, an open flaunting of the tension that must underlie any such art, and supplied the motive-power for such a nightmare film as Dead of Night (and, by extension, for the computer sequence of Kubrick's 2001): the sense of a continual battle for possession.

During moments of impending conflict (they were frequent) when puppets clawed to get at each other, their apparent struggle to escape their controllers almost supplied a sub-plot. The climax comes in the first programme with a puppet's suicide; with shocking abruptness, in the wake of a most delicate depiction of apparently autonomous misery, the doll is picked up and hurled to the ground. But there's a catch: resurrection follows. A Bunraku puppet cannot be killed that easily. When suicide is really final it is treated with respect; as in the stunning closing image of the second programme. Two lovers, who have chosen the sword in preference to separation, slump slowly, and with infinite grace, towards each other, the lights fading. The Bunraku may be ancient, incidentally, but they have nothing to learn about the use of electricity.

The sheer physical dexterity—the loving exact sewing, the shocking realism of the fights—need not be rehearsed here; it impressed but did not excite me. But the most compelling of the plays, The Subscription List, furnished a real virtuoso performance; Benkei, the servant who turns master in order to protect his lord. The mixture of joy in deceit and fear of detection was consummately conveyed by manipulator and reciter: the final dance of triumph brought the house down. Benkei (a creation of the Japanese Shakespeare, we were told, though not by the programme) belongs with Figaro and Truffaldino: he took his calls like the star he transparently is.
