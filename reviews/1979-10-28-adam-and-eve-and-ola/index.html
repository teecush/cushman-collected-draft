Oct 28, 1979

Adam And Eve And Ola

Robert Cushman · The Observer

THE HERO of David Lan's Sergeant Ola and his Followers (Royal Court) is a New Guinean NCO who fought for the British and Australians against the Japanese and returns home after the war to act as an intermediary between his own people and the white rulers.

The New Guineans, as Mr Lan presents them, are literalists. They believe everything the white man tells them and make no allowance for his propensity for metaphor. However, since those metaphors are now mostly cliches, the New Guineans who emerge as the more poetic; and it is his deployment of this poetry that gives Mr Lan's play its peculiar, and altogether delightful, flavour.

It is there particularly in the character of Ola himself. "That's not progress," he snaps on meeting a native with two wives. Linked to Ola's belief in progress is that of the Guineans in "cargo" -- the material goods that will arrive by boat, or maybe drop from the skies, as the British have promised. For the sake of cargo they abandon their own religion and turn to the white man's big, big ancestors, Adam and Eve. They have previously believed themselves to be descended from parrots, and when Ola stumbles across some fragments of Darwinian theory he realises the extent to which he has been tricked. The whites' real ancestors are monkeys; they have fobbed Adam and Eve off on his people in order to keep them in subjection. All right, he says, all right and okey-coke, and sets about the work of re-enlightenment.

I fear that I may have made the play sound insufferably quaint; it is actually extremely tough, but blessed by a pervasive lightness of touch: capable too of great poignance when dealing with the turmoil of Ola himself, or the desperation by her belief that "because of me cargo never come". In the second act, perhaps, one wishes that Mr Lan would bring on some heavier ammunition; tracing the first steps towards New Guinean independence, he hardly allows us to distinguish Ola's views from those of the more primitively inclined local leader, Takis. Weightlessness does have its limitations, but its attractions are excellently captured in Max Stafford-Clark's production, which boasts a major performance, quickfire but with rumbling depths, from Norman Beaton as Ola. There being, I imagine, a shortage of New Guinean actors in London, the play has been given a multi-national cast, among whom Bruce Alexander is particularly funny as Ola's Australian opposite number.

*

My Fair Lady is superlative: a fresh coat of paint for what I take to be the best romantic musical ever written. Surprise and enchantment set in from the moment the curtain rises, which it does much earlier than has been customary. What used to be the overture is now a prologue, in which Adrian Vaux's set (which remains as a glass-and-metal casing for the entire show) and Gillian Lynne's choreography bring Covent Garden market to pulsating life.

I have never seen a chorus act better, and Miss Lynne keeps them and the principals at it throughout. Wouldn't It Be Loverly is a touchingly funny slice of wish-fulfilment; dustman Doolittle's two numbers, crammed with comic detail, are great Cockney cadenzas radiating out from their central figure. But perhaps the most remarkable achievement is I Could Have Danced All Night, in which Eliza, tired beyond sleep, comes to emotional life. Liz Robertson (helped by a slowed-down final chorus I don't remember from the original) magically combines the academic mind at bay but fighting mad, emotionally childish but resilient.

There is a touch of archness about Tony Britton's Higgins and Peter Bayliss's Doolittle, but in the musical-comedy context that is reasonable enough. Mr Britton treasures his Shavian set-pieces and delivers his closing confession, I've Grown Accustomed to Her Face, with a finesse upon which Rex Harrison at his best (by the time I saw him his performance was a bit pitiful) could hardly have improved.

Shaw, you may remember, had Eliza marry the hopeless Freddy, rather than face a lifetime of fetching Higgins's slippers. (The ending of My Fair Lady, it must be confessed, makes The Taming of the Shrew look like a feminist tract.) Valentine, the philandering dentist, looks forward at the end of You Never Can Tell to similar subjection.

Peter Egan's performance of this role at Hammersmith is marvellously airy, and as much in command of the rhythm (letting it drag, then picking it up again in double time) as a first-rate jazz singer. The character men are out in force: Paul Rogers, who is expected perfection as the waiter, John Bryans in storming form as the Q.C. ex machina, Frank Middlemass as the solicitor and David Waller as the tetchy long-lost father. Mr Waller, though, makes Crampton rather lovable, and he wouldn't have lost his family in the first place if he'd been that. This softness, and accompanying sloth, in David Giles's production also spoils the antiphonal twins in whom speed and sharpness are all. But the last act is charming showing, with its harlequins and columbines and false noses, that Shaw knew both what he was about and where he got it from.

Ever vigorously Shaw may have protested his un-erotic intentions, his perversity was our profit; we have two pieces for the price of one, and Pygmalion leads a double life.

*

I am not sure what Brian Trueman, the author of Tishoo (Wyndham's), is about: it could be apocalypse, or the unacceptable face of trade union militancy, or the intellectual's supposed inability to love, or the place of disinterested science in a shabby materialist world. Most of these themes cohere in the central role, a researcher who has probably discovered a cure for the common cold, but finds the world doesn't want or can't afford it.

His defence of pure knowledge (above politics, above everything) yields a splendid scene in which he confronts his cynical vice-Chancellor: Geoffrey Palmer in the latter role is the embodiment of clipped pragmatism, while Alec McCowen repeats his matchless interpretation of the academic mind at bay but fighting mad, emotionally childish but resilient.

To watch this actor in top gear is always a pleasure. He also has interesting relationships with Penelope Wilton as his assistant (who, when asked if they're sleeping together, says "No, it's gone beyond that") and with his experimental rabbit, the most dramatically prominent of its kind since Harvey. Other elements are less satisfactory, and the play accordingly spends a lot of time running on the spot. But it's promising.
