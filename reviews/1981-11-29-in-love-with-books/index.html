Nov 29, 1981

In Love With Books

Robert Cushman · The Observer

In love with books

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

There were, for a rarity, two plays last week which produced in their audiences a palpable feeling of enjoyment. Usually the most you hope for is numbed respect.

84 Charing Cross Road (Ambassadors) consists almost entirely of letters. On one side of the stage is the New York apartment of Helene Hanff, aspiring playwright and TV scriptwriter; at the other Marks and Co., a second-hand bookshop located at the address in the title. In 1949 Miss Hanff saw an ad by the firm in an American paper, and wrote to it requesting volumes of Hazlitt, Stevenson and Leigh Hunt. The firm -- represented by a gentleman signing himself FPD -- was able to supply her with two out of three; and thus began a twenty-year correspondence.

FPD soon stands revealed as Frank Doel, chief buyer; within three years he and Helene are on first-name terms. She continues to demand books, and to dispatch forthright opinions on them when received. ("This is not Pepys's Diary, this is some busybody editor's miserable collection of excerpts from Pepys's Diary, may he rot in hell.")

For payment she always sends straight dollar bills. She also, during the years of rationing, sends Christmas food-parcels, thereby gaining a small shopful of devoted pen-friends, one of whom sends her in return a recipe for Yorkshire pudding, a dish later described by the recipient as "a high, curved, smooth, empty waffle."

Inevitably they beg her to come to London. So -- to great theatrical advantage -- a suspense element creeps in. Will she make it? If not, why not? The implication, never spoken, is that she is a born solitary. What is budding between her on one side, London, the shop and Frank Doel on the other, is a love affair, and she needs to keep them at ocean's length.

David Swift charmingly depicts the ageing, mellowing process in Frank; but the dynamo, the show has to be Helene, self-projected in the letters as a feisty, generous, irascible, Anglo-cum-bibliophile autodidact. Rosemary Leach's performance breathes New York; her presence conjures up the public library, the neighbourhood delicatessen and long, brisk walks in Central Park.

James Roose-Evans's production falters only in an abrupt ending and in some unfortunate bursts of music. Miss Hanff appeared alongside her impersonator (whom she looks, and may well sound, nothing like) at the first night curtain-call, which choked us all up a bit, but it is a warmly tearful evening even without that: a rearguard fanfare for London and literacy, and the most likeable West End offering in years.

*

Equally relished, though otherwise quite different, is Trafford Tanzi (Lyric Studio, Hammersmith). Here the audience have a chance to express themselves; since they are deemed to be spectators at a wrestling match, some of the heckles were as good as Clare Luckham's dialogue, and the shouts of encouragement in the final bout were blood-curdling.

In this match Tanzi, a lady wrestler, takes on her husband, Dean Rebel, a man wrestler. We have already watched her entire early life rendered as a series of wrestling matches; with her mother, alternately domineering and sentimental, with her father, alternately sentimental and domineering, with her prettier girl-friend, with the school psychiatrist, with Dean who finds her sorrowfully working in a chippie and produces a hanky from his fly by way of comfort. Nobody understands her, and her request for a career lands her flat on the canvas. But she learns.

Miss Luckham is hardly making new points, but she writes with edge, music-hall brio. Her metaphor, in Chris Bond's production, is irresistible and the cast, led by Noreen Kershaw, fair dazzling. Whether Tanzi's ability to floor her old man in the ring actually proves anything is another matter.

*

The main Lyric stage is taken by a Cambridge Theatre Company production of The Soldier's Fortune, a lesser-known Restoration comedy. Its heroes are discarded soldiers, infused with more than regulation bitterness by their creator, Thomas Otway, who was one himself. Its pimp, Sir Jolly Jumble, is the most fully-developed specimen of his kind: in love with his work and an inveterate toucher-up of both sexes. Its two heroines show an independence of mind in tormenting their men (in or out of wedlock) that goes beyond roguish giggles, and its cuckold, Sir Davy Dunce, is a real menace.

Sheila Hancock's production is stranded between two styles. Inclining more to realism than prettiness, it yet uses some of the old conventions. Where once maids and gallants stood around looking decorative, now drabs and ruffians loaf on corners looking downtrodden. But they still look planted; and Robin Archer's closed-in sets forbid us to imagine a society for ourselves. Individual performances impose themselves as the play proceeds, and the complex last scene is excellently handled. Hugh Paddick effortlessly dominates the proceedings as Sir Jolly, emerging from unfelt beds where his more timorous author only specified "a delight in keyholes." Brian Murphy is a less endearing Sir Davy than once was Arthur Lowe (in a production that also featured Miss Hancock as a Lady Dunce of Vanessa Redgrave radiance), but his resentful brutality may well be closer to the script.

Richard III (Aldwych) is another of Alan Howard's player-kings, able to assume any role except that of the clean-limbed. He succeeds, with Richard Pasco's Clarence, by making him partner in a double-act; at other times his dissimulations are so blatant you wonder he takes anybody in.

Neither Mr Howard's performance nor Terry Hands's production run out of energy or, much, out of invention, but the play is left stranded before its close. Half the laughs earned by Joe Melia's second Murderer are inspired, half regretted. Sinead Cusack as Lady Anne in Richard III is herself: it is a relief to find, in Derek Godfrey as Buckingham, an actor who can get his laughs on and with the text.

That text is not sacred, but the changes Mr Hands has made, here and in Richard II, seem to me impoverishments. Permit me to go into detail. In Richard II the Queen's proposal that Richard be sent overseas with her is greeted by Northumberland with the hint that there were some love but little policy. At the Aldwych, Richard appropriates the last three words. This tells us that he has a bitingly realistic sense of political humour, but we know that already from the deposition scene; we are deprived of Northumberland's only avowal of his own nature, one he never dared to make while Richard was still nominally king.

In Richard III, Tyrrel, who has just announced the murder of the Princes in the Tower, is dispatched with an invitation to return and tell the tale in full. Here he is followed off by two of Richard's cronies, who are obviously going to kill him. We trade this new insight into Richard the lip-licking sadist for the platitude of the dictator who lets nobody out of his employ alive. Before Bosworth, Richard is brought a threatening note, which in Mr Hands's production is changed to merely insulting, one aimed at his henchmen. The actor loses the chance to show the king faltering and recovering his nerve; the audience discovers that he runs an unpopular administration, which with half his country in arms against him can hardly be news.

With subtlety so suspected, it is hardly surprising that the production's excitement runs down. The Hands-Howard collaboration (black-avised designs by Farrah) has been long and often rewarding; but they bring out the overkill in each other. This year they have done their best work apart.
