Aug 24, 1980

Lucifer On A Fork-Lift Truck

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Lucifer on a fork-lift truck

EDINBURGH FESTIVAL 1980

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the National Theatre's 'Passion' at the Assembly Hall.

THE pews have been removed from the Assembly Hall, and the ground-level is one great playing-space, where the audience, and even the actors, can disport themselves.

Ira Gershwin said it forty-odd years ago:

Each girl and boy alike, Sharing joy alike, Feels that Passion'll Soon be National.

Here is the National Theatre production of The Passion, drawn from the York Mystery Cycle, and familiar from three years' appearances at the Cottesloe, now fitted out with a first part of equal length. This has been labelled Creation to Nativity, though, in the abrupt and rather frustrating manner of the Mysteries themselves, it gives up the Old Testament narrative halfway through Genesis and jumps straight on to the Annunciation.

Jesus's story takes up the last play-and-a-half, but in this version it has little contact with what has gone before. We are given a human drama issuing in a cry of despair. There is no resurrection, no judgment; only a curt statement of belief delivered by Brian Glover in a miner's helmet, whose beaming lamp provides the most original lighting effect in contemporary theatre.

But this moment hardly returns us to the beginnings of the first play, where Mr Glover is God: a businesslike deity as he goes about creating everything, glimpsed through a warming forest of lanterns somewhere up among the rafters of the Assembly Hall. In an even finer burst of visual inventiveness Lucifer (Jack Shepherd--the medieval guilds of glovers and shepherds would be proud to see their descendants carrying on their traditions in such surroundings) delivers his doomed challenge from a red plush armchair, perched halfway up a forklift truck. Perdition is a rapid descent into a smoke-filled tub, which is then whirled fiercely around the hall like an outsize dodgem car.

Such toys as these are the modern equivalent of the pageant-wagons on which the plays were originally performed (the medieval theatre was machine-mad, far closer in spirit to Drury Lane than to the Warehouse) and Bill Bryden's production resembles the RSC's The Greeks in being an all-purpose raid on whatever aspects of the ancient tradition may be made to serve a modern turn. It is unprincipled in all respects save the most important, that of telling the story. The means vary, even the promenading audience perform a different function in each half. In the first play they are employed as witnesses of the performance, in the second of the events themselves.

The new piece is in fact a patchwork, episodic where its sequel is centrifugal. Humanity only breaks in with Dave Hill's Joseph, honestly aghast at the news of his wife's pregnancy. It is maintained in the famous episode, interpolated from the Wakefield Cycle, of Mak the sheep-stealer. (Somebody should write a ballad of Mak the crook.) This astonishing playlet, with its story of a sheep hopefully passed off as a newborn child ("What the devil is this, he has a long snout") parodies the Nativity as a prelude to celebrating it.

Its equivalent in the second half is the scene of the grimly practical torturers at the Crucifixion, a triumph of unselfconscious sophistication. The Assembly Hall acoustics are not too kind to extended conversation-pieces like these, but actors with tirades--notably Mr Hill's John the Baptist and Mr Shepherd's Judas, registering resentment in alliteration--rise splendidly above them while the Albion Band contribute at least two smashing numbers.

Another smashing number begins the Canadian production, Billy Bishop Goes to War (Moray House Gymnasium): a quietly mocking lament, with simple but artful rhymes for the generation who went off to die in World War One. It is sung by the complete cast, which is to say Eric Peterson and John Gray; Mr Gray also plays the piano and wrote the show. He comes up subsequently with nothing as good. This story of Canada's most productive (or destructive) air-ace masters all the ironies about the simultaneous horror and exhilaration of combat and the patronising attitude of the British establishment towards its colonial cannon-fodder, but it does little more than lay them efficiently out. Mr Peterson plays all the parts well.

I might have enjoyed it more had I not visited the Traverse that afternoon to see Barry Collins's The Iceman, another monologue about a survivor of the same war with scars left this time by class rather than race, and seeking to erase them or himself by undertaking a solo ascent of Everest. Chris Ettridge, the actor here, does nothing wrong, and neither, in strict literary terms, does Mr Collins. But theatrically it is a deadening exercise. A one-character play with a subtext is, I suppose, possible, but in practice we have to believe what the character says. There is no undercutting, no conflict. We know the man has weaknesses all right, but only because he tells us.

I began to yearn, after these two shows, for the spectacle of a couple of actors talking to each other. The Tarnished Phoenix (St Cecilia's Hall) offered two actors, side by side reading--Ian McKellen as D. H. Lawrence and Sheila Allen as Frieda. Mr McKellen can give me enormous pleasure when immersed in a character or when he is simply being himself--as here in a few carefully artless sentences of welcome--but he was mostly betwixt and between: compelling admiration when turning the ending of "Snake" ("I have something to expiate: a pettiness") into a cadenza of disgust, but defusing Lawrence's intolerably censorious outbursts against people assuredly no worse than himself with a self-deprecation completely alien to them. Miss Allen got closer to the iron self-righteousness in "How beastly the bourgeois," though some of her other selections were, at the first performance, less assured (she was deputising at short notice for Janet Suzman).

The fringe are offering two Jacobean rarities, Massinger's A New Way to Pay Old Debts (the Priory Theatre Company) and Middleton and Dekker's The Roaring Girl (Cambridge University Mummers). The amateurs do far better than the professionals, and this only partly because they have the richer play. Massinger's piece, for centuries a stage and schoolroom classic, has fallen into disuse and it does emerge here as thinly as disconcerting to realise that the legendary extortioner-villain, Sir Giles Overreach, never stands a chance of grinding anyone's face in dust: they're all on to him from the start of Act One. The production is inspiringly stylised; because his name merely denotes his job: he's a watchman.

The Cambridge Players have correctly deduced that characters with allegorical monikers (as it might be, Dapper or Trapdoor) had better be played as real people. This they do, with assurance in a pleasing variety of styles: it is possible to make a book on which of these players is bound for the Half Moon, which for the Olivier, and which for the issue desk of the nearest public library.
