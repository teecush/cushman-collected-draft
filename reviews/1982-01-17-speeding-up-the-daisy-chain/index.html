Jan 17, 1982

Speeding Up The Daisy-Chain

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Speeding up the daisy-chain

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the RSC's production of Schnitzler's 'La Ronde'.

I HAVE a vested interest in La Ronde (Aldwych), one that originated some 17 years ago. I was at Cambridge, and involved in undergraduate drama.

The vogue word, then as now, was 'workshop'; and though none of us knew quite what it meant any more than anyone does today, there was a general feeling that Schnitzler's play, being a series of duologues, must have some relevance to it. Here were 10 discrete scenes that could be taken away, intensively rehearsed, improvised around and brought back for the judgment of one's peers.

I was given responsibility for three scenes. One of them, between a Husband and a Wife, took place entirely in bed, so that is where it was rehearsed; I still remember the surprise on the faces of fellow-students when they looked in to borrow the milk. I also remember being impressed by the sly humour of this scene, which consists chiefly of the man sermonising at a supposed innocent who in fact has already been unfaithful to him; by the sleek brutality of the subsequent restaurant episode, in which the Husband seduces a Viennese prototype of alluring helplessness referred to by most translators as The Sweet Girl; and by the vain regrets of Schnitzler's last duet, in which a faintly ridiculous Count awakens in a Prostitute's room and tells himself how beautiful it would have been if he had done nothing but kiss her eyes. (I don't know what became of the Prostitute or the Sweet Girl -- typical male negligence, I admit -- but I can tell you that the Husband and the Count both grew up to wear masks in the National Theatre production of 'The Oresteia'.)

I remember that the dialogue revealed great depth and resilience, warranting any amount of digging. And I remember that the text I used (an American anthology) bore on its fly-leaf the name of John Barton, now director and co-translator of the play for the RSC. To adapt Stanley Kowalski, Mr Barton and me have had this date from the beginning. A long time anyway.

However, one of the lessons of Schnitzler's piece is that long-awaited encounters are apt to issue in anti-climax. I was entertained by Mr Barton's production, but also disappointed. There is some difficulty in appealing to the author's intentions, since one of them -- formed long before riots and court cases fed him up with the whole business -- was that 'La Ronde' should not be staged. Being written in dialogue form by a dramatic genius it is playable, but it seems actually to have been meant as a literary exercise, a confessional joke for a small circle. One stage direction calls for a pause of 'a couple of minutes'. Clearly any director is going to have to speed that up. But Mr Barton speeds up the whole scene, which is one of leisurely summery seduction, and indeed the whole play.

When Trevor Nunn directed 'Once in a Lifetime', he called in Gillian Lynne to choreograph the scene-changes, thus eliminating gaps in the action. The device worked, since it provided a smoother ride for what was always intended to be a fast machine. Mr Barton, too, has summoned Miss Lynne; the play is built like a daisy-chain and she shows us the survivor of each encounter hurrying on to the next through a suitable cross-section of old Vienna. The character changes clothes while the sets prettily and efficiently change themselves. Continuity is preserved. Fatal.

If 'La Ronde' is a good play, it is so in a different fashion from any other good play. (For one thing it disposes of Tynan's over-quoted dictum about the necessity of a protagonist being brought to a state of desperation; there is no protagonist and nobody despairs.) Discontinuity is its point; no character on his or her second appearance makes any reference to the first. The theatrical effect lies in the surprise of discovering each in a fresh situation; and that surprise is destroyed if we have seen them dance their way there. Miss Lynne's work anyway is far below her best; a set of hesitation waltzes makes no call on her particular dynamism.

There are no gaps within the scenes either. Each includes copulation, represented in the text by a row of asterisks; we get to see thrashing bodies (generally under sheets) and to hear grunts and squeals. Here too speed is of the essence; clearly the Hapsburg Viennese did not believe in foreplay and had enviably rapid powers of recovery. Here we can call Schnitzler to witness; when he assisted on the first production he was all in favour of discretion. Would it seem unbearably square just to lower the lights and raise them again?

The alternative, on this showing, is forced and irrelevant comedy. So the sexual act looks funny from outside; it wasn't meant to be spectator sport. Here it imposes a farcical style on the true comedy of the surrounding dialogue, which is based on the lies people tell to their partners and themselves when sex is approaching or has just gone.

Not all the actors succumb. The restaurant scene (all Austrian plays should have one, and most do) proves as good as I had thought it, with Judy Buxton provocatively vulnerable and Tony Church swinishly complacent. Mr Church also shines in the marital chamber, lecturing Susan Fleetwood. Going another step farther back, Miss Fleetwood has fun as the wife getting herself bedded by an over-eager Young Gentleman (who winds up, after one false start, congratulating himself over at last having an affair with a respectable woman), but she misses a few layers of well-bred self-deception; she plays like one of those Restoration comedy ladies who cry 'rape' in a delighted whisper.

At the other end of the play John Nettles so stresses the Count's silliness that he has trouble in gaining our serious attention at the close; he just makes it. The prize couple, no question, are Richard Pasco and Barbara Leigh-Hunt as Poet and Actress, both monsters of egoism, professional as much as sexual; if, as full-blown caricatures, they step outside the play's boundaries, they do so in the script as well, standing as they do for the author and his warmest flame. And at this point we can do with the change.

The Prostitute begins and ends the play, making if you will the point that sex-without-love might as well be sex-for-money. No, I don't believe that either. And since the productions in London and Manchester have occasioned much male critical breast-beating (of male critical breasts) it had better be stated that, statistically, Schnitzler's ladies are almost -- not quite -- as predatory as his gentlemen, that the post-coital scenes do not consist entirely of male sneers and female tears, and that, to claim otherwise is the grossest sentimentality.

*

Playing the Game (King's Head) comes from Theatre Clwyd, and you will guess that the game is rugby. The first scene, sexier than any in La Ronde, shows the captain in bed with the chairman's wife. When she shows up on club premises, though, he goes off her; the male bond does its work. Later a stripper turns up, to tell the committee -- whom she has just met -- an implausible number of home-truths. The upshot -- that rugger clubs breed fear and hatred of women -- is probably true, but is hardly news.

The author, Jeffrey Thomas, has trouble getting his characters on and off; but his dialogue flows and he is recognisably a playwright. The acting is mostly too blatant for the space.
