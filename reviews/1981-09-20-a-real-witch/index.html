Sep 20, 1981

A Real Witch

Robert Cushman · The Observer

A real witch

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Stratford and London.

Michael Pennington as Hamlet: "An unusually clear-footed production."

IF STRATFORD'S Other Place did not maintain the highest performance standards in the country, something would be seriously wrong. It has all the advantages. Here is the main-house company freed from the task of animating over-familiar masterpieces in a sprawling house for tourist audiences. For the fourth time this year the marvellously compact room down the road rings with the sound of rediscovery.

The Witch of Edmonton is the joint work of Dekker, Ford and Rowley, generally credited with homeliness, horror and humour respectively. It is late Elizabethan: in fact Jacobean, going on Caroline, a period in which drama had narrowed in both social and emotional scope. Tragedy meant either exotic trials of honour (as demonstrated to us last year in The Maid's Tragedy) or, as here, local woes hot from the press.

Elizabeth Sawyer was a real woman executed for sorcery. Dekker--it must have been Dekker--presents her with undeviating insight as a woman who was indeed a witch, but only because her neighbours drove her to it, abusing her as one long before the fact. (Dekker, who spent long periods in the debtors' gaol, was the most charitable of the Elizabethans. This has been his year, the National having already given us his Shoemaker's Holiday; it now remains to add that fine double Dekker, The Honest Whore Parts I and II, the closest English drama ever came to domesticated Breughel.)

Rowley gave the witch a comic antagonist--and incidentally wrote a fat part for himself--in Cuddy Banks, a gullible countryman, whose bigoted father is Mother Sawyer's chief persecutor. And Ford supplied much of the alternate line of action; a weak-willed young man, Frank Thorney, is duped into one marriage by the employer who has impregnated his sweetheart, then bullied into a second by his father as a means of retrieving the family fortunes. Both women love him; he murders the second to stay with the first, prompted to the act by Mother Sawyer's devil.

This familiar is the play's unifying spirit. He comes in the shape of a dog, and his emergence as a long creeping shape from a heap of sacking is the masterstroke of Barry Kyle's production. He frightens as a demon before he endears as a pet, and thus the play's main hurdle is surmounted. He beats out the cur in The Two Gentlemen of Verona as the best canine role ever written; this one talks and from his first ominous words ("ho, have I caught thee cursing") Miles Anderson's agile, grinning presence dominates the play.

Like any Elizabethan fiend he can assist a crime, but he cannot cause or commit it. He exercises a nice sense of proportion among his victims, pursuing Frank or Sawyer to damnation, but Cuddy to only a ducking. If he is one unifying factor, the other--brilliantly realised by Mr Kyle and his designer, Chris Dyer, who works with an economic sense of period--is Edmonton itself, a village with its own standards of kindliness and social responsibility, but bitter to outsiders; the production begins with the whole cast singing a Puritan hymn: all but the presumptive witch who is already isolated.

It is a weakness of the play that the authors plainly ran out of things for her to do, though they reverse normal dramatic practise in one scene where the law intervenes to save her from the town; instead of an imaginary witch hounded, a real one protected. This is the element Arthur Miller left out of The Crucible (women who indeed were witches or believed themselves to be); and Miriam Karlin explores both the suffering and the malice; her execution scene, sporting both, is especially fine.

The two wives are sympathetically played by Harriet Walter and Juliet Stevenson, both excellent at suggesting relationships with a glance. There is a similar alertness in Gerard Murphy's Frank, but since he gives all his words an equal hammering emphasis it becomes difficult to listen to him. There is much natural fun in Anthony O'Donnell's Cuddy, and warm work from George Raistrick, a stricken father who represents the community voice at its best.

*

At the beginning of Hamlet (Aldwych) the stage looks like a build-your-own-Elsinore kit; as the eye sweeps round, it encounters a table with tankards (for Rhenish-quaffing usurpers), candlesticks (for play-going usurpers), a crucifix (for repentant usurpers), swords, halberds, furled banners, benches, a throne: all grouped around a forward-thrusting platform, a stage upon a stage.

As the gravedigger says, "it argues an act"; this is John Barton's theatrical-metaphor production; and though the scheme might not be apparent to those blessed innocents who read neither programme-notes nor reviews, they could hardly fail to notice that something had clearly been thought by someone, that these thoughts had been communicated in rehearsal, and that what has resulted is an unusually clear-footed production.

Clear-voiced too; both Michael Pennington as Hamlet and Derek Godfrey as Claudius can whisper to excellent effect. Mr Pennington is to begin with an almost aggressively (i.e. defensively) traditional Hamlet; black suit, open-neck white shirt, sunk in grief, nonsardonic: in short, immensely refreshing. Humour comes in later; as does shock: "oh my prophetic soul, my uncle" is not ha-ha, but a realisation of a long-suppressed fear.

He proceeds to play the antic conscientiously and with enjoyment, with electric moments when--as in Ophelia's reminder that his father has been dead four months--his madness brings him up against the truth. He is best at argumentation, advice, badinage, despair, ratiocination and anything in soliloquy; defiance eludes him. (And if the Barton line is that Hamlet only plays at defiance, but can't bring it off, I don't believe it.)

I have known Hamlets who provided more incidental kicks, but none who drew a tauter, more elastic line through the play, which becomes a story of a conscientious, wayward young man in impossible circumstances, doing his best. The play has never made more sense, the acting motif helping it round corners and over stiles (and, indeed, styles). It also enables Bruce Purchase to provide an avuncular First Player showing the noble amateur how these things should really be done (modestly, with one climactic burst), and illuminates the Polonius household.

Carol Royle as Ophelia has also to go through a series of charades at her father's bidding, and the strain visibly breaks her. Nor is it surprising that she should so desperately miss Tony Church's loving, officious Polonius; anyone would. Derek Godfrey, who excels at playing jokey villains, is efficient but opaque as the tormented one of this play; Barbara Leigh-Hunt, however, finds volumes in Gertrude.

*

The Case of David Anderson Q.C. (Lyric Studio) offers the piquant spectacle of Corin Redgrave playing a defrocked Conservative MP; and playing him well, as a driven, honourable man. John Hale's documentary play is an argument for the reopening of Mr Anderson's case, he having been dropped from public life after allegations of importuning women; and a persuasive one. It is also an enthralling tale, marred only by a glut of undigested facts (undigested by me, anyway) and some melodrama--much curt turning on heels by subordinate characters--in the production.

*

Macready (Arts) is an instructive and entertaining one-man show prepared and performed by Frank Barrie; an account of one of the last century's two or three most lauded actors who hated doing it, except when he loved it.
