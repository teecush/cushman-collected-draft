Biases In Bombay

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Biases in Bombay

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on David Hare's 'A Map of the World.'

DAVID HARE'S A Map of the World (Lyttelton) is set, for the most part, at a UNESCO conference on poverty, held in Bombay in the mid-1970s. One of the participants is a novelist called Victor Mehta, born in India, but living in England, who has written satirically of Marxist regimes.

He thus attracts the hostility of Third World delegates, who demand that he preface his address with a disclaimer to the effect that since all fiction is a distortion, his audience need not believe a word he has ever written. It turns out that this has been drafted by a young British journalist, Stephen Andrews, who regards Mehta as reactionary and has already told him so. Mehta, meanwhile, has put Stephen down as a courier of received ideas. Mehta refuses to have Stephen's words put into his mouth, but consents to engage with him in a formal, private debate.

There's more to it. Mehta has been to bed with Peggy, an American also fancied by Stephen, and she offers herself as prize to the winner of the contest.

There's more yet. Mehta has turned his Bombay experience into a novel. This novel is made into a film. Intercut with the scenes in and around the conference are episodes on the movie-set. Shooting is interrupted by the arrival of the real Mehta and Peggy, who complain of falsification.

To take the three levels separately: the first, the polemical, is fine. Mehta speaks for the writer's duty of non-involvement, Stephen for his public obligations. (There is also a good number for an African delegate on the irrelevance of Mehta's ironies to the experience of a developing nation.) Mehta and Stephen know how to expose each other's weaknesses, and we can sense in both of them the moments at which prejudice or defensiveness take over from argument.

Indeed, with this sense of what the current Tom Stoppard play calls "the public configuration of private derangement" already so firmly expressed, it is doubtful if we need the second, sexual, level at all. However we have it, and it's calamitous. The characters have no existence outside their opinions. Peggy has to be hastily fitted up with a case for sexual freedom to justify her generous offer.

Her one scene with Mehta in the bedroom (not in bed) tells us nothing; the play indeed is continually stating relationships - as when Stephen says he's grown fond of Mehta - that it doesn't dramatise. And this exacerbates a feeling that the argument, if not actually rigged, is hanging on the wrong hinge. When Mehta is asked to proclaim something he doesn't believe, one is shocked, and nothing that can later be said for or against him erases that shock. What good would it do anybody for him to tell lies? (The conference can go on without him, and does.)

Finally, the form: the play-about-a-film-about-a-novel-about-real-life. I assume that the conference scenes depict what really happened rather than the movie distortion. (The production doesn't make it very clear, but there's a clue in the text. The real Peggy is an actress; the film turns her into that rarest of birds, a jazz violinist. If you hear references to Peggy acting, you'll know you're all right.) If this isn't the case, then the whole play is frivolous beyond redemption; but unless Mr Hare is offering us the longest takes in film history it can't be. The alternative, though, is only the lesser of two evils.

We get some entertaining backstage stuff, including a very funny scene in which the solving of a crossword puzzle sets off a political row conducted by people entirely locked into their own preconceptions. This is a pleasing comic comment on the main action, but it could have been worked in without the superstructure.

That structure itself of course echoes the play's concern with partis pris; here are all these characters seeing things from their own points of view, and here's the film adding its, and getting it wrong. Oddly enough, not even the director gives Mehta much of an argument on this and the actors all agree with him that the picture's rubbish. So all we have is another statement about all art and all thought being biased: the film, and Mehta's novel, and Peggy's reminiscence, and Mr Hare's play. It's true of course. It's true of every play ever written. Audiences (Dr Johnson pointed this out 200 years ago and he can't have been the first) take it for granted.

Roshan Seth has charm and assurance as Mehta, though he seems to be forcing himself up to the size of the theatre. Bill Nighy is a speedy, pugnacious Stephen and between takes he is the most convincing actor I have ever seen acted. Diana Quick as Peggy is defeated by an American accent and the amorphousness of the role. Sheila Scott-Wilkinson is forceful and refreshing, except in one scene where she is just forceful. There are witty cameos from Stefan Gryff as the director and Ronald Hines as a pugnacious performer. Nick Bicat's music is powerful. The best of Mr Hare's stage pictures as director is of four diplomats sitting in a row, listening intently to the main proceedings.

*

The Actors Touring Company have begun a season at the Warehouse with Alfred Jarry's Ubu, which they bill as Ubu the Vandalist. Ubu is the regicidal monster, egged on by his wife and with much else of Macbeth about him, in whom Jarry caricatured what he took to be the worst aspects of the French bourgeoisie, and he does indeed, after his career in a mythical Poland, take refuge in France.

Peter Brook, who must have suffered in Parisian lodgings, has described him as the archetypal concierge, but his own production was noticeably unvengeful: I remember it as good fast knockabout. John Retallack's for the ATC is a lot slower, and seems to be taking the piece very seriously.

The play was designed to make its audiences uncomfortable, an invitation few directors can resist. But it is now a classic, fit to be performed by a group whose repertory also includes Cervantes, Shakespeare and Vanbrugh; so the ATC are trying to scandalise, enlighten and entertain us all at the same time. (Scatological dialogue and frantic action are good for you, runs the message.) The production is inventive, in bursts, and the cast are neat in their timing and their miming.

Chris Barnes and Christine Bishop, masked and puffed up as Pere and Mere Ubu, have found a vocal style to match. It's a slow bellow and is cleverly, if wearingly, maintained. The not noticeably bad King Wenceslas is played by Russell Enoch, in whom I thought I recognised William Russell, television's Sir Lancelot of the Fifties.

*

Carol Williams's And All Things Nice (Old Red Lion, Islington) is about a 15-year-old black girl whose dad throws her out and who ends up double quick in a punk squat, on drugs and the game. Only linking narrative makes any attempt at sophistication. The jagged short-breathed scenes may reflect an inability to write anything else, but as the essence of the world it presents is a brief attention-span (life would be unliveable otherwise) it suits very well. Charlie Hanson's production uses the window of this upstairs pub-room, to breezy verite effect, as the window of an upstairs room.
