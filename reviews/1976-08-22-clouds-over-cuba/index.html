Clouds Over Cuba

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Clouds over Cuba

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

The theme of Michael Frayn's Clouds (Hampstead) may best be considered in the light of two exchanges between Hamlet and Polonius. The first, concerning what might be called the cumulative effects of cumulus, demonstrated that a cloud might assume the shape, given goodwill or gullibility in the beholder, of a camel, a weasel or a whale. The other consists of the question 'what do you read, my lord?' and the answer, concrete without being specific, 'words, words, words.' Words, like clouds, can mean anything.

This is hardly a new idea, even in the theatre; indeed the theatre might be considered its natural home. Life can be presented as a continual banana-skin joke: every man the prisoner of his own preconceptions and continually stumbling over his neighbour's. One of the best Jacobean comedies (Chapman's The Widow's Tears) was described by a critic as 'irony made visible' and that, in his own kinder fashion, is what Mr Frayn gives us in Clouds, a comedy in the classic line.

Clouds is set in Cuba, and most of its characters are writers. We therefore have a locale which acts as a litmus for contrasting prejudices, and protagonists whose lives are dedicated to articulating them. We meet a male journalist sent out by one of the Sunday supplements, and a lady novelist despatched by the other. (It is not too difficult to guess which scribe belongs to which paper.)

The gentleman, given an enchantingly bilious acidity by Nigel Hawthorne, is inclined to slight the achievements of the revolution, and even more inclined to slight those of his competitor. 'Isn't it typical of that lot,' he inquires at a moment of easily won exasperation, 'to send out some manic-depressive amateur.' He then proceeds to exclaim 'Oh God!' three times. There is no great passion in the exclamations; they are rattled off in a vein almost of self-caricature. For a moment I suspected Mr Hawthorne of stylising his performance, then realised that in fact he was giving a wholly realistic performance of a man who stylises his own life.

Amateur or not, Barbara Ferris's hackette is inclined to get the stories that Mr Hawthorne misses: obviously, human interest still means something. But she too has her bad moments, usually descending on her in the morning when 'you know you can't get to the next day till you've been all the way through this one.'

Joined to these two are an American academic about to add another to his long line of enthusiastic publications on the new Cuba ('another country floating free of money and greed'); they are guided from farm to factory to incipient city by a cheerful driver who is mostly silent (since he has no English and the play little Spanish) and a government guide, beautifully portrayed by Paul Chapman, who is considerably more voluble but whose habit of beginning every sentence with 'I think' helps to give the play its peculiarly unnerving quality.

Every scene is a game of cross-purposes, and the play is in fact staged by Michael Rudman as a series of games; half-a-dozen chairs are continually rearranged to form the set. The only thing to be said against this format is that, smacking of revue, it lacks a little in narrative excitement. The play's many virtues do not include suspense.

They do, however, include surprise. Each of the four men is concerned to make a play for Miss Ferris and it seems to be only by accident that she ultimately (or, not to give too much away, penultimately) is matched with her compatriot, Mr Hawthorne. She claims she planned it, but why believe her? One of Mr Frayn's achievements is to provide the first convincing love-scenes to reach our stage in years but, the play being what it is, the love presented is conditional and much undermined. Our hero is very distressed by our heroine's habit of using him for copy, noting his words as he says them. There are recurrent choric scenes in which the characters are seen frantically typing or fretfully scribbling, ostensibly about Cuba, but actually about one another. They are the play's method, and ours, of keeping score.

At one point a dispute occurs over what Mr Hawthorne, with elaborate casualness, refers to as 'labour-camps'; the American prefers the expression 'work-camps.' Miss Ferris, belatedly surfacing, only hears the word labour and accordingly asks about the Cuban attitude to abortion. Mr Frayn's word-play is less effervescent than Tom Stoppard's, but even more acute; his view of the middle-classes better humoured than Alan Ayckbourn's (and his farce accordingly less mechanically expert), but his satire even more accurate.

*

Like Clouds, Troilus and Cressida (Stratford) is an open-ended play. In his celebrated, and lamentably influential, production some years ago, John Barton reduced its various moods to garish nihilism. His new version (co-directed with Barry Kyle) goes some way towards making amends. His Prologue (John Nettles, who doubles as Thersites) is properly sardonic, mocking the 'fair beholders' with the inevitably limited amount that may be 'digested in a play': the last word spat out.

Troilus and Cressida themselves can seldom have been so well played. Both of them are emotional gourmets, precocious sophisticates. Mike Gwilym smiles through both his expectation and his misery, Francesca Annis, both when holding off and giving in, palpably enjoys being a girl. But too many of the previous production's excesses linger on: trimmed and played against this season's sturdy permanent set, they lack even the courage of their own convictions.

Robin Ellis goes gamely through the flamboyant motions expected of this vicious bisexual Achilles, but seems, possibly to his credit, distrustful of them. Tony Church is a lucid enough Ulysses (though his best moment is silent as he shudders at Thersites's cackling that will outlast all his diplomacy); his famous comparison of Time to 'a fashionable host . . . with his arms outstretched as he would fly' finds amusing echo in David Waller's Pandarus, who flutters everywhere.

But neither of these performances is fully realised, and the rest are mostly mediocre. When the actors are dull, who cares what brilliant ideas the directors may have had, especially when one suspects that they may be partly responsible for pinioning the players.

*

In Mustapha Matura's Bread (Drum Arts Centre at the Young Vic), an unproduced black playwright declares that he would like to eat Shakespeare; learning that there is only pasta in the house, he settles for Pirandello. And Mr Matura in fact offers us a Pirandellian entertainment; his hero's play takes shape before us, showing us greed, revenge and extortion in Trinidad to match those the outer action depicts in London. Writing, acting and direction are all better in Trinidad, though the same players feature in both halves. Gordon Case, good as the creator, is even better as his creation.
