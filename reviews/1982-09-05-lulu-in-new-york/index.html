Sep 5, 1982

Lulu In New York

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Lulu in New York

FOR its second production at the Lyceum, the American Repertory Theatre offered Wedekind's Lulu, directed by Lee Breuer and set in New York.

One was less aware of time and place than of technology, though I suppose the production was trying to prove that they are the same thing. The stage was filled with cameras, lights, projections. Lulu danced in silhouette behind a screen. All lines were delivered through hand-mikes, as in an old-fashioned rock musical.

According to Mr Breuer (this paper, last week), this does away with artificial vocal projection. It sounded pretty artificial to me, having the whole play done in a sinister whisper. The actors seemed competent enough, but they were prevented from ever making contact. I liked the looks of Catherine Slade's Lulu, but can make no other comment. It looked to me like a stage version of the 20-year-old Julie Christie film Darling.

This, again, was Concept Theatre, much in vogue in serious America. You buy this concept--it usually boils down to relocation--and it's so dazzling you don't have to worry about character or dialogue or situation. If you don't believe me, read some American criticism. It results in a series of jokes--solemn jokes, what's worse--at the expense of the plays. And, of course, of the audience. There's only one way to build a classic theatre tradition, and that's through acting.

To be fair, Lulu is a concept play; this story of a sexy girl sounds more fun than it is. I left half-way through. In other circumstances I would probably have stayed, but there were hundreds of things to see on the fringe. Many of them are concentrated this year in the Assembly Rooms and the Circuit, both multiple spaces operating round the clock.

The Assembly Rooms offered Mr Breuer in better form with The Gospel at Colonus, Sophocles in black American church, preacher as protagonist, choir and congregation as chorus. This was powerful stuff that brought the play to life, though only in bits; the idea was deposited on stage rather than developed, the story so truncated it was hard to care.

Ben Halley Jr as Oedipus looked, as you would expect of a black man playing blind in dark glasses in a gospel setting, like Ray Charles; fortunately the resemblance was aural as well. He also made a smooth narrator in the companion piece, Sister Susie's Cinema, prettily inconsequential and described as an a capella doo-wop opera. The singing by 14 Karat Soul was much the same in both secular and spiritual settings, which may make a musicological point.

Also singing a capella, and not quite doo-wop, though related, were The Flying Pickets, who obviously want to be the Four Seasons, but are handicapped by being six. Never mind: their rock 'n' roll recreations are awesome, their occasional jokes barbed, and their appearance, especially their hats, superbly delinquent. Even better, slick and chic, were Raving Beauties (Anna Carteret, Barbara Rosenblatt, Sue Jones-Davies), whose In the Pink is a fast compilation of songs and poems, written for, or slanted for women.

Feminist cabaret trios (it's a tough city, even the traffic wardens go round in threes) are a feature this year, and the same songs criss-cross: one staple, "My Heart Belongs to Daddy" another. I heard Angela Holmes do both in Whores and Wars; she's very strong on Brecht, also on "Brother Can You Spare a Dime," which is every bit as good. More soberly, the Cherub Company presented John Spurling's chronicle of the various invasions of South America, Coming Ashore in Guadeloupe, respectable if cerebral. Mary Keegan is a promising actress.

On the Circuit: The Medieval Players, the best-educated company I have seen (they'd have to be, doing all their plays in Middle English) failing to make Gammer Gurton's Needle primarily hilarious, and not helping by doing it in masks. (Concept again.) The National Theatre of Brent, advertising Gotterdammerung but actually offering the entire Ring story with two actors, a pianist and help from the audience: fun except when they turn serious. A cabaret act, Bouncing Cheques, who play another cabaret act entertaining at a Hawaiian honeymoon hotel with hits of the Thirties, Forties and Fifties, spoof and musicality beautifully balanced. They were getting some lyrics wrong, but I put a stop to that.
