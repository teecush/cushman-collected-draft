Feb 15, 1981

Tom Among The Thieves

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Tom among the thieves

THEATRE

Robert Cushman on a Victorian thriller and a new play by Howard Barker

Tom Taylor, the Victorian playwright who edited Punch and wrote The Ticket-of-Leave Man (Cottesloe), seems to have been an optimistic soul.

The play revolves around a young Lancastrian who comes to London, falls among thieves, and is unjustly arrested for passing a forged banknote. He goes to jail for three years and emerges, not in the least embittered, indeed positively ennobled. 'This place,' he says in a letter to his sweetheart, 'has made a man of me.'

Besides this implausibility, the others that necessarily arise in this vintage thriller (the play is that, rather than a melodrama) are of small account and are anyway -- especially in this production -- greatly enjoyable. The tribulations of our hero Bob Brierly are, except on a personal level, difficult to take seriously; he was innocent anyway, though inclined to carelessness and champagne, and his difficulties in finding work when released early, for good behaviour, on a 'ticket of leave,' are put down neither to his own inadequacies nor to those of society; they are solely due to the machinations of the two crooks who first ruined him and want to keep him in their power.

Opposing them is a plainclothes detective called Hawkshaw, who has a private feud going with the more daring of the wrong'uns, Jem Dalton, known as The Tiger. Goodie and baddie alike are masters of disguise, capable of sitting out an entire first act on either side of a restaurant garden without recognising each other. The play provides us with six locales, abundantly peopled. I don't suppose Taylor had any particular intention of putting Victorian society on stage; he just happened to be writing for a theatre that, as a matter of course, employed a large number of actors.

We have few such establishments ourselves, and are the happier to see a large-scale production when we can get it. The director, Piers Haggard, manoeuvres the swells and day-trippers of Act One and the navvies drinking at the dockside pub (same proprietor, down in the world) in Act Four with delightful adroitness. Taylor gives them fruity lines to speak, even if some of the fruit is preserved. Dickens apparently selected him to dramatise A Tale of Two Cities, perhaps making him a gift of something he would otherwise have borrowed. In this play we have a character called St Evremond, nee Traddles; and a landlady who talks like Sairey Gamp gone respectable. What he didn't get from Dickens was moral complexity: any sense that the law might ever be wrong or respectability a mask.

As a television director, Mr Haggard has demonstrated an immaculate sense of style and rhythm: I'm thinking not just of Pennies from Heaven, but of a production years ago of Gershwin's Tip-Toes, kept masterfully on the edge of absurdity. He brings off the same trick here. There is a musical trio in attendance; and their comments -- sad, baleful or romantic -- provide a necessary comic distance. Often they play behind speeches which are, however, delivered with perfect seriousness: a wonderful counterpoint.

Paul Copley's performance as Bob, in its successive shows of weakness and strength, has a depth I would not have thought the play capable of supporting; simple from moment to moment, complex in effect, it is the equal of Roger Rees's Nicholas Nickleby. Michael Elphick as Hawkshaw (with his extraordinary ideas of secrecy) and Jack Shepherd as Dalton are allowed more latitude; but if their swagger is playful it is never ludicrous.

Mr Haggard has a stunning cast: Rebecca Saire as heroine, Harry Landis as second villain, Pat Heywood as loquacious granny, Keita Jayne as infant prodigal, and Tim Wylton as comic lovers (and minor theatricals, which seems to have been a Victorian rule), Anthony Langdon as publican, all in key. Robin Don's set, which frames the play without patronising it, supports the finest succession of stage pictures currently available in the metropolis.

*

Howard Barker's No End of Blame (Oxford Playhouse) is also, in several senses, a play of notable pictures. It begins on a World War I battlefield where a Hungarian soldier, Grigor, confronts a naked, terrified woman. He is drawing her. His friend Bela comes on and, being of a more practical temperament, tries to rape her; though, not being as practical as all that, he takes all his own clothes off first. This leads to the two men being arrested.

Bela is a cartoonist by vocation and after the war we find him and Grigor at art-school in Budapest. Bela tires of conventional standards and goes off to Moscow. It is a revolutionary thaw-period, but eventually he finds himself in trouble for producing unhelpfully critical work, and is most ungently rebuked by a committee of politicians and bureaucrats. (The contrasting scenes are well-handled and probably, as far as they go, just.)

After the war this, as it might be, Vicky is retired from, as it might be, the Evening Standard and replaced by, as it might be, Jak, who promises more laughs. Bela attempts suicide and is last seen in a wheelchair in a mental hospital. Next to him sits Grigor, who has periodically reappeared, and who proves disconcertingly capable of working magic. Bela, at first beyond caring, ends calling for a pencil.

I suspect that Mr Barker would not much approve of this effortfully resonant ending if it turned up (as it very well might) in a Peter Shaffer play. His works are difficult to summarise because they proceed by incidents. As far as I can judge, Bela is a permanent rebel with a disabling strain of weakness. (He tries in the first scene to shoot an officer, but can't go through with it.) 'I believe the cartoon to be the lowest -- and most important -- form of art,' he says for, I assume, his author.

He refuses to worship either high art or any political creed. He is caught between two credos. One is 'cartoons are not about me, they're about us, I don't like me'; the other, 'I can only draw what I see.' He is bad at relationships; but here I falter, because Mr Barker isn't good at them either. It is difficult to make much of Grigor or of the girl who accompanies the two of them. Paul Freeman plays him well, but leaves little mark. He is making, after all, the same statement in a succession of circumstances. I fancy that Mr Barker's roles are not actually very rewarding to actors: what is there to do with them once you have got your tongue round the ornate sentences? Most of the performers here get their chance to play the icy orator. One of the Russians talks of 'stretching our little fingers out to try and catch the swinging boot of history'; who knows whether this is parody? Bela's cartoons have been drawn by Gerald Scarfe and they are fierce.

Thomas Middleton's view of humanity was hardly rosier than Mr Barker's, but he seems to have agonised less over it; in his comedies, anyway. The greatest of them, A Chaste Maid in Cheapside, presents a Jacobean London divided into cuckolds, lechers, whores and, nastiest of all, Puritans; with a couple of true lovers tossed in to make a plot.

The Cherub company, in their production at Theatre Space, come near to stylising the play out of existence. They seem intelligent and, by and large, talented; but they are too keen to let us know that the play's society is corrupt. It is also fun: a fact that in the second act gets inescapably through to the Cherubs, to the great benefit of them, us and the play. This week No End of Blame is playing at the Arts Centre, Warwick University; A Chaste Maid in Cheapside at the Upstream Theatre Club, Short Street, SE1.
