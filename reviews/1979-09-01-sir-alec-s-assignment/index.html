Sep 1, 1979

Sir Alec's Assignment

Robert Cushman · Radio Times

Sir Alec's Assignment

THE MOMENT I walked into the lounge of the Connaught Hotel I spotted Alec Guinness. This is no great boast since I was supposed to be meeting him there for lunch anyway, but it was pleasant to prove to myself something I had always believed: first, that the invisible, anonymous Guinness of popular myth is indeed a myth. Even Kenneth Tynan has subscribed to it, declaring in his biography of Guinness that 'were he to commit a murder, I have no doubt that the number of false arrests following the circulation of his description would break all records'. This is possibly an unkind reflection on the capabilities of the police force, but it does little justice to the actor whose face is decently distinctive, whose voice has a totally individual sonority, and whose personality is unique.

The master-of-disguise legend may have started with his multiple appearance in the classic comedy film Kind Hearts and Coronets. However all the characters he played there were members of the same family. and it was the point of the joke that beneath their various beards, wrinkles and hairpieces they should all possess identical and immediately recognisable features. There was a time he says, when he used to hide behind false noses and spirit gum onstage, but this was in the 30s and 40s when all the rising young actors were doing it, chief among them Laurence Olivier and Ralph Richardson: (The apotheosis of nose-putty came of course with Olivier's Richard III.) Guinness is a quieter actor than either of them - perhaps that is how the legend grew.

Before taking on the role of George Smiley in Tinker, Tailor, Soldier, Spy he had done very little television. There was some, he said, in America many years ago, 'in the days of black and white stuff'. Nearer to home, and nearer to now, he can recall only about four appearances. This is not due to any stand-offishness on his part. 'I don't,' he says, need to be wooed to do anything.' Later in our conversation he amplified this by saying that he has always ' been a snap-decision person. If I start to dilly-dally about something it usually means I shouldn't do it.' The trouble with accepting television work has been the companies' habit of phoning up and asking '"Can you do this in two weeks' time?

" In films and the theatre one's life has usually been arranged a bit previous to that.' He admits, though, that he had more fundamental doubts about television as compared to the two older media: 'I always had a slight feeling that it provided the worst of both worlds. But Tinker, has changed my mind This was partly because it was all done on film anyway ('no sets, studio work') and on 16-millimetre 'None of those huge 35mm cameras, or those cameras with cables trailing all over the place.' And the feeling of freedom was decisively reinforced by the director, John Irvin, to whom Guinness paid repeated tribute. The shooting, brought industrial as well as the usual technical and artistic problems, and Guinness thought Irvin's control magnificent throughout.

'I'm sure he's a superb technician. And he was very understanding of the actors. He'd alter set-ups to enable us to move more naturally. Often in films you find yourself making false moves purely for technical reasons.' He'd be perfectly happy to play Smiley again if he could have the same director. There is a strong possibility of such a repeat performance. Smiley, of course, figures in several of John le Carré's novels, 'It's all very well for novelists to describe their characters' mannerisms; actors actually have to do them' and is in fact the central figure of the latest of them, still unpublished, but already - with Tinker, Tailor a rumoured success - being discussed for future film treatment.

Smiley is the spy as super-professional: not a James Bond dare-devil, more a Sherlock Holmes of the files and dossiers. An infinitely conscientious civil servant, he devotes himself to his work as a defence »> against the ravages of life with a chronically unfaithful wife. Tinker, Tailor has him broughtout of retirement to trace an enemy infiltrator in the department of which he was once the prize employee. Thereisa story currentthat le Carré actually had Guinness in his mind's eye when he created Smiley. The actor discounts this; and, between accepting the role and beginning to shoot it, he got to know the author well. Le Carré has personal experience of the world John Timbers he depicts, and, when he lent the Guinnesses his house in Cornwall, they felt they were really getting into the world of espionage.

Not that there were mysterious faces the windows or unexpected cars parked outside the door; it was just that everything seemed so electronic: *There was BBC 2 AUTUMN SEASON a battery of telecommunication things, all sorts of knobs to press - oh, and one door that was permanently locked. knew it led to his study but all the same. It is possible to rise from reading Tinker, Tailor impressed by Smiley's brain-power but with little sense of him as a physical presence. Something about the name George Smiley suggests to me a rotund untidiness (I always rather visualised Arthur Lowe in the role) and indeed Guinness put on a lot of weight to play the part, weight that he is now trying to lose.

He agrees that Smiley would be'untidy in his clothes but thinks that 'in the arrangement of things around him he would be very careful... meticulous. However it's all very well for novelists to describe their characters' mannerisms; There are three of them and Alleline. Sweat them, George. Tempt them, bully them - any damn thing' Toby Esterhase (Bernard Hepton), Bill Haydon (lan Richardson) Percy Alleline (Michael Aldridge) and Roy Bland (Terence Rigby)the all-powerful London Station. Once Smiley's colleagues and now the unknowing subjects of his scrutiny actors, when they actually have to do them, can find some things slightly inconsistent. The book endlessly gives descriptions of Smiley taking off his glasses and polishing them with the end of his silk tie.

But the story Guinness claims to have no favourites among his films, hardly to give them another thought once they're made takes place in wintertime, when ties are just not get-atable; there are pullovers in the way, or three-piece suits. I had to use a handkerchief instead, There is apparently one interior scene in which the silk tie routine is given its due before the cameras. We discussed Smilev over drinks (he had a Pimms) and Guinness over lunch (he had seafood crépes, followed6 raspberries and cream, some of which he generously transferred to me). More and more one is struck by the extraordinary qualities of that group of actors who came up in the classical theatre (mainly the Old Vie) of the 30s, and by the closeness of the links between them Guinnessis the youngest of them, and still talks admiringly of Gielgud as "the great enlightener' of Shakespearian verse. of acting with Olivier and Richardson in the legendary New Theatre seasons just after the war, of watching Michael Redgrave turn, at one crucial rehearsal of The Three Sisters, from a handsome leading man to a really fine actor and of the releasing effect of working for Tyrone Guthrie who "didn't know anything about acting as such but was a wonderful giver of the stage?

And of the 'great influence of-Edith Evans from the generation above, though in her heyday she was apparently very much the grande dame. Indeed, talking to him about his own career you continually find yourself discussing other actors. He claims to have no favourites among his films,hardly to give them another thought once they're made. I tried to +I want something new, although there are one or two classies P'd be amused to do? draw him out over Kind Hearts and Coronets, to my mind the best film ever made in Britain, but after paying tribute to its unusually literate script (whose wit, I may say, shone particularly through his own series of deliciously exact characterisations) he merely remarked that he had recently gone to see the grave in Sark of his co-star Dennis Price.

He is notoriously self-deprecatory, and aware of it;in fact, he is apt to be self-deprecatory about that, too. Recalling his legendary Fool to Olivier's Lear, he says. 'Even I couldn't pretend I wasn't successful-but that's mainly because I only played half the part. Larry was directing and he said "A lot of it's very boring, old dear," andcut it.' He's been an actor now for 45 years, and did the classics solidly for the first 15 of them. He's now virtually abandoned them. I want somethingnew. There are one or two classics I'd be amused to do.... but in a revival there's always a feeling that you're there to show off, you're saying now I'ul do this one...

Lear or Macbeth or whatever But I think Lear must be playedby youngish man,if onlyso he'll have enough strength to swing Cordelia around. Once you're 50 they're out. He admits to *68 or something'. I suspect that if Guinness seems to harp on what he can't do, it is because he is very sure within himself of what he can: a matter, as he says, of 'recognising one's own limitations and saying to hell with that'. Along with this goes a shrewd estimate of his own worth in the commercial market; his well-publicised two per cent of Star Wars has left him, he says, pretty flush' and free of any immediate necessity to work, but I doubt if he has really worried very much about that for years.

He has divided his career between screen and stage more carefúlly than 1 had suspected.a When I frst started taking an interest-in the-50s, I thought of him as an immovable pillar of the British film industry (along with Jack Hawkins) and hardly associated him with the theatre at all. In fact, he has never let a year go by without a stage appearance; and in the last few years, in plays by Sim on Gray and Alan Bennett and in adaptations of Swift and Ivy Compton-Burnett, he has been one of the few-regular ornaments of an increasingly. dispirited West End. He always projects a keenly amused moral intelligence. He has long since forsworn heavy disguise, only wearing a wig so that the lights don't beam too dazzlingly off his bald head.

He practises economy - 'I think the less I do the better'-though he characteristically adds, think maybe I've gone too far in paring things down? And then ne makes it more complicated Dilys Powell gave me a notice in a film, saying something like how nice to see him trying again. I thought, to hell with that, if you can see me trying, I'm not doing it well. You like it because I don't seem to be trying' He quotes T. S. Eliot, favourite writer, to the effeet that every new sentence is a struggle, you know you're not I I think one gets disillusioned with oneself. Irealise that I'm not as passtonate about aeting? going to get it quite right but you have to go ahead and try, and applies it to acting.

The inference is that you may occasionally surprise yourself by how well you have done it. Occasionally his quiet urbanity suggests a certain weariness but he denies that he's in any way disillusioned with acting. I think one gets disillusioned with oneself. I realise that I'm not as blinkered and passionate about it any more.' But the lure of the job remains. His Smiley should be fascinating.
