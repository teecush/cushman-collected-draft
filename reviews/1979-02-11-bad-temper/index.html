Feb 11, 1979

Bad Temper

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Bad temper

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'A Fair Quarrel' at the National.

THE National Theatre continues its season of plays with turbulent titles. Strife, Betrayal, and The World Turned Upside Down are succeeded by A Fair Quarrel (Olivier). No doubt this bellicosity suits the times, but it reflects all previous ages as well. In the entire world repertoire the only beatifically named play that comes to mind is Aristophanes's Peace, a very long time ago.

Otherwise the world's drama is crammed with bad temper. There are three courses of it in A Fair Quarrel, which comes from Middleton and Rowley, the Jacobean firm who gave you The Changeling. Critics have pronounced that its separate plots are bound together by their concern with codes of behaviour under strain. It is difficult to imagine any story that wouldn't somewhere support that interpretation, but we may give the scholars two out of three.

Plot A, which is about duelling, is indeed parodied by Plot C, which deals with the 'roaring boys' who made a cult out of rowdiness and in one scene even give lessons in it. Plot B, though, leads a discrete, if not discreet, existence and is concerned with a girl courted against her will. All these juxtapositions involve the authors in some odd time-keeping; a heroine goes through the greater part of pregnancy, and is brought to bed, while a hero is strolling back from the field of honour. 'No wonder his mother worries.'

Her honour, I should explain, is itself in question. Young Captain Ager is called by his colonel a 'son of a whore' and, as was expected, challenges him immediately. Lady Ager, not wanting to see her boy carved up, blushingly (and quite falsely) admits to having slipped in her time, and the captain sets off prepared to apologise. His antagonist promptly calls him a coward, the captain's reflexes are reawakened and the fight is on again.

The Jacobeans, while regarding such behaviour as something of a joke and more of a public nuisance, would still have found it understandable. To us, unless we are properly instructed, it is only a joke; and the only time when a perplexed first-night audience felt the ground firm beneath its feet was when it could laugh at Nicky Henson's Captain striking noble attitudes.

William Gaskill's production is very clear; you can hear most of the words and the actors seem unusually convinced of what they mean. They also know what the issues are; to a man they jump whenever their reputations are called in question. But though clarity is an essential basis for a revival, it is not enough and this play -- no masterwork, but an entertaining genre-piece -- needs filling out. Some of the social detail that Mr Gaskill used to supply in Restoration comedy might be helpful, along with actions that explored and projected the characters' passions instead of just tastefully indicating them. The actors come mainly from Joint Stock (and are therefore, according to current doctrine, beyond criticism), but for this play, on this stage, they look under-powered.

The stage is part of the trouble -- this is another main house production with its heart in the studio. On this occasion, the back of the Olivier platform has been given over to the audience, which may cut down on the stage's intimidating vastness, but makes the house itself the biggest arena theatre east of Hollywood Bowl. The acting area is a timbered rectangle with a hole in its middle: a large trapdoor which the actors, thus democratically prevented from taking the centre of the stage, warily circumnavigate.

Those who make the strongest impression are those previously in residence: Mr Henson, and Dermot Crowley as a creepy physician. Among the insurgents, Fred Pearson has a good swagger as the Colonel and Marjorie Yates a nicely judged vulgarity as a bawd. I honestly believe plays like A Fair Quarrel should be revived, and I realise that I have not greatly assisted their chances this morning. But they can be done.

*

The staging of Tommy (Queen's) reopens some questions which one might have thought buried. It is boldly billed as a 'rock musical' and is one of the few of its kind to deserve the name. Hence of course its failure; rock cannot co-exist with drama, since the first depends on repetition and the second on variety. Whenever a supposed rock-score has been praised in the theatre it has been for its 'eclecticism': i.e., it has got away from rock as far and often as possible. (This has often been the case with pop music outside the theatre as well.)

But Tommy is sanctified as The Who's masterwork, a hit on records and filmed by Ken Russell (they deserved each other), so we get it now with its rhythms crushingly unvaried, its decibels ringing and Pete Townshend's atrocious lyrics generally inaudible -- which does nothing for the plot but something for the sensibilities. (Mr Townshend is not, as you might think, proudly indifferent to rhyme; when he can think up 'sorrow-tomorrow' couplets, however lame, he shoves it in.)

It is not altogether the authors' fault; Tommy, after all, wasn't written for the stage. To put it there is brutally (the right word) to expose its masochistic narcissism: here is the hero as deaf, dumb and blind boy, here he is as teenage idol, and here finally as crucified star. Filling in the visual details can't be easy; apart from some spectacular laser-work, this production settles for third-hand eroticism. If a couple of nurses have to appear they naturally flash their suspenders.

To show you how original it plans to be, the show begins by having its cast clamber down scaffolding in their leotards while clouds float across the back-projected sky. Things that on the record were just meretriciously menacing are here clumsily nasty; his relatives' sexual assaults on Tommy are played for laughs. (Bob Grant makes Uncle Ernie a perverted Bruce Forsyth.) Allan Love mimes quite touchingly in the opening scenes; but when his faculties are restored he has to grab a hand-mike just like everybody else. Character conflict in those constricted circumstances is hardly possible. Listen, people, nobody in America writes rock scores for the theatre any more, and they know far more about musicals than you.

*

It is a relief to turn to Talent (ICA), a cheeky title but something for the sensibilities. Victoria Wood, as author and performer, fully justifies. She is already the best songwriter we have, the best refreshing melodist to wry, funny, concrete lyrics, and she may have it in her to be our best female playwright (if we are to encourage such distinctions). Her present piece, though short, has perhaps too many personal memories pushed into it; but her dialogue lives and breathes, and though she can be sour -- she writes of northern working-girls with dead-end prospects -- she is resilient, and accompanies sexy, ambitious Julie, would-be singer, to a club talent contest.

As the night in the dismal dressing-room proceeds, Julie learns that she has nothing to hope for, while Maureen's prospects, briefly expanding, subside as quickly. Hazel Clyne, brittle and edgy, gets both Julie's selfishness and her vulnerability. The show is an object-lesson in making upbeat theatre out of downbeat material without ever falsifying it.
