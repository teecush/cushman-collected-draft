Sep 29, 1974

Lament For A 'Little Night Music'

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Lament for a 'Little Night Music'

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN casts a gloomy eye over the commercial theatre scene.

AFTER three weeks away I glance casually down the theatre lists and am horrified. I have never felt inclined to storm at the endless succession of West End farcical comedies; I see them one at a time, shrug, forget, and go on to other things. But I must acknowledge that the plays themselves refuse to follow my amiable example; they stay around, accumulate, taking up space.

The unsubsidised theatre currently offers a few things that are recommendable: two first-class revivals, a mass of plays by Alan Ayckbourn. There are also a couple of borderline musicals; "Billy" at any rate sleek, "The Good Companions" at any rate engaging. And with these modest virtues it seems we shall have to live, this week having brought the gloomy news that the London production of "A Little Night Music" has been cancelled.

The management were probably right in thinking that this musical would lose money; if it would be depressing to think that we are currently being given the theatre we deserve, it is hardly less so to reflect that we are getting the only theatre we can afford. The loss of "A Little Night Music" (as of its predecessor "Follies," a magnificent piece of stagecraft which has never even been announced for London) deprives us of the work of major talents: of Harold Prince, a fine if uneven director; of the superlative designer Boris Aronson; of Glynis Johns, whose frayed, affecting singing of "Send in the Clowns" could hardly be imagined by anyone who has not heard the song in context; and of Stephen Sondheim, whose music, and above all his lyrics, proclaim him the most important writer now active in the American theatre. No playwright can match him for wit, insight, irony or felicity of expression. Set in a high-comedy Sweden (and based on an Ingmar Bergman film), the show lacks the Manhattan bite of "Company" or "Follies"; an intermittent, uncomfortable sense of every one on their best behaviour trying to be stylish and European communicated itself to several of the performers in New York. For this very reason, though, it would have been fascinating to see it played by a British cast. Perhaps it is time for musicals (the few good ones) to move out of the private sector; "Follies" would have looked well in a World Theatre Season, and "A Little Night Music" might even be the musical that the National Theatre have coquetted so long with producing.

Why not? Over at the Aldwych bursts of good song have much enlivened the Royal Shakespeare productions of "The Bewitched" and "Travesties." Indeed, songwriters and musicians have contributed much to the London stage this year. "Cole" at the Mermaid is more or less of a delight; the Beatles play at the Lyric is (I realise with surprise) almost the best new play around, fragmentary but rueful, tough and acidulous like the music it celebrates. At the Mayfair--unless he has radically changed his act since I saw it a couple of years ago--John Gould is providing understated, bespectacled fun at and around a piano; and I believe all the good that I have heard of Sammy Cahn. And, saving the face of the West End this week, there is show business.

More or less to the tune (omnipresent at the moment) of "That's Entertainment" a toothy quintet called Dallas Boys, closing the first half of The Ken Dodd Laughter Show (Palladium), announce that "the stage is the place to see a real live show," thereby giving the lie to the rest of their act, which is a celebration of television. Presumably the aim is to make the audience feel at home--though home is precisely where they have paid a great deal of money not to be. Nobody, however, could feel at home in Mr Dodd's outlandish presence; we feel at ease (there's a phrase he could do something with), but that is quite different.

For a man who tells so many jokes (and never a one mistimed) he is surprisingly independent of the telly as a source of material. Nor does he merely bombard us with bad jokes, hoping that they and he will escape in the confusion. Many of his observations are original, perhaps profound; few others, confronted by a real and ham pie, would gaze at the egg in the middle and muse (it is of necessity a very quick muse) on the cleverness of some hens. Two surprising facts about Mr Dodd: he has not, except on his frequent ascents into falsetto, a very individual voice and, alone among our major clowns, his act is free of hidden aggression. (There is plenty of overt aggression, but it is harmless.) He seems very good-natured, free of the desperation of Max Wall, the scorn of Frankie Howerd or the off-hand humiliation that Morecambe inflicts on Wise. These are all comics whom I prefer, but this week at the Palladium (first house Wednesday, which is all I have so far seen) Mr Dodd would not have missed.

The Turning Point (Duke of York's) I saw, and the second half I actually did. I was driven away by the embarrassment of hearing the protagonist continually exclaim how stock the situation is. There is, as if the admission made them any better. The author, Francoise Dorin, is French, but the play has been anglicised. (One character is called Roderick Timms-Taylor.) David Tomlinson directs, and plays--seemingly in one continuous woebegone sentence--the leading role. He more or less survives, but his colleagues can only beat the empty air. The play concerns the conflict between boulevard drama, of whose decadence it is itself an uncomfortable specimen, and an avant-garde of which it seems spectacularly ignorant.

I particularly regretted this last deficiency, since a progressive bill at the Theatre at New End had put me in the mood for some good reactionary satire. We are given two pieces by Copi, an Argentinian playwright living in France. The curtain-raiser, The Four Twins, was done in Paris last year by Jorge Lavelli; it looked like spectacular rubbish, but in Hampstead the spectacle has departed. The main piece, Eva Peron, is an acrid cartoon of the lady's last day. It contains one arresting image--Eva, dying of cancer, is played by a man--and one effective twist. These are enough to stimulate interest in the playwright, but not in the play. Hatred might be able to fuel an entire evening, but contempt--of which this one is composed--exhausts itself in 10 minutes. Steven Darnell's direction is spruce; Cornelius Garnett is the best of the turns, and Murray Salem a haughty, muscular Eva.
