The Making Of Alec McCowen

Robert Cushman · The Observer Magazine

The making of Alec McCowen

by Robert Cushman

IN 1959 Alec McCowen went to play his first season at the Old Vic. He was 34, and was invariably, almost officially, referred to as "one of our most promising young actors". This reputation had been gained entirely in modern plays, and most recently in television, where he had played 18 parts in two years. He had virtually never appeared in the classics, had done hardly any Shakespeare. Within six months of joining the company he was the most accomplished actor in it, and critics were making respectful reference to his speaking of the verse.

Last year he returned to the Old Vic, appearing for the first time as a member of the National Theatre. It was hard to believe that the National could ever have got on without him. He is probably the most skilful actor of his generation.

Having said that, I have to admit that I am not too sure (and he couldn't tell me) quite which is his generation, theatrically. He fits in somewhere after Paul Scofield but before Albert Finney. Though he made his name in the 1950s he appeared only once in that decade's particular shrine, the Royal Court. He was sufficiently in tune with the zeitgeist to play Jimmy Porter in what he describes as "the last of the first lot of revivals of 'Look Back in Anger'." That production was directed by John Dexter, and the two didn't get on. Times and personalities have changed: Dexter directed McCowen's last two London performances--in The Misanthrope and Equus at the National--and he is also responsible for Pygmalion, in which McCowen is due to open at the Albery on Wednesday.

McCowen's peculiar position--on his own in a one-man generation--reflects the pattern of his career. He entered RADA at 16 ("far too young"). Then, without finishing his course, he went into repertory (at Macclesfield, Llandudno, York) full of what he recalls (again deprecatingly) as "the enthusiasm of youth", a confidence which inevitably evaporated by the time its possessor was 30. But the enthusiasm must have co-existed with considerable insecurity since it was, as much as anything, a mania for disguise. "For the first five, maybe 10 years, I was using acting as a means of disguising my personality. I was happier the more make-up I wore." His ultimate achievement here (and it must have taken nerve as well as nervousness to bring it off) came in York when at the age of 19 he essayed the title role in a popular piece of Spanish sentiment called A Hundred Years Old. The company's resident old man (aged 75) played his son--"He was furious."

So when he began playing characters of his own age he felt "terribly naked". Ironically his first real London success, in Roger Macdougall's Escapade, came in the role of a schoolboy. He was then 27, and there then ensued 10 years as one of the theatre's most highly regarded juveniles. He refers to it as my "neurotic young man period". He was forever turning up on television as comically (occasionally tragically) shy youths, dangerously out of their accustomed depth. The first time I saw him on stage was at the Edinburgh Festival of 1958, at which he played the wastrel son of T. S. Eliot's Elder Statesman turning on his heel and demanding "What is my inheritance?"

The physical impact of that moment was electric (remembered, I think, by everyone who saw it at Edinburgh, or later at the Cambridge Theatre in London) and it exemplified some of the approach to acting that McCowen, by now soberly acclimatised to the theatre just as a way of making a living, had begun to work out for himself. "Ideally, in a performance the actor shouldn't know what he's like but what the character does. One is easily led into descriptive acting. You come on and say 'I am like this.' But really what you do should add up to what you're like."

The man most responsible for guiding him along this path was the actor-director Vivian Matalon (best known for his work as director of the Hampstead Theatre Club), who had himself been greatly influenced by working and studying in America. There has always been a strong transatlantic pull on McCowen's work. As far back as 1948 he visited New York (on holiday from a spell of repertory in Newfoundland) and saw A Streetcar Named Desire, which he describes today as "an eye-opening experience; wonderful and comforting. I have always loved naturalism, and it made me realise that what I'd seen in the West End was only stylised naturalism; if someone wanted to indicate worry he put his forefinger and thumb to the bridge of his nose. It was often brilliant, but not very human. When Brando came on in Streetcar he was so completely natural, I remember blushing. I thought for a moment that a beautiful stage-hand had wandered on by mistake."

The next major influence was also American: the great comedienne Ruth Gordon, with whom he worked in 1955 in Thornton Wilder's The Matchmaker. "She was the most inventive and dedicated actress I'd worked with up to that time. She was alive every night--she recreated the performance every night. I was bewildered by how she did it." Somehow, although they were in the same show, they never seemed to meet, so in the end McCowen wrote her a fan letter, asking her how she worked this nightly miracle. "We started a correspondence. She said the miracle was getting the job. After that it was work." What he finally learnt from her was the secret of comedy playing lay in "intensity of belief".

His first success at the Old Vic was in comedy--Touchstone in As You Like It--supposedly the unfunniest of Shakespeare's clowns, a supposition he decisively shattered, winning an award for the best supporting performance of the year, and curing himself of "the awful fear that you had to be a different kind of actor to play Shakespeare".

He stayed at the Vic for two years, playing Mercutio in the famous Zeffirelli Romeo and Juliet, Malvolio and the best Richard II I have seen. Then, after a slump, during which he appeared in two infinitesimal West End runs (a revue, and a play by Francoise Sagan), he went to the Royal Shakespeare Company. He played in two enormously successful productions--The Comedy of Errors and Peter Brook's King Lear--which kept him busy (at Stratford, in London, and on a world tour) for 18 months. He worked for the first time with Diana Rigg, who subsequently played opposite him in The Misanthrope at the National and is now his Eliza in Pygmalion. But it was not a very happy time for him. Playing the Fool to Scofield's Lear he drew, as he often does, on his own childhood: in this case of "waiting when I was five or six years old for my father to come home cross, wanting to relax him, and being physically frightened of him."

The result was a poignant, and supremely intelligent performance, but he felt that his "ultimate in character juvenile parts" had brought him to end of the road. "I couldn't go on trading on my juvenile appearance. I felt very played out and worried." His solution was to go out and buy a grey wig. "I played in that grey wig and glasses for the next three plays." The third of them was Hadrian the Seventh, a dramatisation of Baron Corvo's novel of the bitter, arrogant young man who became Pope. For McCowen, it was definitely third time lucky.

When offered the play he was at his lowest ebb, and accepted it without any great belief in its possibilities. He played it first in Birmingham, where it did terrible business. Nonetheless a London run was arranged: six weeks at the Mermaid. McCowen, by now thoroughly despondent, wondered whether it would not be better to settle for four.

He said at the time that he read the reviews, the morning after the Mermaid opening, "with a mixture of astonishment and pleasure. I began to have the guilty feeling that I'd written them myself." It became fashionable to say that, fine though the performance was, he had done many other things just as good to less applause. But that Mermaid opening was really memorable. It was exhilarating to see an actor rising so fully and surely to each successive climax (it's certainly one of the showiest parts in recent drama).

What difference did Hadrian finally make to him? "By giving me box-office success it stopped me chasing after it. And people started sending me scripts second or third rather than tenth on the list."

There were some other consequences. He found himself making a dreadful movie with Charlton Heston, called Master of the Islands, "with so much spirit gum on I couldn't speak. The character had to be 72; someone had said 'Get the Pope'." It made a change from the usual experience of an actor being refused the part of a 33-year-old because he was only 23.

In 1970, having played Hadrian in New York, McCowen returned to Birmingham to play a controversial Hamlet; one would have expected him to be a worrying if not exactly a gloomy Dane, but his performance was generally found to be surprisingly extrovert. He himself says that he was too old for the part, feeling that for a mature man to go on at his mother like that (why shouldn't she marry whomever she wants?) is fairly insufferable.

He returned to general favour (and incidentally to the Royal Court) as the word-obsessed hero of Christopher Hampton's The Philanthropist, trying desperately to lead a sheltered life; a much enriched version of the shy young men of his earlier days. Hampton deliberately planned this character to be the obverse of Alceste in Moliere's Misanthrope so it was logical that McCowen should eventually find himself at the National giving a volcanic account of Alceste himself. At the heart of the performance as of the play was a man's fury, not with society, but against himself for loving a woman he partially despises. He found Alceste's great outburst against Celimene (Diana Rigg) "as demanding as anything in drama--to come right in with that degree of passion. There was an old fire-bucket in the rehearsal room, and one day I gave it a great kick just before going on. John Dexter seemed to think that was just what the scene needed."

After this came one of his best performances ever as the greying psychiatrist in Peter Shaffer's Equus, from his point of view a problem of restraint. "My instincts are always to have a go, to act. But this character's mostly re-acting. I had to resist the temptation to colour the questions he asks." But being himself a conscientious workman McCowen has always seemed able to illuminate the work-processes of his characters; he emerged in Equus as a supreme exponent of the professional man in crisis. Again one savoured his ability to appear watchful, amused, detached, without ever being uninvolved. And still, beneath the tiredness and the coping with the unaccustomed brisk moustache, there was a trace of ineradicable boyishness. His face is slightly puffier than it used to be, but it is still possible to think--or even unguardedly to write--of him as "one of our best (not promising: that's been redeemed) younger actors". He's growing a beard for Pygmalion: perhaps that will finally age him.

The instinct to act is not confined to the stage. He is alert and responsive in conversation but he blazes into life when he gets out of his chair to illustrate a technical point, or to tell a story. If the story allows him to assume an American accent, so much the better; when I met him he had just returned from a solitary holiday discovering Oregon and was full of it. (Why Oregon? He was a bit vague; I think he liked the name.) On a smaller scale his favourite therapy when he needs to get away from the theatre is to get into a car and drive somewhere unlikely.

For the most part though he remains "steeped in the theatre; he only knows people in the theatre". So says one of his closest friends, the actress Patience Collier, who adds--though he himself fervently denies it--that "he hates emotional involvement. He likes to be fond of people--up to a limit. I couldn't understand when I met him--12 years ago--how anyone could be so un-vain. He used to dress terribly." (Miss Collier takes some credit for having partially cured him of that.) "He can be very generous but he always says he has a terribly mean, puritanical streak. He used to have just one 15-watt uncovered light bulb in his bedroom. Of course that's changed since he started making money."

The money has largely come from his two biggest film roles, in George Cukor's Travels with My Aunt and Alfred Hitchcock's Frenzy, and some of it has gone on a wholesale redecoration of his bachelor flat, several flights up in Notting Hill Gate. His instructions to the decorators are said to have been "You can do what you like with it--only don't ask me." Outside the theatre, says Miss Collier, "he doesn't know about personal tastes and wishes. He just about knows if he doesn't like something." She adds that he plays the piano "divinely" and displays great tact and skill in persuading unlikely ladies to sing along with him. (His selections always begin with Jerome Kern's "I'm Old Fashioned": conceivably a musical credo, certainly an assertion of good taste.)

Someone comes back to talking with him about performers, not necessarily straight actors. "I've found more inspiration and enjoyment from music-hall and from entertainers than from straight theatre; I learnt most from Sid Field, Max Miller, Jack Benny--because of their magical capacity to transmit thought from the stage to the audience. To remember Max Miller coaxing an audience to think the worst and then changing the subject is marvellous; or to watch Benny thinking."

Interviewed on the air in America, McCowen once described Benny as his favourite actor. The word spread and Benny, declaring that it had brought him $10,000 worth of publicity, made McCowen a present of a gold money-clip stamped with the comedian's unmistakable features. (Whether this confirms or belies Benny's own celebrated "puritanical streak" is a nice question.) So, standing on a bookcase filled with playscripts and surrounded by posters, there it is. Flanked majestically by two Evening Standard Drama Awards (for Hadrian VII and for The Misanthrope), a tribute from one professional to another: Alec McCowen's Benny Award.
