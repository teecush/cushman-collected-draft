Oct 10, 1976

In Triumph

Robert Cushman · The Observer

In triumph

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the opening of the Olivier Theatre

WE are owed three National Theatres and last week, after much bilking, the second and largest instalment of the debt was redeemed. The Olivier Theatre opened in grand style.

I mean that quite literally; few playwrights have cultivated grandeur as assiduously as Marlowe in Tamburlaine the Great. Since it is written in two parts (here performed together in a trifling four and a half hours) and covers a deal of time and space, it qualifies in our usual loose definition as an epic. But it will satisfy sterner criteria as well; it is as close to Homer as to Hollywood.

It is an inordinately regal play. Tamburlaine's great celebration of ambition has its climax, 'The sweet fruition of an earthly crown,' and Marlowe's dramaturgy shows how far he endorsed his hero's sentiments. To be allowed a speech of any length in this play it is necessary to be a king, or at the least a captive princess. The only character allowed to break this rule is Tamburlaine himself, who, in his earliest days as a Scythian bandit, reels off a couple of arias which win him, at two blows, a fiancee and a lieutenant-in-chief.

Plainly, if Marlovian decorum is to be kept, such a man is born to be king, and so it proves. Reaching the Persian throne at the end of Act Two, he remains there until the penultimate speech of Act Ten. (Two parts, remember.) All that stands between him and the play's last words is death, the only comer he cannot defeat.

The intervening action has shown him defeating all other challengers. He is particularly successful against the Turks, whose Emperor, Bajazeth, he captures and humiliates in Part One. In Part Two his main opponent is Bajazeth's son, Callapine, who continually escapes him, though leaving behind a train of tormented allies.

Bajazeth is starved, climbed over (literally) and kept in a cage, against whose bars he finally brains himself. This shows us Tamburlaine in a comparatively genial mood, like a schoolboy realising his wildest bullying dreams. In Part Two a certain sourness sets in; when the captive kings he has harnessed to his chariot prove unable to draw him more than 20 miles a day he sounds genuinely disappointed in them: disappointed, too, that the game is proving less fun than it promised to be.

Marlowe was 23 when he wrote the play, and English drama comparably young; at a later stage of development such enthusiastic cruelty would be intolerable. Here it is seductive; the play draws you into Tamburlaine's ruthlessness, makes you one with it. You find yourself hoping that he will remain invincible: you do not want so perfect a pattern of victory to be spoiled.

You do not even want him to be merciful, and spare those he has threatened with destruction; if he did that he would break his word and Tamburlaine is pre-eminently truthful. His main complaint against Bajazeth is that 'Turks are full of brags and menace more than they can well perform.' Tamburlaine performs all that he menaces; you might even call him chivalrous, since he never strikes without warning. Indeed, if his adversaries would only listen to him and surrender or go home we could go home too; there would be no play.

Only two things can halt Tamburlaine: love and death. Both are once-in-a-lifetime experiences (since as regards the one he is that kind of guy and as regards the other we all are), so the course of most of the play is even, almost soothing. The spaciousness of the full text wards off the tedium that an abridged version, with the incidents jumping upon one another's backs, might produce.

The main acting-area of the Olivier is a wide ring crowned by a circular lighting-grid; the audience sit in an arc. Peter Hall has grouped his actors to reflect these patterns; though there are 51 names in the cast, the stage is never crowded until the curtain-call. Ranks of golden mannequins represent armies, waiting in the background while their generals harangue one another.

The staging is fluent, logical and very simple; the set, a huge pair of dark sliding doors, with deep vistas behind. Battles are represented by percussive music and widening red spotlights; red takes over as the evening progresses and throats are graphically cut. Actors unexpectedly involve the audience: 'and so would you, my masters, would you not,' says Tamburlaine to us of his desire to be king, though in the text he might be addressing his followers.

Marlowe's drama, says Mr Hall, stems from the morality tradition: hence the stylisation. I wonder, though, if Marlowe thought of it as stylisation, or if he thought of it at all; it may have been his notion of naturalism. I imagine he wanted his horrors rough and hearty; here the carthorse-kings (including the saintly Robert Eddison) are given crowns of thorns and devotional poses. But I do not think you can make Tamburlaine, or Marlowe himself, into Antichrist.

Marlowe's recorded views on Jesus are similar to Jens Thorsen's, but his plays are surprisingly orthodox; his attitude may have been dislike rather than disbelief. There is a faithless Christian in Tamburlaine, but he is punished and Christ is accorded the credit. Tamburlaine is partial to Christians and implacable against the Turks who persecute them. Himself a Muhammadan, he finally rejects his prophet for 'The God that sits in heaven, if any god. For he is God alone and none but he.'

I don't want to push it; Marlowe is capable of expressing any number of views, and barely bothers to reconcile them. When he is not partaking in Tamburlaine's sadism, he can let other characters condemn it in unobjectionable compassionate terms before returning with added relish. One character--Tamburlaine's bride--gives voice to both feelings indiscriminately; Susan Fleetwood tries hard for consistency, but it is wasted effort.

Albert Finney brilliantly encompasses the gamester in Tamburlaine, the blazing eyes and more blazing cheek, takes full advantage of the brief, friendly descents into prose and drives resourcefully through hundreds of end-stopped iambics; only as the hundreds mount into thousands does his grip slacken. Love and death defeat him, too, both agony and ecstasy eluding him. He is a springy, earthbound performance, but I do not know who could better it.

Once again he is matched against Denis Quilley, who doubled Claudius and the Ghost in Hamlet, and is now, as Bajazeth and Callapine, again his own relation. He rides the verse finely, but is miscast: too likeable, for all his booming, for the old Turk and too old for the young one. If Callapine is Tamburlaine's contemporary, the tension of their relationship vanishes; Marlowe was better at relationships than Mr Hall will allow.

Barbara Jefford goes mad in a split second, the right way to do it; Philip Locke gets every sympathy for the King of Persia, a rational coward. Six Virgins of Damascus in Part One are Turkish Concubines in Part Two: the way of all flesh.

Tamburlaine, true to form, has annihilated the week's opposition. Romeo and Juliet (Shaw) was, at the preview I attended, unusually bad, except for the fights, the skeletons in the churchyard, and some stabbing moments from Angela Pleasence. Yahoo (Queen's), a Swift biography-anthology?, offers Alec Guinness on his knees as the King of Lilliput for a blissful couple of minutes, fear popping in his eyes and indignation crinkling in his lips; but erect for a good deal longer and as blandly Hibernian as Bing Crosby singing 'Galway Bay.' Best of the bunch is Scribes (Greenwich) by Barrie Keeffe, a naturalistic piece about a newspaper before, during and after a strike; in the way of soap-opera, it grows on you and is jauntily acted.
