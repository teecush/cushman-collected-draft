Comic Cuts

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Comic cuts

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

I HAVE to go down on my knees, and maybe my hands as well.

When Rowan Atkinson first appeared in a show called Beyond a Joke I jumped on him, giving him credit for timing and an intimidating appearance, but not much else. He seemed to be doing more than he or the audience could cope with. A more limited sight of him in the Amnesty International show changed my mind, television of course assisted the process, and after seeing Rowan Atkinson in Revue (Globe) my capitulation is complete.

Mr Atkinson has at least two funny voices: one high, pinched and unaggressively demented, the other languid and incipiently sadistic. Neither, I feel fairly sure, is his own. This is one of the strangest things about him; after an evening in his company you emerge not knowing what he really sounds like, or indeed without much personal sense of him at all. He comes at you as a set of gifts.

He's a cult, but he never trades on it. He works. His stage-hand, failing to save a show when the star goes sick, is marvellously surreal, a one-man collage of earnestly mouthed routines. This of course is done in his dim persona. The smart one is most famously deployed in his sour schoolmaster, a derivative routine that continues to crease me up.

It reaches logical fulfilment in his Devil, obviously bored with the job and a little revolted by it, extending apologetic welcomes to Christians (`I'm afraid the Jews were right'), atheists (`you must be feeling a bunch of right Charlies') and Americans (God sends special regrets to the Mormons who, he realises, put in a lot of work). Mr Atkinson also gives accurate impersonations of the different kinds of Shakespearian messenger and finally dazzles as a newsreader for the deaf, supplying graphic physical commentary on everything including the House of Commons (actually commentary isn't the point of bestiality), Margaret Thatcher (a shake of the hand, a knee to the groin) and Willie Whitelaw (each component part of the name spelt out). If there is a funnier act around than this last one I would like it brought to me. Now.

*

Stories about prostitutes used to be defended in court on the grounds of their redeeming sociological value. The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas (Drury Lane) is brimming with sociological value though not much of it relates to whoredom. Based on a true story (I mean it was printed in Playboy), this musical tells of what seems a spectacularly inoffensive sportin' house closed down by a coalition of local interests, i.e. a combination of prurience and cowardice.

A television crusader reveals to an amazingly startled public that `Texas has a whorehouse in it' and then, proud of his scoop, hounds the politicians into acting on it, even raiding the joint on Thanksgiving when it's given over to the entertainment of a winning college football team, for whom a local Senator is standing treat. He declares that he was planted there by Communists, and from then there can be no turning back. The case goes to the buck-passing Governor, characterised in a diverting song-and-dance called `The Sidestep.' The sheriff, hating himself and his superiors, is forced to foreclose on his old friend and possible flame, the brothel-keeper Miss Mona.

One of the fascinations of the piece is its eye for hypocrisy. There is no great public outcry, and certainly the Press aren't fooled. But it's in too many people's interests to pretend otherwise, and television is unstoppable. The show provides a sharp, entertaining look at the tangles of power (there's a mayor in there too somewhere) and a winning, convincing picture--not that I've ever been to Texas--of a small township with the coffee shop as its second favourite rendezvous.

Credulity only stops short at the whorehouse itself, known as the Chicken Ranch and run by Miss Mona as a home from home with never a thought of exploitation. She is salty (`I'll be quick to hand you a sandwich and a road-map' is her form of dismissal) and compassionate (`some pimp beat up on you?' she asks a damaged new recruit). All right, accept all that; then you are faced with the show's periodic excursions into raunchiness. The footballers' lustful chant has point as satire, but it's prolonged to the point of grossness (and incidentally, given standard chorus-boy casting, is none too credible). That element overpowered the show in New York, where I hated it (this seems to be recantation week) and it sops goodwill here.

Nobody is going to give this show marks for neatness; the initial theme--the adventures of two new girls--is quickly dropped, and songs are arbitrarily inserted. A waitress's lament, a black maid's exultation, do nothing for the story, though something for atmosphere and celebration respectively. (The two singers--Sheila Brand and Miquel Brown--are excellent). Most of Carol Hall's score is attractive; country works much better in the theatre than rock, and is artfully blended with Broadway bittersweet in the girls' goodbye number, Hard Candy Christmas.

The direction by Peter Masterson and Tommy Tune is smooth. Of the two imported leads Henderson Forsythe is a fine spitting sheriff and Carlin Glynn--sitting doing her accounts in a wicker wheelchair wearing off-the-shoulder red and spectacles--a stunning Mona. Against all augury the show seems right for Drury Lane; it should be--and I mean this as commendation--really great for the coach-parties.

*

Richard II (Young Vic) is a puzzle. The stage is largely occupied by a hole, around which the actors gingerly progress. There seem to be steps leading down from it, so maybe some allusion is meant to the ladder of power; but it is hardly worth crippling the production for, and besides there is an upper level (largely unused) to make the point as well.

The hole seems to be in Russia in revolutionary year, a strange place for a play that keeps breaking into patriotic bits about England. A strange time too; it may account for Richard's feeling of divine mission, but not for Bolingbroke's desire to succeed him as king rather than party chairman. Perhaps the idea of Richard as the last Tsar was inspired by the Christian name of the actor, Nickolas Grace. (The surname may have been influential too.) A fearsomely intelligent player, Mr Grace gives full, if lightweight, measure to Richard's early frivolity and late maturity, but does little to fuse them. Stuart Wilson adds another to the line of strong quiet Bolingbrokes, and the uncles--Clifford Rose and Robert Eddison--are out in strength.
