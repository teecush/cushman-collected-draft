May 25, 1973

Making Words At The Keys

Robert Cushman · The Daily Telegraph Magazine

Making words at the keys

Gypsy, the musical memoirs of stripper Gypsy Rose Lee, opens in London next week. The lyrics are by Stephen Sondheim. Lyricist of the hit shows, Company and Follies, he rescued Broadway from the treacly sentimentality of the Fifties with his sharp lines. Robert Cushman reports on the greatest talent behind the form today

Every so often it becomes possible to send out a message to the effect that the American musical lives. At the moment it lives chiefly in the person of Stephen Sondheim, a 42-year-old New Yorker who writes music which is alive and lyrics which are incomparable. The lyrics in particular have made him the object of a small cult in London (which had his Company for most of last year) and of a rather larger one in New York (which sustained his Follies for roughly the same period), but to the English public he is still - as far as anyone successful in his business can be - an unsung hero. Still most of that public have Sondheim phrases going around somewhere in their heads; "Maria" may not truthfully be the most beautiful sound you ever heard, but it has stuck.

Sondheim began his career as a boy wonder and he still looks boyish - round-faced and with the curious New Yorker's gift of appearing intense and relaxed at the same time. If there is such a thing as a Manhattan drawl, Sondheim speaks with it. He is courteous but not - by the pressurised standards of the American theatre - particularly effusive. He is clear-headed and articulate about his own work and everybody else's, and extremely clever - not just intelligent, but ingenious. For some years he contributed puzzles of celebrated intricacy to New York magazine, and he has a considerable reputation for gamesmanship. He may possess the sharpest, most fastidious mind ever to have been applied to the Broadway musical.

He is also a paradox; a trained musician (majoring at Williams College and going on from there to study with Milton Babbitt) whose greatest successes have been with words, and who has never been able to reconcile himself to the fact. That he is one of the best current Broadway composers is as much a comment on the competition as a compliment to him. But though the musical has dwindled melodically since the days of Gershwin and Kern, lyrics, at their best, are better than ever. (At their worst, they are as terrible as they always were.) And it is important that they should be, since they belong with the verse, stories, plays and film scripts that have defined for us the American comic spirit; which means a very high proportion of the most durable American writing.

Good lyrics, good musicals, ally wit and feeling - and exclude solemnity and sentimentality; which is why they are worth taking seriously. Sondheim's lyrics are the form's second peak; the first was reached in the Thirties in the work of the sardonic, unhappy and finally alcoholic Lorenz Hart whose declarations of love were coated in brashness or flippancy and who treated even lost or unrequited love with an astringent cheerfulness:

[Song lyric excerpt omitted.]

Sondheim is apt to take off into lengthy diatribes against Hart ("over-rated... technically sloppy... the words don't fit the music"), but in fact what he grudgingly admires as "that world-weary view of life" has seeped rewardingly into his own work. And thereby hangs another paradox.

Kenneth Tynan once wrote that "American musicals traditionally divide into two opposed categories: folksy optimism (Rodgers and Hammerstein) versus city cynicism (Rodgers and Hart)." But though, by this rule, Sondheim qualifies as a city cynic, he received his theatrical education from the headmaster of the other school, Oscar Hammerstein. (Richard Rodgers, playing on both teams, cancels himself out).

Sondheim's parents divorced when he was a boy; he and his mother left New York for Pennsylvania. Hammerstein had a farm nearby and his youngest son became friendly with the young Sondheim who in time became fascinated by the Hammerstein craft of musical-making.

"Oscar had an entire influence on me. A lot of people have a mistaken idea of Hammerstein as a sort of great, woolly bear. In fact, he was a highly sophisticated, sharp-tongued witty man. You don't get that from his work, because those weren't the kind of subjects he was interested in writing about. As a critic he was also sharp-tongued and articulate. And he never tried to impose a style on me."

It was Hammerstein who, with typical generosity, defended Hart's lyrics when Sondheim attacked them: "He said that it was Hart who made colloquial lyrics possible". And this was a legacy of which Hammerstein himself took full advantage. But where Hart's colloquialisms had always been personal to himself, Hammerstein made an attempt to find a style for each character. It was this emphasis on character more than anything else which made his first collaboration with Rodgers - Oklahoma! - appear so revolutionary at its premiere in 1942. It was an emphasis which Sondheim inherited, applying Hammerstein's techniques with a Hart sensibility. (His blending of the two traditions became unusually clear when he himself collaborated with Rodgers on Do I Hear a Waltz?)

His first professional show, a product of his tutelage under Hammerstein, for which he wrote both music and lyrics, was accepted for production in 1954 but the impresario who bought it died before the necessary money could be raised. Still the project enabled Sondheim to make theatrical contacts, among them the playwright Arthur Laurents. In 1956 they met at a party and Laurents revealed that he was writing the book, Leonard Bernstein the music and nobody as yet the lyrics for a musical based, as the billing was one day to phrase it, "on a conception of Jerome Robbins". The conception was Romeo and Juliet retold in terms of Manhattan gang warfare. The show, of course, was West Side Story, and Sondheim, of course, was engaged as lyricist.

West Side Story has aged badly; and Sondheim can claim to be the only member of the creative team who realised that it would. "They all thought I was being a smart-ass, but the show had no basis in character. I prophesied that if it were revived in ten years' time it would look ridiculous. And in ten years' time it was revived at Lincoln Centre and all the critics - being critics - sensed something was wrong and couldn't place it. So they blamed the production. But it wasn't the production; it was the show."

It will be interesting to see how the critics jump if this year's mooted London revival materialises.

I suggested that even back in 1957 (when all the critics thought it belonged to the ages) the show might not have been that hot. "Oh, no, it worked. You may not have liked it, but it worked. But it's not about juvenile delinquency or Puerto Ricans or any of the things it pretends to be about. It's about theatre. It's a celebration of theatre - of a way of telling a story."

Of his own contributions to the musical Sondheim is most disparaging about the love songs - Tonight, Somewhere ("that should never have had words anyway"), One Hand, One Heart and Maria. The last two, with their portentous Bernstein tunes (what Sondheim describes as "Lennie's classique style") certainly bear out his strictures about lack of character content; as soon as Tony and Maria meet they lose all connection with any street except Tin Pan Alley. Tony's rhapsody on his lady's name has the misty religioso feeling familiar in the worst of pop. Unlike Hammerstein, Sondheim cannot write convincing lines for unsophisticated characters and never, since West Side Story, has he tried.

There are songs in the score, though, which seem to be giving us, if not the characters' own words, then a good academic translation of them. Tonight communicates urgency, even if it is not quite their urgency. And the best of the love songs, I Feel Pretty, makes captivating use of casual, slangy adjectives:

[Song lyric excerpt omitted.]

We are still seeing Maria from the outside, but we are seeing her, and the conceit (in both senses) is so self-aware as to be disarming. (Sondheim, by the way, disagrees with this estimate. "No Puerto Rican girl would sing quite so much like Noel Coward, I fear.")

As far as the author is concerned, "there are now only two songs in the score I like: the Jet Song and Something's Coming." The latter has always been my own favourite song in the show. Bernstein's jagged rhythms are here at their most exciting and Sondheim matches them with a flow of short phrases attuned to Tony's pent-up excitement. The images are abrupt and always concrete, with a touch of wit. We are presented with a mood rather than a person but, though not a great lyric, it declares a potential master.

The American critic Eric Bentley once summed up the Rodgers and Hammerstein era as "the age of phoney seriousness", and explicitly associated it with the Eisenhower regime. And it is easy to detect a grey-flannel earnestness settling over the last scene of Carousel (You'll Never Walk Alone), gaining complete control of South Pacific (the bloated Some Enchanted Evening is the true ancestor of Maria) and finally descending to the treacly depths of The Sound of Music. Nobody who likes musicals at all is likely to be wholly resistant to sentiment, but it becomes offensive when a moral is attached. (Why a form virtually invented and sustained by Jews should be so intent on promulgating an ersatz Christianity is a question worth pondering.) West Side Story, its good intentions barely cloaked by its tough facade, was probably the ultimate Eisenhower musical.

Still, not all Fifties musicals were stuffy. At the beginning of the decade there had been Frank Loesser's hilarious Guys and Dolls and there was a comparable treat in store at the end. The memoirs of Gypsy Rose Lee, the celebrated stripper, had been acquired for stage presentation by another professional exhibitionist, David Merrick.

He signed up a number of the West Side team - Robbins as director, Laurents as librettist. Sondheim was engaged as lyricist and composer, but Ethel Merman on whom the whole project hinged (she played Gypsy's domineering mother) demurred. She had just had a flop and was in no mood to take risks on an unknown composer. So Jule Styne, an extremely prolific composer with a knack of writing star vehicles, was brought in. Sondheim, after some heart-searching, stayed on as lyricist.

"I'm never sure whether that was the best or worst decision I ever made. On the one hand, coming on top of West Side Story it established me in everybody's mind as just a lyricist. On the other... it was a really great show. And it still stands up beautifully." Asked to name his favourite musicals Sondheim is apt to go through Guys and Dolls, My Fair Lady, Carousel ("my favourite score after Porgy and Bess") and then smile and say "Well, there was Gypsy."

Styne's score is his best by a long way - and Sondheim's lyrics, superficially more conventional than those for West Side Story, in fact marked a great advance. Every number showed a keen eye for specifics in character and situation and he met the challenge of providing seven Merman specialities in one score with great resource. The best lyric, however, goes not to Mama, but to the two daughters she forced on stage to gratify her own ambition, and who cannot wait to be rid of her.

[Song lyric excerpt omitted.]

Mama keeps pets. The deadpan cataloguing of apparently incongruous objects has become a Sondheim trademark.

In this song, too, Sondheim captures a tone of silky bitchiness he was later to explore in some depth. Here it is embryonic, as the girls exhort their already much-married mother:

[Song lyric excerpt omitted.]

During most of the dozen years that have elapsed since Gypsy's Broadway opening, there have been rumours that Merman would be repeating her triumph over here. Even last year they had not been finally stilled (though she was, strictly speaking, too old for the part even in 1959). Now, however, we are to see Angela Lansbury who opens in the show at the Piccadilly Theatre on May 29 with author Arthur Laurents directing.

We have a fair amount of catching-up to do with middle-period Sondheim. We have seen only one of the shows that came between Gypsy and Company - A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum, Sondheim's Broadway debut as a composer and a great success in both London and New York (where it was revived last year), though its music (lightweight, untroubled, easy to listen to and to forget) is both the least distinguished and the least typical he has written. He wrote a far more expansive score for a major flop, Anyone Can Whistle ("full of inventive stage techniques; but it was arch and it suffered from a certain condescension towards the audience"). Throughout all these projects Sondheim's talents were audibly maturing.

Company arrived in 1970, five years after Sondheim's last musical, Do I Hear a Waltz? In the interim Broadway had come up with its nearest approach yet to a serious (as opposed to a solemn or bloated) musical in Cabaret. Flawed though it was, this was a triumph for its ubiquitous producer-director Harold Prince who now performed the same dual function on Company. He and Sondheim, together with librettist George Furth, hit on the ideal form for a modern intelligent musical. Company is a comedy of manners. And, faithful to its genre, it details the capitulation to marriage of an apparently dedicated bachelor. Set, as all musicals really should be, in New York, it gets right to the heart of Manhattan, or at any rate of some variously monied segments of it.

The score is the peak of Sondheim's achievement. The music is sinuous, surprising, vertiginously modern. The wry fruit of Sondheim's own 41 years as a bachelor can be tasted when the hero's married lady friends cluck over his supposed loneliness:

[Song lyric excerpt omitted.]

The soothing accompaniment adds the final touch of patronage (or, possibly, matronage). Even more unnerving is the catalogue of his domestic virtues reeled off by the five ladies and their five husbands, who act both as an unofficial chorus to the show and as the vehicles for its examination of marriage.

Sondheim's own enthusiasm for it is greater than for any of his other shows: "I never get bored by a moment of it. It's full of life, point, joy, guts. It's always fresh to me."

Within a year Company had been joined on Broadway by Follies. As before, the production team comprised Hal Prince, choreographer Michael Bennett, and the superlative designer Boris Aronson, and all three surpassed themselves. Follies is a spectacular production in which the memory of the supposedly carefree musical productions of the Thirties is ironically counterpointed by the frustrations that have overtaken those of their performers who have survived into the Seventies. The setting, aptly enough is a Broadway Theatre on the eve of demolition.

Sondheim's talent for period pastiche is evident in Gypsy and in a couple of numbers in Company, but only in Follies is it given full exposure in a series of minutely accurate recreations of the show songs of the Twenties and Thirties:

[Song lyric excerpt omitted.]

The protectiveness and the preciousness - the sense that falling in love meant the right to try on long words as though they were long pants - are exactly right. And the tune which accompanies is one of its composer's proudest achievements: "I'd challenge anyone to distinguish it from authentic Jerome Kern."

Though he loves the songs of the period, Sondheim has little affection for the shows which encased them, and Follies is, if anything, an exaggerated attack on the cocksure values which the Ziegfeld spectaculars exemplified. Could I Leave You, the reaction of an embittered wife to a husband who has suggested divorce, is in Sondheim's best contemporary style:

[Song lyric excerpt omitted.]

Sondheim is not the first to try this kind of lyric but his predecessors have usually just spluttered in admiration of their own audacity. He is the first to keep his cool and his technique.

That technique amounts here, as in Company, to virtuosity. Consider the wit and economy that can reduce the masochistic ballad formula to the breeziness of a blues song. Or another of Sondheim's laconic catalogues summarising a classic Hollywood career. Or perhaps best of all, the accumulation of evocative detail with which two middle-aged men recall their youth as stage-door johnnies:

[Song lyric excerpt omitted.]

The principal characters of Follies, so richly depicted in the songs, are rather thinly drawn in James Goldman's book. Sondheim defends this on the ground that "they're not really characters, they're essences". But you could have said the same of the lovers in West Side Story, and maybe Follies too is best enjoyed as a celebration of theatre. The title, of course, is double-edged, referring to follies both Ziegfeld and human; in a few years' time, if the musical dates, it could have taken on a triple resonance, and we could be thinking of it as Sondheim's Folly. But follies can be magnificent buildings, and London should have been allowed a look at this one before it was finally dismantled.

Company and Follies have given Sondheim the same position for his generation as Rodgers and Hammerstein had for theirs.

He and his collaborators have broken down technical barriers and restored to the musical the fluidity that was its birthright and which was being buried under trundling narratives. Company has no plot-line and a bizarre time-scheme involving three 35th birthdays for its protagonist; Follies makes successful use of flashbacks and double exposure. They have also followed the path mapped out by the most successful parts of Cabaret and given the musical back to music; the numbers are once again the core of the show instead of having to sneak in between wedges of book.

Sondheim has now written his third show for Prince, A Little Night Music, which opened in New York on February 25 and has already picked up the Tony Award for the season's best musical. The team remains much the same as for Follies, with the addition of a new choreographer, Patricia Birch, and a new librettist, the playwright Hugh Wheeler. Wheeler, following a fashion for making musical plays out of non-musical films, has found his source in the classic Ingmar Bergman Smiles of a Summer Night. The film is comedy veering towards farce, an elegant bedroom pavane, set in turn-of-the-century Sweden. Again it is comedy of manners, though the manners are different from those of Company, and the score - though filled with waltzes, a new departure for Sondheim as a composer - can hardly accommodate the pastiche of Follies. Perhaps to ensure a proper tone, two English comediennes - Glynis Johns and Hermione Gingold - have been cast in the leads. When the show was tried out in Boston somebody was heard to remark in tones of respectful wonder, "It has a beginning, middle, and end, and no-one picks up a microphone." It sounds as though Sondheim has retained his complete technical assurance, and his sense of form.

He is, despite his refusal to write his own books, a natural dramatist, with the dramatist's delight in paradox, in character and in narrative (his declared ambition is "to go on finding new ways to tell stories"). But beyond this he has the great lyricist's delight in the sheer flavour of words and rhymes: Elaine Stritch's big belting number in Company may illustrate it best of all:

[Song lyric excerpt omitted.]
