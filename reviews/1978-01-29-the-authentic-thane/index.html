Jan 29, 1978

The Authentic Thane

Robert Cushman · The Observer Magazine

The Authentic Thane

Profile of Ian McKellen by Robert Cushman

LAST YEAR -- in Newcastle and London -- we met Macbeth: met him, since this was a studio-sized production, face to face. We were in fact locked up with him, and felt it within ourselves to achieve success, to crave more of it, to kill for it, to live without hope and to smell damnation. We gazed on the face ('a book,' says Lady Macbeth, 'where men may read strange things') and heard the authentic voice, withered and curdling, of despair. Just occasionally we laughed, since the performance revealed a sardonic, courtly wit in Macbeth. And very often we smiled, from sheer joy at the skill and imagination of the actor; and above all -- making connections visible where none before had been apparent -- at the intelligence of Ian McKellen.

In my experience McKellen is the only actor -- almost the only person -- who admits to enjoying interviews. He comes on as a practical intellectual. He can argue with his directors on their own ground -- Trevor Nunn, welcoming the challenge, described the effect on him as 'rejuvenating' -- but having done this, he can get on with the job. 'He can be fantastically objective -- but then he can do it, he can deliver.' The words are Susan Fleetwood's, once Ophelia to McKellen's Hamlet and an adoptive sister ever since, and they differentiate McKellen from the majority of university-bred actors.

The fact that McKellen went to Cambridge already begins to set him apart from those other top stage actors of the past 30 years who have aroused general excitement, if not unanimous acclaim: Richard Burton, Paul Scofield, Albert Finney, Peter O'Toole, and Nicol Williamson, those who arouse the fiercest partisanship, for or against. Scofield excepted, they all have the name of being roughnecks. McKellen has been generally regarded as a sensitive plant; surprisingly, since he is one of the most solidly built actors around (all muscle, no fat) and even possesses the fashionable qualification of a north-country background. In fact he lays his first acting ambitions at the door of his primary school in Wigan. He was in the school reciting team, which 'used to go around to Nonconformist church socials.'

From here he progressed to Bolton School, a highly theatre-conscious establishment where 'we virtually did rep every term. I was forced into taking acting very seriously.' At the same time he was encouraged to follow his parents' example of going regularly to Bolton Rep, and began to enjoy working out for himself -- 'by surmise and reading the occasional book' -- how they got the plays on.

'What was amazing was that by the time I got to Cambridge and suddenly met, for the first time in my life, 20 other people who were as daft about the theatre as I was, I had more experience of acting than even Corin Redgrave. I knew more about upstaging and the techniques of acting at 14 than many of the people I now work with do. It was as if I'd been born in a trunk; which I wasn't -- my father was a borough engineer.' (Both his grandfathers, though, were preachers, a kind of histrionic lineage which can also be found in Laurence Olivier, Alec McCowen and Sybil Thorndike.)

He had been through the teenage stage of 'this is Ian, he's going to be an actor'; but (again a common condition) he had given up the idea by the end of his schooldays, and had decided to become a teacher. Continued success in university plays made professional acting seem possible and sensible. The turning-point seems to have been a Justice Shallow in his first year which, when Trevor Nunn came up to Cambridge a year later, was still being discussed.

That McKellen should have specialised in old men as an undergraduate is an index of his technical accomplishment. It also summed up the problem that faced him when he went professional. 'I was rather proud and pleased with myself that I knew how to do it; what I was hopeless at was stepping on the stage without any aids and just behaving in the style of the play as myself. It's only very recently that I've had the courage to forget the technical side of and think about the spiritual side. Not think about it, just let it happen.'

At this point in the conversation he pulls himself up and says proudly that he remains 'a very technical actor indeed. When I come to the performance, there's nothing I do that I'm not aware of.' Then he qualifies again: 'Most nights that I'm on stage I do something, consciously or unconsciously, that I've never done before.'

McKellen improvises, to bring the threads together, within a firm structure; and for the last two years it has been a structure on which he has collaborated with an extraordinary collection of other skilled masons. His half of the RSC (seen at Stratford last year and in London this) has been a compact yet various ensemble, who have restored belief in actors' democracy. There are pleasing ironies here; Nunn has remained the boss, his authority firmer and more popular than ever, and McKellen, once co-founder of the self-governing Actors' Company, is a brighter star than before. He has in fact played nothing but whopping leads; the chutzpah of attempting Romeo, Macbeth and Leontes ('The Winter's Tale') in the same season is what led him to Stratford; at the Aldwych he has added Bernick in Ibsen's 'Pillars of the Community' and Face in Jonson's 'The Alchemist'.

That's good casting for him, and not only because it allows him for once to be as amusing on stage as off. ('Not many laughs in Romeo; I make myself laugh as Macbeth.') Face is a virtuoso con-man; and McKellen plays him as a blank in repose, who comes instantly to life in each new disguise, and goes into a silent acquisitive frenzy at the end. The action and the frenzy are the distinguishing marks.

They do not necessarily imply frantic mobility. An actress who worked with him earlier in his career loved his stillness in rehearsal, though he lost it all before an audience. Susan Fleetwood, though, admired his Romeo for its repose: 'When he was still, he seemed so vulnerable and young.' There was none of this relaxation on the first night at Stratford; he fell back, presumably through nerves, on the idea of adolescence, looping himself round handy pillars. There was no comparison with the performance he had achieved when the play reached London a year later. His Stratford Romeo was (in the words most frequently used against him) a mannered performance; it obscured the part and the play. His London performance merely had a few mannerisms, which relate to his ordinary speech-patterns, are unassumed and unassuming: at best part of his appeal and at worst harmless.

He himself accounts Romeo a failure ('I came to it too late') but his comments on the role illuminate why the performance and Nunn's production gripped the audience as this play seldom does. 'Romeo's mad -- he's a suicide case -- he can't reconcile the enormity of love with the business of having breakfast and being nice to people.' McKellen commands the hollowest stare and the most drained and hopeless voice on the English stage. He can suggest blank black caverns in the mind.

Hence his extraordinary Macbeth, which owes something, to be sure, to the production and the other players and the intimacy of the studio auditorium (the way in which he wants, for the foreseeable future, to play Shakespeare), much to the intelligence of his characterisation but most of all to his physical and spiritual energy. For the first time the play in the theatre justifies the text-books; it releases a sense of evil. Though he bristles at talk of mannerisms, he recognises the complaint that 'Ian McKellen flogs his charm too much'; all the same, the performances of his new maturity have been marked as much as anything by their uncompromising ugliness.

Perhaps that's only possible for an actor who is sure of his charm to begin with; anyway it marked his thrusting arriviste Dr Faustus (a watershed for him in my book) and his grotesque Marquis of Keith (another con-man). It enables him to play the envy, the malevolence and the final nihilism of Macbeth and not to fuss about the 'nobility'; and yet to leave us with a sense of nobility, attained by forcing us to look at the truth. All actors talk, given half the chance, about the Truth; but it was Shakespeare's truth that reached us here.

The plumber of these depths is in his undisclosed late thirties, lives in a house in Camberwell (a district, he says, which can only go up), travels around London by scooter in 'an electric-blue nylon-plated boiler-suit' (Susan Fleetwood's description), steps out in white suits and smiles a lot, thus producing an overall dazzle, makes with the hands like 'some kind of octopus', is happiest working, inspires enormous loyalty in friends and colleagues, probably secures more confidences than he gives out, says he has no idea what he'll do next . . . and immediately produces half-a-dozen possibilities: National Theatre, return to the Actors' Company, running a rep, a big series on television, films, round the world with a one-man show -- none of them, however, have proved as attractive or feasible as a renewed stint with the RSC, so for the moment he stays where he is.
