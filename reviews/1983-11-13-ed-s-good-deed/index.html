Nov 13, 1983

Ed's Good Deed

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Ed's good deed

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN decides he hates musicals.

Spacious and resplendent, the redone Old Vic is once again an exciting place to visit. Ed Mirvish has done a good deed, giving the place not only a new coat of paint, but a new sense of purpose.

His first subscription season kicks off with Blondel, which is fun if nothing else. Its hero is the medieval minstrel who notoriously hung around outside every prison window in Europe, humming and strumming, before finally locating his incarcerated monarch Richard Coeur-de-Lion.

In this version he unhistorically, or at least unlegendarily, arranges his master's escape, though since he is but a singing fool he needs help. He gets it from his determined girlfriend; she may be a serf (though an uncommonly well-dressed one), but she is never the weaker vassal. Also of assistance is an assassin; hired by Prince John to kill the king, he would prefer to murder the minstrel (he knows his own limitations), but he actually and accidentally bumps off Richard's gaoler, the proto-Nazi Archduke of Austria.

Blondel ("he was overdue to write a million-selling rondel") is presented as an aspiring pop-star whose previous near-misses include Groats from Heaven and Send in the Jesters and who travels with a trio of back-up vocalists called the Blondettes. Doesn't that tell you all you need to know? Welcome to 1189 and all that.

Narrative links are chanted by a quartet of monks; these are played by Cantabile, who sound like the King's Singers, only -- since their plainsong McGonagall is meant to sound funny -- a lot less irritating. They explain that the Lion Heart's subjects would never have thought of him as Richard I, since they were ignorant of any future royal Dicks.

Tim Rice's lyrics take him back to Joseph and the Amazing Technicolour Dreamcoat, a good place for him to go. His jokes do not always sit comfortably on the music, but they are mostly good and they keep coming. Stephen Oliver's tunes have less theatrical size than Andrew Lloyd Webber's, but more wit and taste; and the main love-duet, The Least of My Troubles, is charming. It does not manage to make the love-interest interesting; and though Paul Nicholas does heavy duty as Blondel, the evening runs out on him.

It is probably impossible to be funny to music for a whole evening; and, like all the Rice and/or Webber shows, this one eschews dialogue almost entirely. If the most successful performances come from David Burt as John and Chris Langham as his hit-and-miss man, this is partly due to the allure of villainy, but also because they are occasionally allowed to talk.

Peter James's production makes the most of all opportunities, including those provided by Tim Goodchild's sets, which divide and conquer like richly coloured amoebae. The Falklands are momentarily a factor when our heroine derides the Third Crusade as "a war in some far-off place no one has ever heard of." (Jerusalem?) Blondel, outside the prison, does not sing "Open the door, Richard."

Blondel was by far the best of the week's three tuners. Many of its weaknesses -- a dull central romance, undeveloped characters, a tendency to wander musically into the middle of the road -- are shared by Dear Anyone (Cambridge), where they are far more damaging, since this show takes itself very seriously. Its heroine is the agony-aunt on a New York daily. She seems to reply to her correspondents directly rather than in print, but this odd practice at least brings together (through a mix-up in envelopes) a shy Manhattan cabby and a New England illiterate.

Their story is told in the best number of the evening; they are then dropped. So, for the most part, is the show's ostensible subject; we proceed to the familiar story of the career-woman, and the conflict between her work and her love-life. We are told nothing substantial about either, so the show keeps wrong-footing itself.

Don Black's lyrics are competent and consonant, but lack any individual spark. Geoff Stephens's music is mostly hollow, especially when it attempts to be dramatic. Jack Rosenthal wrote the book, which, coming from so able a workman, is a disgrace. However, since he had to fit his script around existing songs (this is one of those started-as-an-album projects), it may not be his fault that the story caves in. The uncertainty of focus was there from the start, and he has thrown in some good one-liners.

An abiding puzzle is why an all-British team set their tale in America. Have they doubts about its universality? Or are they merely trying to make us, and themselves, feel hip? Imitation Americana is all over the theatre at the moment; it is a trend to which I have probably contributed myself, but that doesn't mean I have to approve of it.

As a vehicle for Jane Lapotaire (authentically tender-tough) it halts. Better to have brought over Woman of the Year, a more efficiently mechanical show with the same plot. Or revived Bells are Ringing, which also has a scatty interfering heroine. Other influences, conscious or otherwise: Chicago, for one whole song; On the Town, for one whole dialogue routine. Not to mention Guys and Dolls; Stubby Kaye, the first man to sing "Sit Down You're Rockin' the Boat," obliges here with another gospel rouser, only this one doesn't rouse. Was it in his contract, or just last-minute desperation?

Mr Kaye, who is wasted, plays a mailman who gets to be features editor. I thought this was going to turn to a practical joke, but it's for real. (The other way round, I might believe it.) Ralph Koltai's three-decker set is equally cut off from journalistic reality; it also eats up most of the playing-area, confining choreography to a few automaton formations on the front strip of the stage. I liked Arlene Allan best, as Birdbrain of Boston. David Taylor's direction is diffuse.

*

The Duenna (Young Vic) is a ballad-opera by Sheridan to which Lance Mulcahy has affixed not only new music but additional dialogue and extra lyrics. You do not need a crib to identify these; whenever a clinch is imminent, Mr Mulcahy has one lover sing to another "Each time I wonder where you are/I turn round and there you are." This happens five times; no wonder we are gladdened.

Sheila Steafel looks deeply uncomfortable in the title-role, Trevor Peacock is a reliable heavy father; but the only performer to emerge with real credit is Amanda Redman, a very handsome heroine. Sheridan's comic Jew, who was not very offensive, has been changed into a comic Australian, who is. The setting is Seville, so Frank Dunlop's production has a lot of heel-clicking. I think I hate musicals.

*

Marivaux's masterpiece False Admissions has been brought to the Lyric Studio, Hammersmith, by Shared Experience. In this comedy every character is simultaneously gripped by sexual and economic passions, and in his determination to make all clear Mike Alfreds offers an over-disciplined but fascinating production. Another Marivaux is added this week, so we shall speak more of this.
