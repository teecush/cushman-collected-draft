Mar 7, 1976

Fond Farewell

Robert Cushman · The Observer

This week the critics, in advance for once of the public, are allowed into the new National Theatre. My column next Sunday will very likely be full of its wonders. But last Saturday we witnessed the National company's last performance at the Old Vic, its 12-year home; and an event less calculated to make anyone glad at the move to the South Bank it is difficult to imagine.

The performance was entitled Tribute To The Lady and it celebrated the eccentric and indomitable Lilian Baylis who in 1914, seeing attendances flagging at her Royal Victoria Coffee Music Hall, turned "in despair" to Shakespeare and who, by her death in 1937, had seen her Old Vic become the most important theatre in the country. Peggy Ashcroft played her rejoicingly; at the close, acknowledging that her boys and girls were about to depart to a posh new building costing thousands and thousands and thousands of pounds (she seemed to think them mad but forgivable), she promised that if new incumbents were not found for the old place she would come back and haunt us.

This injunction was delivered much in the style of Miss Baylis's own curtain speeches; or at least in what Dame Peggy fully convinced us to have been their style. She could pat her audiences' heads a bit; if they had been especially good; but on the whole flattery of the public was not her style. She was more likely to rebuke them, in a cascading voice, full of teeth: they had come short, or not come at all. Mondays were particularly shameful for poor attendance; and an entire production of King Lear seems to have perished from neglect. On such occasions the audience were addressed as "bounders"; and as they bent over in their stalls and tugged guiltily at their socks they had presumably to console themselves with the reflection that their task-mistress must have loved them, so to chastise them.

The real Miss Baylis, as was made clear by an actual recording, did not sound much like Dame Peggy; her voice had a double twang, part Cockney, part South African. Nor, I would guess, was she as endearingly aware of her own outrageousness as Dame Peggy seemed to be. But if not a historical facsimile, her attacking eccentric was a magnificent creation. This actress has not knocked me so completely out since The Wars of the Roses (not quite as long ago as it sounds); and never before has she made me laugh, though here she overflowed with comic energy. She seemed as grateful for its release as anyone; and it was this sense of liberation that pervaded the occasion.

Earlier Sir John Gielgud had given us a superb, indeed revelatory, account of Hamlet's "rogue and peasant slave" soliloquy, marking, with a precision I have never heard before, the transition from ungoverned emotion to disciplined thought: "About, my brain." Later Ralph Richardson wandered on to the stage and ambled around it for several heavenly minutes reminiscing. He then waylaid two unsuspecting fellow-actors who found themselves supporting him in the barge scene from Antony and Cleopatra. There should, of course, have been a third knight. The absence through illness of Laurence Olivier, whose career has been more closely linked with the Vic than that of any other actor, made the evening incomplete. He was to have read the narration; Albert Finney deputised with great good-humour.

A couple of nights later I passed the Vic in its newly darkened state; already it looks sad and peeling. This is the first time it has ever been closed with no company imminent to open it again. Anybody who has grown up in London, interested in theatre, must have received part of his education there. Mine began in 1951: Henry V, with Alec Clunes, directed by Glen Byam Shaw, and, if anyone asks you, definitive. I grieve and hope for the place as much as anyone: the fact that it has now had a perfect requiem is the more reason for not laying it to rest.

Miss Baylis's attitude to her plays, her players and her audiences--proprietorial, committed, and, except with money, generous--has yet to be improved on. But the Vic's glory resided in its doing work no one else would attempt; it was, for years, our best and only classical theatre. A reconstituted Old Vic on original lines would now be a poor relation of the National and the RSC. It has, when it re-opens, to find a new identity. I would rather see it permanently closed than second-rate.

*

In The Bells (Greenwich), Freddie Jones plays Irving's legendary role of Mathias, the guilty Burgomaster driven to despair and death by the ringing in his ears (sleigh-bells, church-bells, all sorts of bells). Mr Jones, a heap of crumbling flesh, brilliantly conveys the tell-tale gnawings of fear; only the final set-piece, a silent re-enactment of his crime, strains his resources and our attention.

As regards Eric Thompson's production I must tread carefully; I once directed a Victorian play at this theatre and was dashed to find comic business that I had lifted bodily from the stage directions derided as my own insensitive invention. The silent-picture music which Mr Thompson repeatedly employs, though it seems to me to undercut the reality of Mr Jones, is indeed specified in Leopold Lewis's text. Maybe melodramatic conventions wear out more quickly than comic; I can see no justification for the lurid green light that hits the stage for Mathias's briefest asides. At best these devices remind us that we are seeing a specimen of nineteenth-century drama; it might be more enjoyable merely to see a play about a conscience-stricken murderer. In fairness, Mr Thompson's staging of Mathias's fatal vision is impressive.

*

Sporting the best-heeled set ever seen at the Bush, Andy Smith's Winter Visitors has one penetrating lovers' row, and a great deal of overwrought domestic bickering. At the end the writer calls down vengeance on his characters for taking up everybody's time with their tedious and rather fanciful problems; it's difficult not to feel that he has himself to blame. The final scene brings on a gunman and with him some real suspense, not all caused by natural dread of a loud bang in a small theatre.

*

One of the Bush's biggest successes, Stephen Poliakoff's City Sugar, now makes a smaller bang in a larger theatre, the Comedy: strong on detail but short on punch and shapelessly directed. John Shrapnel's weary ambitious DJ has gone, and with him much of the lazy, grimy, three-in-the-morning feeling that previously distinguished the play. Adam Faith's performance has speed, but no grip.
