Dec 1, 1968

Funny Sunday

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

Funny Sunday

Sunday is funny, I spend it reviewing things, Eating the hours when I could have been doing things.

Plays that are dead and, please God, won't get up again. Some nervous theatre has purchased a pup again. Death is a joke, and we all know how gay it is, Joe Orton lives with the posthumous deities. Little white wreaths of most flatt'ring sincerity, Born to the manner but not the dexterity— Funny Sunday.

“Somehow,” says Florence, in connection with her deceased spouse, “it spoilt the whole day, Alfred popping off like that.” That is the best possible summation of the mood of Funny People, the first of the two plays here, if you'll pardon the expression, commemorated. A casual understated reference to death is always good for a nervously approving titter, though I have yet to hear it raise a healthy laugh. As a comic ploy inter alia it no doubt has a legitimate function but it is thin nourishment for an entire play. It's too easily fabricated for one thing. Even echt Orton (particularly Entertaining Mr Sloane) seemed to me to place monotonous reliance on it, and in imitation Orton it becomes positively oppressive.

I'm a gullible fellow and if you were to tell me that Roy Minton was completely unacquainted with his presumptive master's voice, I would believe you. It is, after all, a small and funny world. But I could not credit such innocence to the management concerned; it is the theatre manager's unquestioned prerogative to be in tune with yesterday's zeitgeist. And really what, in the circumstances, could be more appropriate than a nice little play about necrophilia?

Florence keeps her husband's corpse in the bath, and being a lady of rigorous virtue, gives it not a lascivious thought. Alice, though, is made of frailer stuff, and is soon embarked on a round of what the girl in Black Comedy called “kinky games”. And not just with her own coffined husband, mark you; she covets her neighbour's as well. But it does wonders for releasing her inhibitions which, we are given to understand, were plentiful. They are also, I think, the point of the play. I reserve a particular corner of my loathing for plays whose characters have been called into existence purely as targets for their author's pity or disapproval—in fact, to feed his sense of superiority. (Please note “pity” as opposed to compassion; “disapproval” as opposed to hatred; either as opposed to love.)

For what it's worth, my notes include “Hazel Hughes arranges her clothes authoritatively”. Miss Hughes, of course, does everything authoritatively. More power to her, not that she needs it. Patricia Lawrence offered a remarkably sustained impersonation of Dora Bryan.

Come Sunday was better, even if its characters are called Mr and Mrs Seedy. Mr S is a tramp who speaks in the accents, though not the accent, of Pinter's caretaker. His wife drudges in an East End café and, desperate, has married him on an acquaintance of five minutes. This struck me as a trifle improbable, so a sign flashed up in my compartmentalised critic's mind: Theatre of the Absurd. However, what power the rest of the play had depended on its being taken literally.

Mrs Seedy is systematically humiliated, and sadism is not much fun unless you believe in the reality of the victim. That we did believe was largely due to Gwen Watford who raised the play's routine unpleasantness to the level of real pain as, endlessly battered, she kept returning for more.

The author has obviously worked hard, or hopefully, on the tramp's lines; they are painstakingly demotic, and one is left wondering why they seem so unreal. I can imagine no improvement on Alan Clarke's direction of either play.
