Mar 14, 1976

First Impressions

Robert Cushman · The Observer

The National Theatre's administrative staff moved into their new home on the South Bank some time before the actors. I have been haunting the place for a couple of months, researching a book and enjoying the view of the river: a glorious prospect from Somerset House to St Paul's. I was therefore inclined to regard the building as a marvellous office block which someone, reversing the usual practice, had to go and turn into a theatre.

Last week the opening repertory--Hamlet, John Gabriel Borkman, Plunder, Happy Days, Watch It Come Down--transported from the Old Vic, was unpacked for the critics. The process took four days, at the end of which I begin already to take the new National for granted. It is very rare for a critic to spend four successive days anywhere: quite enough to convince him that he has found a home.

It is not a building to lift the heart: the squat grey fly-towers which crown and dominate it put paid to that. But there is a reassuring sturdiness about it. I like concrete; I even like the South Bank. Unless I am actually in a hurry to get somewhere, I enjoy threading my way through the channelled mazes from one culture-dome to another. The theatre is separated from the rest of the complex by Waterloo Bridge, but it is enfolded by its own profusion of balconies. Since the building fronts the river you have, if you come by car, to drive right around it, which spoils the impact and makes you feel like a tradesman. Much better to take a bus or taxi, get off on the bridge and descend on its steps. Do not, however, go straight down to entrance-level; make a detour, along as many terraces as possible. They are wide and gratifying.

The foyer seems at the moment the architect's most decisive success. It has two drawbacks: the carpets are decidedly tacky and the ceilings are menacingly low, progressively so as you ascend the building. But the three storeys do interact; they are furnished with what are referred to in Macbeth as buttresses and coigns of vantage. Looking up you are always liable to see someone poised nursing a drink and playfully prepared to spill it over you. Denys Lasdun's professed aim of using the patrons themselves to furnish his open spaces looks like being brilliantly realised. The fact that last week the obliging bodies were largely those of my colleagues made the effect all the more comforting if not invariably decorative.

But, even denuded of life, the main foyer is far from being a bleak plain: it curves, oddly, beguilingly, and unpredictably, from box office to bar to bookstall to bar again. And you can get a drink without too much suffering, and having got it can consume it without being buffeted. You can even sit down, at tables which break the area without cluttering it. The bookstall sells newspapers, which is a good idea, and the usherettes continue to sell a mass-produced ice-cream, which isn't. If the National could do a deal with one of the more individual manufacturers they might make theatrical history. The programmes are those from the Old Vic, slightly blown up and almost purged of advertising.

Of the three auditoriums, only the Lyttelton is operational and that only just, as witness the lengthy scene-changes on Plunder and the roaring air-conditioning (particularly noticeable in the pauses of Happy Days, where it made its own contribution to Beckett's desolation). It is a strange animal: an orthodox, though phenomenally wide, proscenium stage matched to an auditorium that seems uneasy at being yoked to anything so conventional. The picture-frame stage is now old-fashioned. That does not invalidate it--many good plays have been written for it, and the National must produce them--but it demands a certain cosiness. Since the audience cannot enfold the stage, as in an open or studio theatre, the auditorium must caress the audience (as at the Old Vic or Wyndham's). There has to be some affection somewhere; Peter Hall, talking contemptuously on television of plush, gilt and cherubs, simply begged the question. The Lyttelton has, undeniably, the air of a large lecture-hall; the audience, spread out in long rows with never the relief of a centre aisle, are beleaguered by an imminent back wall. You feel splayed and crushed and the leg-room is none too generous.

Undeniably, everybody in the stalls is near the stage and nothing obviously mars the sight-lines, but the focus, if you are seated at the end of a front row, can be odd. There is not so much to look at in Happy Days that you want to view it extravagantly sideways-on. The crowded action of Plunder is even more resistant to such angling. The plays in general seemed dwarfed by the stage; the only exceptions were Hamlet and Borkman, both ironically major classics which belong, according to Mr Hall's plan, on the unfinished open stage of the Olivier. Ibsen's play, especially its last scene, attained mythic dimensions. For Hamlet I had the luck to be seated dead-centre: the lines of John Bury's set (dowdy at the Vic, magnificent here) were completed by those of the auditorium. The stage-floor was patterned like a target, and soliloquies were delivered with magnetic effect from the bull's-eye.

A seat in the circle frees you from claustrophobia, but removes you from the action. Such distance is unkind to actors (except acoustically--aurally, upstairs is even less peccable than downstairs) but generous to designers. The plays look mapped out; a full view of Hayden Griffin's majestic railway-station lent a new dimension to Watch It Come Down; it has to stand for something.

The plays themselves seemed, in last week's ambience, like so many demonstration-discs; and anyway no company can be at its best before an audience composed exclusively of freeloaders. Detailed criticism would be out of place, so I merely suggest:

(a) that Happy Days on a Monday afternoon was an ostentatiously bleak beginning; that one immured actress is a boring spectacle, however terrifying the insights (and they are intermittent in this play); and that I would rather have seen Peggy Ashcroft give her Lilian Baylis again, since here, with all the finesse in the world, she seemed a bit bland and lacking in individuality;

(b) that Dinsdale Landen now supplies Plunder with just what it needs (viz, a new self-perpetuating comic convention); his silly ass continually taking himself by surprise is miraculously delicate;

(c) that Albert Finney's Hamlet, sketchy at first, flowers in madness and never loses its hold; that Susan Fleetwood's Ophelia is heartbreaking; that Angela Lansbury (though still handicapped by an unyielding voice and a crown and costume fitted for Snow White's stepmother) now fully imposes her view of Gertrude as an amenable woman, lulled by queenship, whose illusions visibly fall from her in the closet scene; that most of the other performances are now at least respectable; and that this was the peak of the week.

(d) that nobody who hated Watch It Come Down the first time will be converted by a second look but that a sharp unspecified pain often breaks through the flaccid construction.

(e) that though I believed in the crazy present of Ralph Richardson's Borkman, and strongly in his apotheosis, I extended no credit to his banking past; that Dame Peggy is nastier and more precise than she was, Wendy Hiller even more horribly erect, and Philip Stone a definite acquisition, but that this seemed, in general, an off-night.

Anyway, I'm glad it's open.
