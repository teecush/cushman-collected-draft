Jan 9, 1977

Blunt Point

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Blunt Point

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

A Christmas gift unwrapped on Twelfth Night is apt to seem a little frayed. We critics were compelled to miss The Point when it opened at the Mermaid because, it was intimated, the technical effects were not in working order. Well, they are operative now; smoke swirls and retreats on cue, projections appear and disappear, and are often pleasingly geometric, and Ron Pember's production is altogether a tidy affair.

But though the trappings may have been brought up to scratch, the story itself itches. It concerns a boy who lives in a land where everything is pointed, notably the tops of the citizens' heads. He himself, uniquely flat-surfaced, is persecuted and driven out. (Brecht, curiously enough, allegorised Nazi anti-Semitism in a piece called Round Heads and Pointed Heads.)

That is Act One, the better half. Act Two finds him in exile, in a place called the Pointless Forest, which fails to live up to its adjective, though the pronouncements of its inhabitants are none too easy to follow. Much of The Point, to use the only idiom of the kind the script neglects, is beside itself, but not, I hope, with pride. Anyway, our hero gets back home and declares that everything has a point, so his compatriots can stop giving themselves such airs and tolerance is proclaimed.

The show originated as a TV cartoon, and the lack of an equivalent visual style undoes it on stage. Even the most regular inhabitants of the Land of Point have barely noticeable sharp ends, and had I been king (an office here performed in kindly fashion by Bernard Miles) I should have banished them all, especially the one who, doubtless on her director's instructions, trills every third word. Wayne Sleep is energetic, though hardly taxed by his own choreography; Oscar James has and communicates a good time impersonating Louis Armstrong, in by far the best of Harry Nilsson's songs, the rest of which contribute largely to the general flavour of cracker-barrel mysticism, soft-rock Disney, though his fables were far more inventive and made more sense.

*

I attended the King's Head to hear Loaded Questions, presented by the Phantom Captain School of Thought. Every spoken line in this piece is interrogative, with one exception. "And the next question is, what are the Greek, Roman and Nordic myths?" is of course a statement. Anyway, the entertainment is surprisingly varied in tone if not, strictly speaking, in mood.

The School of Thought has three pupils whom the programme identifies as Joel Cutrara, Louise Jones and Peter Deman. When they enter, the first looks serene, the second disdainful, and the third surprised and a little appalled to see us. They begin by tossing off a few conversational commonplaces ("Can we offer you a lift?" "Are you prepared to meet us half-way?") proffered with a steely politeness that immediately makes us feel got at.

They then proceed to some party pieces: various areas of life are defined for us in terms of the questions they occasion. Childhood is particularly vulnerable: a long period of being asked "isn't it about time you grew up?" They make up dialogues entirely composed of questions; a sex encounter is particularly easy to enjoy, as the performers are disconcertingly aware. Turning on us again, they ask us if we found the presence of recognisable characters helpful. They have already inquired if our attention is wandering, and if so where to: barbed, I'd call that.

Every so often they get portentous ("Are you changing your mind? Is your mind changing you?") and when they attack the fabric of their own performance ("after a while, don't you get hungry to make a simple statement?") they sound not so much self-doubting as self-pitying. Nobody, after all, asked them.

They also, from time to time, sing; pondering whether it's true what they say about Dixie, or wondering who's kissing her now. That, I'm afraid, is another statement. They might try Cole Porter's At Long Last Love, the only song I know composed entirely of questions. "Is it an earthquake or simply a shock? Is it the good turtle soup or merely the mock?" Loaded Questions is on the whole the good turtle soup. (Which reminds me; the food at the King's Head has improved enormously.)

*

Uhlanga (Theatre Upstairs) is merely the mock. So, at least, it seems here. In Johannesburg, where it was planned, or Lagos, where it was premiered, this one-man show, apparently dedicated to evoking the whole of black experience, would have found an audience more able--for we were certainly willing--to piece that experience together for themselves, though I am not sure they would need to be reminded of it in what appear to be simple terms, muddily presented.

In one respect James Mthoba's act rebounds on itself. He satirises the black image (simple, fawning, anxious to please) adopted to appease the whites, but when he himself breaks through as a performer it is on identical terms. His biggest asset is his shy smile. Though bitterness is implicit in his material, there is none in his persona. The most powerful thing in the evening is to be found in the programme, where is printed Strange Fruit, the American poem about a lynching which became a song-hit for Billie Holiday.
