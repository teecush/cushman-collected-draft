Dec 5, 1982

Life And Times Of The Slab Boys

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Life and times of the Slab Boys

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Still Life, Call Me Madam and Aladdin.

THE theme of John Byrne's Still Life, the last play in his Slab Boys trilogy which is now being given in its entirety and in repertory at the Royal Court, is that we all grow old, or at any rate older: "If"--as John Whiting, the late John Whiting, once put it--"we are lucky."

It is not a new theme, but maybe it is the only one that any writer is really capable of treating. Write anything that has a time-span attached, and there you are. Anyway, Mr Byrne gives it a very entertaining whirl, clinching it by writing two acts between which years elapse, but which both occur in the same spot, a graveyard, designed incidentally by Mr Byrne himself.

Of the three original Paisley slab boys only two remain, the always unfortunate Hector having met with his final accident. Over his grave meet Phil, with aspirations towards being an artist, and Spanky, now a scuffling pop musician, who may be about to get his big break with a fleeting appearance on Juke Box Jury. That dates us all.

They reminisce about the past years, and we may join in, especially if (as in my case) it is ages since we saw the previous plays. Mr Byrne is very good at jogging our memories through jogging theirs. They also fight, since boys will be boys, even when men, and especially when back in the company of those with whom they were boys. They run up against another despised old acquaintance, now in gents' outfitting, and they exchange a wife: Lucille, formerly sweetheart of the slab room.

In Act Two, Spanky has actually made it, and comes back a rock star. Underneath of course he hasn't changed, though whether this is as much to his credit as the fan press would have us believe is a matter for doubt. Phil has grown increasingly bitter, which is nothing for the audience to worry about, since it also means he has grown increasingly funny.

The play's greatest attraction, apart from its extreme believability, is its exploration of a shared, and persisting, joke-world. Black humour very often: Mr Byrne can be hilariously callous. He also covers a wide span of remembered pop-culture: from the days when George Elrick presented Housewives' Choice (wasn't he the one who used to sing along with the signature tune?) to the days when Spanky could meet and actually rap with Kris Kristofferson.

The only other influence I can detect is Samuel Beckett; Lucille's announcement of her pregnancy, following some farcical business with the late delivery of Phil's late mother's headstone, comes as near to a physicalisation of "they give birth astride of a grave" as most of us will care to get. The acting is very lively, with Billy McColl as Phil, Gerard Kelly as Spanky.

*

Rep revivals of large-scale musicals are chancy things, but Roger Redfarn has made a slick and sensible job of Call Me Madam at Birmingham. He has been much aided by Terry Parsons, whose sets for the two mythical countries of Lichtenburg and Truman America are lavishly practical, and by Tudor Davies for choreography conceived and executed without raggedness (though with one notable lift from Evita).

Mr Davies moves American politicians and socialites wittily about in the Washington Square Dance and gets his people dancing as if they had just popped out of clocks for The Ocarina, a manufactured folk-dance that sounds bearable when speeded up. Then, in Something to Dance About, he sails buoyantly through a medley of wartime-and-after styles, transforming the gentlemanly Jeremy Hawk into a flaming jitterbug, with upstretched wing and intoxicated smile.

Linking the two countries, as ambassadors, is party-giver Sally Adams, queen of all the madams, an Ethel Merman role probably unplayable as anything else. Miss Merman's strength, as we were reminded at this year's Royal Variety Performance, is uninhibitedness under complete control. She never overstates, but then she never has to. A Lichtenburger calls Sally "the most American American I have ever met," to which she replies, with her authors' obvious approval, "that's the nicest thing anybody ever told me."

It may not be Henry James's idea of Yankee innocence abroad, but it is American, 1950s American anyway, and when it is Miss Merman's the confidence cannot be begrudged. Noele Gordon's impersonation has nice moments of elephantine tenderness; she looks right, dumpy and determined, and eventually she sounds right, though the orchestra drowns out her first two numbers: Ethel would never be so unready. She has solid support from Basil Hoskins; William Relton and Veronica Page are tuneful and mobile in the juvenile element.

The script is thin (being post-Rodgers and Hammerstein, it is obliged to have a story, but would probably be happier as a string of gags), but there is an atavistic pleasure in hearing the thumping song-cues, especially when the resultant sound is something so effortlessly effervescent as It's a Lovely Day Today. This is reprised four times; by the last I felt we deserved It's a Lovely Day Tomorrow, which Irving Berlin, who had firm ideas about weather, had actually written earlier.

Other Berlin goodies in the score are The Best Thing For You, subtler than he is generally given credit for, and You're Just in Love, which is the one in which he complains that he smells blossom when there's no-one there, or something on those lines, and she tells him that he's okay, indeed enviable, and then they repeat it, contrapuntally, at increasing speed, and generally with encores. Miss Gordon, on the first night, was more than happy to oblige.

*

There could hardly be more contrasting composer-lyricists than the supremely popular Mr Berlin and Elizabeth Swados, who makes pronouncements like "some people don't want intensity, they want entertainment. That's not what I'm about." Her more ambitious scores are as unentertaining as she could possibly wish, but she has provided a propulsive backing of mainly African sounds for Aladdin (Commonwealth Institute), performed by an international cast, but stemming from New York's La Mama company. Visually, this is a ceaselessly inventive production. The language is invented, Peter Brook style, so it would be kind of the management to leave us enough light to read the three-page synopsis. I never met anyone who could memorise a synopsis.

One more week to see the American Dance Machine (Adelphi), who preserve old routines. Their selection has many gaps, but there is Gower Champion's funny shrine-number from Bye Bye Birdie, a beautiful intersection of black-and-white clad figures (like dancing dominoes) in a twenties party routine by Dan Siretta; a clog dance arranged with typical wit by Danny Daniels from Walking Happy, based on Hobson's Choice; and the presence of Zan Charisse, remembered here from Gypsy. Barry Preston does a wonderful fast walk in a routine from Half a Sixpence, but should realise that singing cockney doesn't mean dropping your t's.
