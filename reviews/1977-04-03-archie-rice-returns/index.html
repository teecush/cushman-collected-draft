Apr 3, 1977

Archie Rice Returns

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Archie Rice returns

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the Actors' Company.

WITH half its Arts Council grant about to disappear, the Actors' Company is understandably in turmoil. It has, however, other things to worry about. Three years ago it was a vital, individual and, as seen from the audience, exceptionally likeable organisation. Aesthetically it never strayed far from the middle of the road, but it lent exceptional gaiety to the locale. It has shrunk in spirit, in numbers, and in repertoire. With five plays in a season, across-the-board casting can be fun for everybody. When the board contracts, it seems pointless. There are only two full-length plays in the current list, and that is not enough for us to get the sense of a company.

The truth is of course that the organisation of a company matters far less than its personnel; when the original bright sparks moved on all they had to bequeath was an unwieldy--though not necessarily impracticable--principle. A committee of actors is still a committee, and its failure to come up with any exciting new plays (which are usually the products of individual enthusiasm) is understandable. Even their old plays have mostly been drawn from stock.

In the company's London seasons it has laboured under a further handicap: the looming Wimbledon Theatre, which blighted the rather sprightly version of Pinero's odd romp The Amazons. In John Osborne's The Entertainer, however, it has lit on a play which benefits to some extent from performance in a half-empty barn. If we respond warmly to Archie Rice's routines--the incredibly awful songs and the credibly awful jokes--the point is gone. Ronald Lewis's Archie offers a strikingly stylised image of variety in its death-throes. He is multi-coloured (grey hat, gold jacket, white gloves, red pants) and yet pallid: a puppet pierrot. Nicholas Renton's production makes a fine thing of Archie's preparations for his last performance: a slow, solemn robing followed by the ritual bound on stage to appallingly perky music. The speech that follows may still, in its controlled ashen bitterness, be the best Mr Osborne has written.

But the composition hardly matches up to its coda. One speech for Archie's son (given a tight, explosive delivery by Allan Corduner) exhausts what the play has to say on its removed subject, England; on its immediate subject, the music-hall, it is unconvincing, repeatedly stating a case for the glory of Archie's veteran father without ever demonstrating it.

When his daughter-in-law loses patience with him, it is a supremely welcome moment; it is tied to an actual incident, almost the only one in a wasteland of talk. Phoebe is a good chance for the right actress; and Margery Mason, alternating comfort and hysteria, is on her home ground. These domestic scenes need all the realistic help they can get, but the dramatists' scheme and the wide Wimbledon stage both deny it.

*

William Douglas Home's In the Red (Whitehall) started at the Theatre Royal, Windsor, a theatre which--like Chichester--tends to be more West End than the capital. Here we have our own problems and the anguish of a horse-backing playwright (this is the plot, not the author's biography). Surprised in his £65,000 house by demands that he pay off his £11,000 overdraft is apt to defeat us.

Mr Home in similar plight would probably buckle down and write a few more plays; he has three due in town this year. His hero, though, resolves (a) to play on his bank manager's sympathy and (b) to blackmail him: two plans which seem to me to contradict each other but which he puts into practice simultaneously. The plotting is repetitive and desultory, but the dialogue has pace if nothing else. Gerald Harper is smooth, Dinah Sheridan is gallant. Norman Rossington, playing banker, wins sympathy, both as character and actor.

*

The productions which have looked best at the Lyttelton, other than Bedroom Farce, have been imports: from Lyons, from Berlin, and now from Leicester, whose offering turns out the most exotic of all. The Phoenix Company has brought us The Magic Drum, adapted and directed by Michael Bogdanov from an original by James Kirkup, about a Chinese boy with a talking drum that only he can play. (Anyone remember Sparky and his magic piano?) Summoned by the emperor--a music-fancying tyrant, apparently--he runs away and is drowned, but enjoys a posthumous triumph. Aimed at five- to 11-year-olds, the play will actually enchant anyone who looks and listens. Its use of oriental techniques is the most delightful yet encountered on our stage.

*

There is more to gaze at in the Cambridge Theatre Company's Dog's Dinner, a first play by Robert William, playing this week in Harlow, after a fortnight at the Cambridge Arts. It is set in a rotting many-storeyed house. Jonathan Lynn's production, crisply manoeuvred and acted throughout, gets off to a taking start when a voyeur, spying on his ex-wife next door, tumbles from attic to cellar, setting off laughter, gasps, and a terrific dust-storm. He finds that he has fallen among tramps and beseeches them to kill him. They agree, though he nearly goes back on the idea when he finds that they dispose of corpses by eating them. He ends up crucified and the dialogue goes lightly abusive to match. Edward Petherbridge plays him with grace, but his character is too lightly sketched for us to care about the ramifications.

*

In Manchester the Royal Exchange Company has an uproarious though imperfect time with Present Laughter, which begins as Noel Coward's best play and ends as, at any rate, his most likeable. The limitation of Coward's world to an inner circle of theatricals is here a conscious device rather than the unwitting product of a patronising myopia. The major premise--everybody idolises and/or battens on star actor Gary Essendine--is triumphantly sustained; the minor premise--the comfortable mutual devotion of Gary and his immediate cabal is of permanent value--is far more dubious, and the dodo plot unfortunately depends on it.

Coward was always apt to fall back on priggishly elegant professions of faith, which Albert Finney delivers as he does much of the role--raucously. Mr Finney comes on, always, as an ordinary chap: very well for Hamlet or Tamburlaine, but not for Gary. Your true blue Coward is effortlessly actorish; Mr Finney has to work at it. He clowns engagingly, at times superbly, and carries the audience, but he is more friend than idol.

Gary is essentially sexless, but he competes too strenuously with the grotesques who besiege him. They meanwhile are building up their own excessive heads of steam, except as usual Michael Feast as the apprentice playwright Roland Maule, flinging himself devoutly wherever the Essendine feet may be planted: he is ecstatic. Alone in this company--not counting, on second thoughts, John Quayle's coolly independent manservant--he exercises both boldness and control.
