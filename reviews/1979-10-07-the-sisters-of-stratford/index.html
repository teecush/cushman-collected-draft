Oct 7, 1979

The Sisters Of Stratford

Robert Cushman · The Observer

The Sisters of Stratford

ROBERT CUSHMAN on new productions of Chekhov, Shakespeare and Garrick.

MOST people in the RSC production of Three Sisters (Other Place, Stratford) lead lives of noisy desperation.

The play begins and ends, as it should, with the sisters in a tight group, as if waiting to be photographed. But on the second occasion they are broken. Suzanne Bertish's Masha has already shaken us with the bottled fervour of her farewell to Vershinin, and even more with the silent agony with which she sits through her husband's fumbling attempts to cheer her up.

Emily Richard's Irina takes over with the realisation that they will never escape, which hurts even more than usual since Miss Richard has carefully placed each of her previous cries of 'Moscow' at the apex of some scene of frustration, making them sound like the expression of a good and practicable idea. Finally Janet Dale's Olga, who up to now has been brisk and unobtrusive, delivers her message of hope and, without any obvious undercutting, makes it the cruellest threnody of all. The departure of meaningful life from the town has been made to seem inevitable, and the sisters' own survival almost unbearable.

There have been other tirades to match theirs. As Andrei, Timothy Spall, escaping from the mumbling schizophrenia in which he has specialised since Mary Barnes, talks loudly to the wall or to the deaf old porter as he sets his sights progressively lower--and Mr Spall, beginning tousled and clumsy but quite presentable, marks off every stage of his decline. Griffith Jones, though too gentle an actor to explore the drunken doctor's lowest depths, is nevertheless superb in his aria of self-disgust.

Sob for sob, this is the most moving production of Three Sisters I have seen. Its peaks glisten with the intensity that has become the distinguishing mark of Trevor Nunn's productions, comic or tragic. But comedy here is at a discount. It is true that it runs charmingly through Roger Rees's apologetic Tusenbach, and more subtly through Patrick Godfrey's conciliatory cuckold; it also flowers in such a group-moment as the sly descent of the entire company, playing gooseberry on Andrei's proposal to Natalya. But it is absent elsewhere, and some self-consciousness results.

The great virtue of Jonathan Miller's production a few years ago was that nobody seemed specially aware that they were playing Chekhov; a surprising proportion of the text made sense as ordinary conversation. But Miss Bertish tends to stridency even in repose, and Edward Petherbridge rather drifts through Vershinin. (It occurred to me that for all his well-publicised domestic problems this man suffers less than anyone in the play, but nothing is made of the point.) Bob Peck makes a powerful first statement as the murderous Soliony; if he then has to go on repeating it, that may be Chekhov's fault. But Susan Tracy's Natalya, after a fine first act as a nervous but determined man-catcher, develops into an equally nervous hysteric. She doesn't nag her sisters-in-law; she screams at them. This is interesting and unusual and gives her a good share of the production's strongest moments, but it finally impoverishes the play. It is far more frightening for Andrei and the girls to be up -- as they usually are -- against complacent insensitivity.

I should add that the smallest parts -- the junior officers, the silent maid, and Rose Hill's magnificent old nurse -- are better played than I have ever known.

*

Ralph Koltai's set for Richard III (Olivier) looks at first glance like a couple of sculptures purloined from elsewhere on the South Bank; further gazing reveals that one of them has the profile of an outsize executioner's block, and so indeed it proves. Richard's victims are decapitated on stage, although for Buckingham something nastier has been reserved.

If the sets are an unvaried grey, the costumes mainly ring the changes between grey and black (with the occasional extravagance like dressing Lady Anne's attendant mourners in plastic macs.) This has a cramping effect on the actors, who achieve almost uniform anonymity. I would have expected Basil Henson and Andrew Cruickshank to do wonders for Hastings and Derby, but they merely come on, say their lines fast, and go off again. Mel Martin has become mysteriously elocutionary as Lady Anne. Jeremy Kemp's Buckingham and John Normington's Clarence are good, Yvonne Bryceland's mad Margaret better and Anna Carteret's Elizabeth almost excellent, though both actresses are hampered by an apparent effort to play their scenes of incantatory grief (every son a goner, and all of them named Edward) naturalistically.

I also wondered what caused Margaret to make her first entrance in the middle of a court who had to pretend for ages that they couldn't see her, and why Christopher Morahan arranged his ghosts as a chorus instead of (as in the text) a series of solos. Massed supernatural exits are very hard to stage; this lot shuffled off as if someone in the underworld had just blown the whistle on them.

In the middle of it all John Wood gives his Richard, a performance in which for the first half I was quite unable to believe. Every hypocritical line was topped off by a sardonic glee that would have alerted the most unwary dupe. There is enough space in the soliloquies for Richard to enjoy himself, and these Mr Wood explored with suitable relish. But with the dialogue he seemed uneasy, determined to scale it all down to the cool mockery of his role in Travesties.

There is a limit to how far casualness can go in Shakespeare; I am used (though not resigned) to hearing actors preface each speech with 'well' or 'now'; Mr Wood comes up with 'I mean, cannot a plain man live and think no harm,' dealing a casual death-blow to metre and (using the word in its still valuable sense) decorum.

The second act, as the hunted king, gives more scope to his flair for logical dementia: the feeling of a mind running so fast that it will explode. (Mr Morahan in his treatment of Richard's henchmen comes up with some nice moments of what you might call police-state relevance.) I could accept that this Richard would play feverishly with toy soldiers, though toy drums and trumpets seemed a bit much. Besides there were no signs of incipient madness in what had gone before. Mr Wood seemed obvious casting for Richard, and that should have been a danger signal; he is settling into a mould.

*

The Old Vic is honouring Garrick's bicentenary with a double bill of rarities: an opera, The Padlock (music by Dibdin), which he once produced, and a farce, Miss in Her Teens, the better piece, which he wrote. Each has the same plot: a true lover wins his girl from obviously unsuitable suitors. The heroine of the second, though, has a welcome sense of mischief and -- on my visit the understudy, Vicki Luke, played her delightfully. Nickolas Grace has plum roles in both and his cavorting Negro slave should be seen before the Race Relations Board moves in; his timorous beau should be seen anyway. A strong cast surrounds him, including Benjamin Whitrow, Frank Windsor and Prunella Scales, who, as a prologue, informs us with relish that the second piece 'is a bastard.'

Peter Simpkin's Adam's Apple (New End) is an earnest treatment of the problems of the trans-sexual which falls sequentially into the traps of farce, melodrama and insecure realism. So do the acting and production. A brief post-coital dialogue, before the theme gets going, gives some promise of better things.
