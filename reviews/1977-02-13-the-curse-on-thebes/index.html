The Curse On Thebes

Robert Cushman · The Observer

The curse on Thebes

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

TALKING of chorus lines, the shortest but by no means the least effective in town can be found at Greenwich and consists of Freda Dowie. Slight of build, stern of face, sharp and glancing of voice, Miss Dowie compels attention both as commentator and narrator. She can suggest both the anxieties and sympathies of ordinary humanity and the wrath of most of the gods.

You will gather that the work in hand is a Greek tragedy. It is a rare and late Euripides, named originally for its chorus The Phoenician Women. For us that title is uninformative and the disappearance during rehearsal of an intended partner for Miss Dowie would have made its plural form positively mendacious. The new label is The Sons of Oedipus, which at least lets us know which myth we are in; we have fallen among Thebes.

It does less than justice, though, to the play's scope. Euripides and his heirs (the play was touched up and extended after his death) offer an omnibus edition of the Theban story as rendered by Aeschylus and Sophocles. In this version Oedipus's exile and Jocasta's suicide are postponed until the tragedy of the next generation has been played out, and their sons Polyneices and Eteocles have destroyed each other. Jocasta's opening monologue reaches back to Oedipus's birth, to his killing of his father and his defeat of the Sphinx.

The end of the play anticipates both Oedipus's death at Colonus, and Antigone's defiant burial of her brother Polyneices' body; she, plucky girl, seems all set to perform her role in both dramas simultaneously. En route we have been treated to an incident encountered in no other play: the self-immolation, in obedience to an oracle, of Creon's young son Menoeceus. This enables the authors to bring on, as bearer of the fatal prophecy, old blind Teiresias, without whose threats and grumbles they must have felt any Theban tragedy would be incomplete.

Formally the play is hardly among Euripides's neatest; nor is it distinguished for the satiric bite with which he often rehandled his predecessors' themes. But his most distinctive quality is intact: his inquiring sense of what it might mean to be a real person embroiled in a mythic catastrophe. Few of his characters emerge with much credit (the opposing brothers are a pair of self-justifying warmongers), but they are never sneered at. And as disaster follows disaster, striking everyone on stage and a whole population just off it, one loses the urge to apportion blame.

The curse, so often invoked, on Thebes and on the royal family of Oedipus ceases to be a piece of antique machinery and comes to stand for the agony of existence, to be faced without hysteria and without illusion. This is the tragic sense, and we need it. It even justifies the preliminary screening of horror-paragraphs from current newspapers; not that they illuminate Euripides (how could they when he has a 2000 year start?), but the reverse: he helps us to face them and to grow up.

David Thompson's translation and direction are as clear and sensible as his Tartuffe last year: for Greek tragedy he is, with a good Medea and magnificent Bacchae to his credit, literally the only man. Why he has never directed one of them at the National or RSC, Olympus only knows. (I wrote that sentence 10 years ago, but the gods still keep their counsel.)

This production is disfigured by some fumbling bits of naturalism and more seriously by performances that stay on the level of intelligent recitation. When Frank Barrie arrives to fill the stage physically as well as vocally (this does not mean leaping about) with the news of a triple death we are in another world: we enter it briefly, too, when Ewan Hooper's Creon mourns his son, when Ursula Mohan's Antigone mourns her whole family, when Siobhan McKenna's Jocasta just mourns--though since she does this passim (her inclining, not the play's) the effect is rapidly blunted. Oedipus is held in reserve till the last, a build-up to make Tartuffe's two-act intro seem trifling; when he arrives, in the person of Denys Hawthorne, he is a polite anti-climax. But it is still the biggest play in London.

*

In beginning her direction of Theatre Workshop with O'Casey's The Silver Tassie Clare Venables faces several obstacles, chief among them the extreme badness of the play itself. Its main concern is the indifference of society to its cripples: true enough, but once you've said it you've said it. This theme is dressed up in anti-war uniform; a placard reading 'War is hell' would say as much as the drearily extended symbolism of the second act. Since those who reject the hero have no attitude towards the war, the play has no continuity; lacking consistency of style, character or purpose it falls to pieces wherever you touch it, and the comic interludes are more than usually shameless. It needs to be done boldly with every opportunity seized for stylisation; Miss Venables's direction is timid and her players accordingly desperate. Only Vince Brimble imposes himself; the rest stub their toes in a vacuum. It all looks amateurish.

*

The Last Meeting of the Knights of the White Magnolia (Hampstead) by Preston Jones, of Texas, is better performed (with one exception), but similarly redundant. We meet half-a-dozen rednecks whose fraternity, originally a splinter from the Ku Klux Klan, is clearly both ridiculous and doomed. The rest of the play is devoted to reiterating just how ridiculous and doomed their fraternity is. In making this point the author has the aid of many repeated situations and cliche characters; one of the latter looks just like the placards of Colonel Sanders and is indeed both a colonel and a paste-board figure himself. Other people's iconoclasm can be very tedious.

*

Hampstead's honour is saved by the transfer of Pam Gems's Dusa, Stas, Fish and Vi (Mayfair), which, in Nancy Meckler's production, is gorgeous entertainment, full of sharp and stylish corners. Its buoyancy, though, is at odds with its suicidal ending; it is ironic that this much-touted feminist play should restore that by no means unfamiliar and decidedly unliberated figure, the girl who kills herself because her man has done her wrong. Two propositions that Miss Gems may not fully endorse are none the less floating around in her play: (a) that only women suffer and (b) that all their suffering is due to men.

The proffered antidote of female solidarity lacks dramatic force because its realistic basis--the flat-sharing routine--is never established. The girls-together title looks in fact like a fashionable blind: Dusa is a contrivance; Stas and Vi are comic relief, and the author's favourite dish is obviously Fish. But she is a creation; as played by Alison Fiske, a magnificent one. A rich girl on the Left, she delivers a lecture on Rosa Luxemburg with inescapable condescension, her head cocked as she makes her points. A proud woman in a state of sexual dependence, she has to keep her own intelligence at arm's length, and the impossibility kills her.

As the play proceeds, she gets wittier and more driven; she can shrug with her lip. Her movements become more extreme; she prowls the stage with her back arched. Meanwhile her words pour out, until the news of her lover's final perfidy silences her: in a startlingly erotic moment her hand goes up between her legs. Miss Fiske is an actress critics regularly re-discover; after this seething, churning, glowing performance, it should never again be necessary.
