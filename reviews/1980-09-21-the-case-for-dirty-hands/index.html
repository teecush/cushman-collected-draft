Sep 21, 1980

The Case For Dirty Hands

Robert Cushman · The Observer

The case for dirty hands

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Exeter.

IT is possible at Lillian Hellman's Watch on the Rhine (Lyttelton) to appreciate the impact it had on its audiences in 1941.

The play is an arraignment of American isolationism. It addresses itself to, and concerns itself with, the well-heeled. A Washington widow, bright and liberal, and her middle-aged son, similar, are visited by the daughter of the family and her husband, a German who has spent the previous seven years as an anti-Nazi agent.

He hears that three of his comrades have been captured, and prepares to return in order to obtain their release by bribing the guards. ("It has been done before.") First, though, he has to deal with another emigre, a Romanian count who has discovered his identity and is blackmailing him. He kills his man; and his in-laws, confronted by the deed, realise its necessity and become accessories to it.

The point is not that they have learned the truth about the nature of Fascism--they already knew it--but that they have discovered how to fight it. The implication is that America can afford to dirty its hands a little. ("I'm not put together with flour paste," says mother to son. "And neither are you--I am happy to learn.")

Three cheers for that old New England stock. For the Washington drawing-room where the action happens could be in any comfortable cultured Yankee city. For 20 years it was the scene of every American play that was neither broad farce nor grinding expose. Even now, as it approaches dilapidation, Edward Albee can call it home. It is the ground on which middlebrow Americans expected to see issues debated, wisecracks exchanged, marriages saved or sundered. Miss Hellman obliges on all these counts. In doing so she hit her audience where it lived, or at least where it would like to have lived, but she also risked becoming a slave of the mechanism she employed.

That, in fact, is what happens. The play centres on Jessica the chatelaine and we are shown her relations with her butler, with her lady companion and with the memory of her worshipped husband. Some of this may be necessary to clue us into the character and milieu, but it threatens to run off with the play--or rather to lumber off with it; since these are not really the things on her mind, Miss Hellman cannot invest them with much urgency.

The blackmailer's marital problems likewise appear as a heavy-handed method of letting us know that he is a cad. We are kept waiting a long time for the exciting, and perfectly valid, melodrama that is Miss Hellman's proper medium. For us too there is the knowledge that, though she was doubtless more aware than most people, she either didn't know the half or wasn't telling. It is impossible to believe that captured resisters, high on the Nazi wanted lists, could be rescued, or that they would be left alive long enough for anyone to try. The blandness the author castigates has invaded her play.

The acting in Mike Ockrent's production is mostly excellent. Peggy Ashcroft times her characterisation immaculately; it is not complete until she has spoken her last dismissively determined line, whose ingredients are salt, ice and perfect breeding. John Quayle, gangling over her, troubled but confident, is the shy bookman to the quietly philandering life. Of the intruders, Susan Engel's black-avised calm modulates beautifully into caring warmth, while David Burke is a shambling marvel of a hero.

*

Rodgers and Hammerstein's Oklahoma (Palace), which first reached Broadway two years later, is a completer work. The songs may not carry the story--it is light enough to need no help beyond the dialogue's--but they are responsible for mood and colour, for something of character, and (in the title song and "The Farmer and the Cowman") for the theme: the opening-up of the West.

It is undeniably odd (and nothing to do with accents) to hear British performers declare quite so ringingly and repeatedly that the land they belong to is grand; it's like pillaging a national anthem. But when these performers hurl their bodies through the air in the second act barn-dance they are magnificent. I don't remember this exhilaration from last year's Leicester opening; all the dancing in fact has improved beyond recognition. The dream ballet has gained in force and point; the ensemble of aspiring flirts in "Many a New Day" is enchanting; everyone acts and reacts fit to bust.

The songs may be inserted a trifle stolidly into the script, but once there they irradiate their context and each other. Recurring in fragments they make a musical world, and a theatrical one. The balladeering in this production is riper than we are used to. John Diedrich's Curly makes a stirring noise and Rosamund Shelley's Laurey a pretty one, but she can be flat and he florid, declaring three times that everythin's yoin' his way, and once that the breeze is so busy it don't miss a tray.

Acting is done by Alfred Molina's dark and disturbing Jud Fry, Linal Haft's funny shrugging peddler, and Madge Ryan's Aunt Eller, churning her butter in the opening tableau so serenely that she obviously knows she is part of a myth. The legend is secured for me in the second song, "The Surrey with the Fringe on Top," when she and Laurey, hypnotised, echo Curly's "Don't you wisht y'd go on ferever." Yes.

*

Maybe the bravest production of the year has been in Exeter Cathedral, where the Northcott Company tried Hardy's Napoleonic epic The Dynasts (three parts, dozens of acts, hundreds of scenes). The evening began dispiritingly; first came an interpolated folk-dirge about all the fighting being at the expense of the labouring man, which may be true but has nothing to do with the case presented by Hardy who was then allowed his first line, hideously mis-scanned. ("What of the immanent Will and Its design?" What indeed?) It is doubtful if anyone unfamiliar with the text knew what the inimitably turgid choric spirits were about. The driven humans too were often caricatured (mad, sick, terrified George III allowed no pathos), but Ian Reddington's Bonaparte emerged as an engaging, even innocent, opportunist and the others gradually got into line.

Hardy's extraordinary cinematographic stage-directions ("the various battalions are marched away in their respective directions") had, of course, to be ignored; adaptor-designer Hugh Durrant and director Crispin Thomas shaped what remained as a pageant. Each warring nation was equipped with its own booth, and they met and contended on a ramp stretched the length of the nave. We, surrendering to the size and ambition and even to some of the pity and terror, tracked the action, Cottesloe-promenade style. If ever the National want a piece to exercise all three of its auditoria simultaneously, this is it.
