Mar 20, 1977

Fun In The Suburbs

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Fun in the suburbs

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

I MET an actor who was juggling offers; should he accept a role in Alan Ayckbourn's Bedroom Farce at the National Theatre or in the same author's 'Just Between Ourselves', shortly to open in the West End? I felt that I had been vouchsafed a glimpse of the theatre of the future.

He's taking over, all right. 'Bedroom Farce' at the Lyttelton introduces us to Mr Ayckbourn the director (credit shared with Peter Hall), hitherto unknown outside of Scarborough and a very sharp and welcome operator. This is one of the best balanced accounts of his plays to reach London. The eight characters come out equal, though I feel inclined myself to single out one group.

Joan Hickson has emerged over the past few years as one of our most valuable senior comediennes, the spirit of suburban disenchantment. Yoked here in marriage to Michael Gough (gently cadaverous with reserves of patience that he kids himself are about to run dry), she exudes a kind of narrow tolerance, something short of primness on the one side and of outright kindliness on the other.

She has perhaps some reason to be complacent; her marriage has lasted, which is more than seems likely for the three younger couples who complete the cast. One notices, though, that all of them are living more or less in accord with the advice Miss Hickson's Delia claims to have received from her own mother: 'If SEX ever rears its ugly head, close your eyes before you see the rest of it.' But though manners may not have changed, morals have, and so they feel either guilty or deprived.

Trust Mr Ayckbourn to give such a title to a play in which adultery is not only avoided, but barely contemplated. There are three bedrooms on stage, each occupied by a lawful pair trying to get some sleep. That they cannot is the fault of the fourth team, Trevor and Susannah, who are also married, but are the type rarely to be found at home in bed, or at home at all. They live, emotionally, off their friends, which is a very good way of making enemies. They impose themselves, apart from anything else, through sheer bulk.

Susannah is Maria Aitken, one of the tallest actresses in the land, very scared and continually reciting desperate mantras of reassurance ('I am not unattractive. I am attractive'). Trevor, alas, is her male equivalent, unable to reassure anybody, vague and accident-prone, and clad for much of the time (a fiendish ingenuity of Mr Ayckbourn's) in a coat some sizes too bulky for him. One of his achievements is to lean on a nondescript piece of furniture whose owner has Doomed Himself on this same endless night. It collapses, in beautiful slow motion, and Stephen Moore as Trevor somehow manages to convey that the sloth is his fault as much as the collapse; both indeed are a part of him.

Derek Newark is the home-handyman whose final fury is left to our boggling imagination, Susan Littler his patient spouse: patient at least with everyone but him. The ritziest room is occupied by the creamily witty Polly Adams, and by Michael Kitchen; not hitherto my idea of a light comedian, who plays a brilliant variation on his usual thrusting young executive, this one being laid up with a bad back. His misadventures (it hurts when he moves, and people keep disturbing him) raise the loudest laughs of the evening.

Otherwise it is not, in decibel terms, an enormously hilarious evening; the main pleasure at the time comes from seeing Mr Ayckbourn solve his latest puzzle, threading his story through the three rooms with only one lapse in credibility towards the end. In retrospect, though, one admires the psychological patterning as much as the technical. The relationships illuminate one another as they interlock; a stray barb fixes each one; Trevor and Susannah acting as twin catalysts. Unfortunately, though, they also need a scene together and here Mr Ayckbourn deserts allusiveness for head-on collision, composed, it seemed to me, of scraps from his previous plays. Once again, he has failed to do full justice to his own talent; but it is something to have the talent.

Mr Ayckbourn, though one forgets it, is not a metropolitan playwright: his plays belong to a town in which people know one another. This is not maybe what we think of as community theatre, but what we think of as community theatre is not generally engaging. There are acceptable variations and the very lively Duke's Playhouse in Lancaster has found one in Philip Martin's Sambo. This, based on a local legend, reveals to us that Lancaster's prosperity was based on the slave-trade: a local fact. It proceeds to rhyme the past with the prejudiced present. It is part historical reconstruction, part theatrical games-playing, part modern documentary. No part is wholly successful (the last is least) but the whole is intriguing. John Blackmore directs, imaginatively, one of the best teams in the regions.

A couple of years ago I used Snoo Wilson's Vampire as text or pretext for an attack on what I called 'the sins of the fringe'. He and I and the fringe itself have, I hope, all moved on a bit since then; so indeed has the play which, in its revival at the Bush, sports a completely new third act. It also boasts some smashing acting, particularly from Clive Merrison with high fanatical forehead, Patti Love jumping as if electrically programmed, and Neil Johnston reassuringly down-to-earth and a phenomenally spacious production by Dusty Hughes, demonstrating again the value to Mr Wilson's interpreters of a clear head and a sense of humour. (Especially the latter: on a strict laugh-count the play is funnier than 'Bedroom Farce'.)

There remains, however, a disconcerting brittleness about the play; it pursues across three generations a conceit--dead ideas sucking the blood of the living--to which it gives wit but not flesh. The characters (especially the Victorians of the first act) are patronised; and the play is pervaded by a mocking self-congratulatory toughness.

The dead authors most recently on view have admittedly gone some way towards proving Mr Wilson's thesis. James Bridie's Tobias and the Angel (Young Vic) demonstrates the surviving conviction that a Biblical subject is good for children and (witness 'Jesus Christ Superstar' and Chichester's gala in reviving Obey's 'Noah') for trade. The Tobias story, being Apocryphal, may not count, though its treatment here illustrates the linked belief that winsomeness is next to wholesomeness. The home-life of the travelling Tobias seems to have been blighted by a father of stupefying saintliness; once the show gets on the road it improves a bit. Bridie, though, must have done better than this.

Twelfth Night (Greenwich), pretty but torturingly slow, fails to transmute text into enchantment; though in Anna Carteret it has the complete Olivia, melting into life, and in Frank Barrie a subtly narcissistic Orsino. His picturesque poses in the first scene are so neatly satirised that it was a shock to find the sailors in the second step up in all seriousness to the same tricks. Gayle Hunnicutt smiles through Viola, Max Wall grimaces through Malvolio; neither to much effect.
