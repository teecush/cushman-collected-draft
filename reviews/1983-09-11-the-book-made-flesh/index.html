Sep 11, 1983

The Book Made Flesh

Robert Cushman · The Observer

The book made flesh

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on two stage adaptations of Dostoyevsky.

BRITONS never, never shall be Slavs; so at least the legend goes. We have no reason to be ashamed of our record with Chekhov, but Dostoyevsky put on the stage--as, despite all impossibilities, he constantly is--is another thing.

His characters are on a constant switchback between self-assurance and self-abasement. `Prince,' they will say to someone to whom they have just been introduced, `I am unworthy to touch the hem of your garment, and by the way will you lend me 20 kopecks?' You can play them from the outside and make them grotesques, but that is no answer. Getting inside them, though, must be like boarding an express train between stations.

In Crime and Punishment (Lyric, Hammersmith), directed by Yuri Lyubimov of the Moscow Taganka Theatre, with a British cast, everyone gets on and hardly anyone falls off. This is high-grade ensemble work extracted from an ad hoc company: a distinguished team who would be well worth keeping together.

The production is continuously, even monotonously, exciting; Dostoyevsky may actually be too theatrical for the theatre. Lyubimov has apparently re-created the design, the lighting, the music and sound effects of his Moscow production. They are all hallucinatory; and I suspect that he imposed a performing style by exposing his actors to them. How many ways are there of acting in a maelstrom? These are not, however, generalised performances; and though fast they are not rushed. The amount of detailed intelligence applied to the dialogue would be impressive in any circumstance; in a production directed through an interpreter it is extraordinary.

Lyubimov's adaptation is less radical than has been suggested. I may be corrected by those who have read the novel more recently than I have, but as far as I can see he jumps in at Raskolnikov's murder of the two old women and carries steadily on from there. True, he proceeds in short, abrupt scenes, and he cross-cuts from one group of characters to another, but so does the book.

Our own brand of self-abasement has been at work in the reviews; the subterranean message has been that this is really all much different from, and far superior to, `Nicholas Nickleby.' Well it isn't; in fact--since I would back Roger Rees as a hero and John Woodvine as a grand and complex villain against anyone on the Lyric stage--it isn't as good.

It is still fine. It is dominated by doors that have an acrobatic life of their own, turning somersaults to confound the characters who lurk behind them; by dreamlike processionals; and by a harsh, aggressive use of lights; Michael Pennington as Raskolnikov begins the evening by shining one of them at us. This is all thrilling, but it feels--not at all to its discredit--a bit old-fashioned. It is what an Eastern European production of a certain avant-garde stripe is supposed to look like. (It is also, from what I have read, the kind of thing that Peter Brook was doing in London in the 1940s.)

It is not, though, just technique for its own sake. It is at the service of an interpretation--a condemnation of the theorist who puts himself above morality. Nothing here bites harder than Mr Pennington's final gloomy citation, out of character, of a schoolgirl who thought Raskolnikov perfectly admirable.

His performance before that is technique for its own sake. No, that's not fair; it is a skilled and conscientious approach to a fiendish acting problem, one that avoids any suggestion of the inverted holy fool. But it involves him in more shouting than should be required of any actor; and in bypassing the compassion that should make Raskolnikov a criminal worth contemplating.

Indeed when you start to break them down individually, several of the performances disappoint. Among other fine actors Bill Stewart is jerky, Peter Kelly invisible. Gary Waldhorn finds a sourly jocular way of putting over Svidrigailov, the hero's distorted reflection; Paola Dionisotti has a fine mad scene; and Bill Paterson as the inspector--traditionally the most playable role--is a quirky mixture of ministering and avenging angel. He has brought his Schweyk walk with him; when he says `forgive me for moving about like this,' he brings a section of the house down.

Sonia, traditionally the least playable role--the prostitute as redemptress--is splendidly done by Veronica Roberts, who never gives an inch to mawkishness. Best of all, aggressively dejected in a single scene at the beginning, is Christopher Guinee as the doomed drunk Marmeladov (did someone say that he was `In a jam' or was I hallucinating?); he is a page of the book made flesh.

*

Nastasya Filippovna (Riverside), Andrzej Wajda's adaptation of Dostoyevsky's The Idiot, also begins with a murder: the one at the end of the story. When we are permitted to enter the room, Nastasya's two lovers are already locked in converse. Jan Nowicki is the killer Rogozhin, ceaselessly padding the floor; Jerzy Radziwilowicz is the saintly Myshkin, white-suited and apparently contained before falling into terminal catatonia.

These Polish actors convince you that there are areas of the soul into which none of our players could penetrate. But a caveat: not knowing Polish, I have no means of measuring their intensity against what they are actually supposed to be saying; the acid test we automatically apply on home ground. To me it sounds terrific, but it could be the merest rant.

*

Back at the Lyric Studio (aspiring critics are advised to rent a villa in Hammersmith) is Shelter, a double bill of black-and-white studies by Caryl Phillips. In the first, stylishly written and (by Jules Wright) authoritatively directed, an eighteenth-century English lady is shipwrecked along with a Negro, whom she calls `ape.' As well as ape we get essence; this is a political morality play.

The second bit takes us to a London pub in 1958; it deals more prosaically with miscegenation and is less interesting, perhaps because plays set in pubs are far easier to come by than plays set on desert islands. Rudolph Walker, the man in both plays, is very relaxed; the women are Kathryn Pogson, an exceptionally alert actress whose refusal to be type-cast as a waif is one of the few hopeful things to happen in the 1980s.

*

David Gooderson's The Killing of Mr Toad (King's Head) is another play requiring literary knowledge. It deals with the married problems of Kenneth Grahame--which were serious without being desperate--and with the hopes of the Grahames for their young son who ended it all on a railway-line while still a student.

He apparently inspired Toad; and one fault of the play is that it makes him the centre of attention (as Toad's friends always had to) when the man we are really interested in is Grahame himself, seen timidly as Mole. Rat and Badger are family friends; and Mrs Grahame remembers the whole thing.

I wish Mr Gooderson had been more rigorous and more inventive with his Wind in the Willows framework and that he had eschewed parody songs; pointless since none of the characters is musical. But the first half is both well written and well edited; and Peter Watson gives it a clever and sensitive production. Deborah Norton is a vinegary raisonneuse, Robert Austin a veritable supermole and John Warner, Hugh Sullivan and Rupert Graves do the other honours honourably.
