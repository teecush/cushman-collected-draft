The Visit

Robert Cushman · New Cambridge

The Visit

Reviewed by Robert Cushman

Nobody could reasonably accuse 'The Visit' of obscurity. The greenbacks flourished in our faces at the end only make tangible what Durrenmatt has been drumming in our ears all along: that this is a play about selling-out. Obediently we switch on our collective-guilt reflexes and cower in our seats. It's microcosm time again, and Guellen, where money buys justice, is guaranteed to scorch our consciences.

But I am developing scorch-resistance. Neat little parables worked out by characters bizarre or generalised (the Schoolmaster, the Priest) are too easy, and soothe the wounds they pretend to mortify. Money goes hand in hand with deceit—even Claire's limbs are artificial, and she makes humanity her playthings. At which we all murmur 'How true' and go home untouched and unrepentant.

And yet, as a theatrical mechanism, the play works. The mixture of Grand Guignol and eccentric hilarity is its own reward and, feeding upon itself, sprouts vigorously throughout the evening. The effect is of a hectically sick cartoon, with built-in social significance.

As such, it presents its director with a formidable stylistic problem, which Rob Knights solves for the Mummers with something like triumph. He tends to over-build factitious climaxes, and cogency is lacking in the middle stretches, but his overall touch is masterfully sure. Sets, lighting, and sound—all unusually good—are integrated with a rare feeling for mood and tempo.

He is particularly successful with the visitors. Lis Rietberg's magnificent Claire suspends disbelief from exactly the right altitude, and her entourage—from Jeffrey Tate's doom-laden butler through the capering castrati to Tim Davies' comic precision as the husbands—runs the play's horror-comic gamut in miniature.

The Guelleners are fine in concert but lack conviction as individuals. David Lascelles and Ian Barritt get the right metallic ring in small parts, but Bernard Krichefski's querulous schoolmaster is the only wholly successful principal. The penalty of Durrenmatt's method is that approximations will not do; thus the control and assurance of Roger Gartland's Mayor are negated by his lack of weight. And David Collins plays Ill frankly against the grain of the play: he has some marvellous moments, but his attempts to make a three-dimensional hero out of a one-dimensional victim result inevitably in an uncentred performance.
