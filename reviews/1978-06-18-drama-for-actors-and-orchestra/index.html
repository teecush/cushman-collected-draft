Jun 18, 1978

Drama For Actors And Orchestra

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Drama for actors and orchestra

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on plays by Stoppard, Wesker and Strindberg.

WHEN the Tom Stoppard-Andre Previn piece Every Good Boy Deserves Favour (Mermaid) first surfaced as a one-night bash at the Royal Festival Hall, I complained that it contained scenes anyone might have written. I am now not sure what I meant. I don't think that I was trying to father any of the dialogue on Mr Previn, or for that matter any of the orchestral interludes on Mr Stoppard. But I was bothered by the new aura of respectability that seemed to have descended on the playwright: by the spectacle of Mr Stoppard finally getting in line and writing about a specific political abuse -- in this case the confinement of Russian dissidents in mental hospitals. We are not short of plays about oppression (though the ones we generally see are not set in Russia) and the scenes in which the boy Sasha, whose father Alexander Ivanov is just such a prisoner of conscience, is bullied and harangued by his schoolteacher, came over as routine slices of protest.

Blame the verbal (as opposed to musical) acoustics of the Festival Hall, or just my obtuseness. These scenes, though more sparely written than we expect of Mr Stoppard, are in his authentic echo-chamber style, re-stating and re-arranging themes from elsewhere in the play. The continual exhilarating paradox of his work is that its linguistic exuberance always turns out to be strictly functional. Every joke doubles back on itself. Even without a band behind him, he is the most musical playwright we have.

All the same, the actual choice or alignment of themes varies in conviction from play to play. 'EGBDF' was born, we are told, of a compulsion (write about dissidence) and a commission (write for actors and orchestra). Dissidence suggests dissonance; and Ivanov shares his cell with a namesake, a real madman who believes he owns an orchestra. But their association seems to be more a matter of the authors than of their jailers. It lacks the inevitability of the philosopher-gymnast equation in Jumpers. (The philosopher-footballer tie-up in Professional Foul falls somewhere between the two.) The musical patter is mostly beguiling, though the inclusion of the glockenspiel joke ('Not to speak of the glockenspiel.' 'The glockenspiel?' 'I asked you not to speak of it') suggests that the playwright's imagination was working at less than full pressure.

Still, Stoppard at his second-best surpasses most playwrights at their first. Ivanov's encounters with the psychiatrist (Frank Windsor) who will declare him sane if he admits to madness recall Catch-22; but it is probably impossible to write comprehensively of the modern world without doing that. The same psychiatrist is a part-time violinist, which makes his attempts to grapple with his other patient's delusions doubly fruitful.

Ivanov's account of the reasons for his incarceration is, in John Woodvine's stoical performance, both funny and moving -- and exactly judged for being spoken against self. So is, or was, music. And the ending is majestic. The brilliant doctor of semantics who has ordered the joint confinement of the two Ivanovs ends up by mistaking one for the other and releasing both. To the pleasure of seeing authority hoist with its own petard (and a hopeful comment on totalitarian stupidity) is added the satisfaction of poetic justice -- but the music tells us that it is only poetic. It is not how things usually happen. This has all the irony of the Threepenny Opera finale, without the grinding obviousness.

Ian McDiarmid gives a sprightly account of the musical maniac, whose delusions seem to have lost some of their grandeur. Once he owned the LSO; now he has a smaller outfit known appropriately (since they all sport tails) as the Mermaid Chamber Orchestra. The scaling-down, to my ear at least, actually enhances Mr Previn's score. A final point: if a playwright wishes to fire us against injustice or the waste of life he must first convince us, in the quality of his own work, that life is worth living anyway. Mr Stoppard's gaiety is a moral quality in itself.

*

So is, or was, Arnold Wesker's; the enduring quality of his Chicken Soup trilogy has proved to be its family comedy -- which, since Mr Wesker writes of all his characters as though they were his relations, has infinitely wide reverberations. The farm-working Bryants of Roots are as rounded, and as respectfully-treated, as the Jewish, Socialist Kahns of Chicken Soup with Barley, who have more of the right ideas but are hardly more efficient at putting them into practice. It is even arguable that Mrs Bryan's apathy does less harm to her offspring than Mrs Kahn's commitment; at least Mr Wesker seems more optimistic about Beatie Bryant's future than about anyone else's.

The third instalment, I'm Talking About Jerusalem (Shaw), is, despite lively passages, an anti-climax. It returns us to the Kahns' daughter Ada and her husband Dave, who move to Norfolk after the war to make and sell furniture. After 15 years, they acknowledge failure and return to London. The play, like all such finales, has to devote some of its energy to mopping-up. This makes for untidiness, but also for the best writing of the evening: if we crane forward for news of characters from the other plays.

Ada's horrified account of a visit to her stricken father is, as delivered by Adrienne Posta, the most powerful sequence. But the play disappoints, not because its upshot is pessimistic but because that depression is never faced or argued out. Confrontations are promised but not delivered. The acting, too, Miss Posta apart, is weak; play and production hinder each other. The Shaw revivals have always lagged behind the detailed realism the scripts demand; here the direction has not caught up, but the play has dropped back to meet it.

*

Strindberg fans are always trying to sell their idol's plays as comedies; the RSC revival of The Dance of Death does this more whole-heartedly than any before, more than the National Theatre version which was hampered by the difference in scale between Laurence Olivier and Geraldine McEwan. Sheila Allen and Emrys James (whose performance, though complete in itself, suggests an inadvertent parody of Olivier's) are far better matched; each takes it in turn to deride the other's boasted sufferings.

The trick to getting laughs from Strindberg is to play fast, so that the miseries always seem outpaced by the lamentations; the disparity can be very funny. I enjoyed it very much, though I doubt if the author would have. The news that Mr James (as his surrogate) had expired murmuring 'forgive them for they know not what they do' brought the Aldwych down. But Strindberg, of course, was imagining for himself a moment of stunning nobility; he had the genius to recognise his own self-pity, but that never stopped him indulging it.

*

Marivaux was a far more disturbing (because outwardly more dispassionate) observer of the sexes: a butterfly with steel wing-tips. His L'Heureux Stratageme has been revived -- premiered, rather -- at Chichester, done into pastiche Restoration prose by John Bowen with a title -- The Inconstant Couple -- to match. There is obvious strain involved for adapter, players and audience; but there are also rewards. In particular, Keith Baxter gives a finely puzzled account of an honest man amazed at the lengths and depths to which he will go to win the hand of a flirtatious spouse.

The lady in question is Sian Phillips, cast against her stage personality, which is resolutely opposed to artifice. Morag Hood, however, confirms herself the wittiest classical comedienne to emerge since Maggie Smith. The other performances in Noel Willman's production deploy stock period devices, but never without thought or indeed without effect.
