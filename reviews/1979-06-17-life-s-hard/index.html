Jun 17, 1979

Life's Hard

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Life's hard

Theatre ROBERT CUSHMAN in Stratford and London

The only disco bit I have ever liked was Boney M's 'Rasputin,' and that mainly for its coda: a witty, admiring, exhausted cry, 'Oh, those Russians.'

As a reviewer, confronted by the repertory of the Royal Shakespeare Company, I know just how Boney M feel.

A Chekhov is promised, and a Gorky rumoured, for later this year; meanwhile The Suicide (Other Place, Stratford) continues our education in the theory and practice of Stalinist drama criticism, begun a couple of weeks ago by The White Guard. After a play that Stalin permitted, we have one that he obstructed: to such effect that though the author, Nikolai Erdman, lived apparently until 1970, he never wrote another play.

This one was written in 1928; hopes of staging it in Russia were finally abandoned in 1932. One can see Stalin's point of view; it is a wildly subversive piece, culminating in a shattering speech--superbly delivered here by Roger Rees--in which the protagonist, having decided that he doesn't think he'll kill himself today, expands his philosophy of life. 'Our achievements, our success, our reconstructed society, you can keep it. I want a quiet life and a living wage.' He goes on to demand the most inalienable of human rights, the right to grumble. 'Allow us at least to whisper life is hard . . . you won't even hear us.'

I suspect that any contemporary British writer who attempted to put so unrepentant an individualist on to the stage of, let us say, the Warehouse, would get rather short shrift. Erdman's hero dismisses society as 'a slogan factory,' and slogan factories are what our progressive playwrights, if we are to believe their declarations, would like to make of our theatres. And then consider the postman in Erdman's play who sees everything 'from the Marxist point of view' and only wants to read about postmen. Some Socialist companies have been looking for an audience like him for years.

These pleasures are not all that the play offers; if they were, it would only be a more attractive slogan-factory than most. Semyon, the would-be suicide, takes the entire length of the play to find his own voice. He begins, demoralised by long unemployment, nagging and terrorising his wife and mother-in-law. He is immensely impressionable; before he can entertain the idea of self-slaughter someone else has accidentally to put it into his head.

From then on, he is a universal prey; all sectors of Russian society converge, asking him to die for their cause. He may martyr himself for the discontented intelligentsia, for art or for the love of a woman. Death seems a flattering as well as a soothing fate; if only he could bring himself actually to pull the trigger. The play becomes 'to be or not to be,' acted out as farce. It is appropriate that one of the funniest sequences should turn on a misunderstood line from Hamlet, and that the end of the play--which brilliantly wrenches the action into a wider and bitterer focus--should parody that of Chekhov's Hamlet variant, The Seagull.

The play looks forward to the comedy of survival, as currently practised in Czechoslovakia, and back to that of Gogol (Semyon receiving his suitors like the Government Inspector), with whom it shares a certain prolixity. Ron Daniels's production is slow to get going and Mr Rees is probably more personable casting than the author envisaged for Semyon. The early routine grumblings do not sit well on him; only when he has a tangible object--a tuba, as it happens--and a solid comic situation on which to exercise his spleen does his performance spring into life. He is well supported by Lila Kaye as his ma-in-law and by Nigel Terry as chief tempter. Play and production could do with room to breathe; they should transfer to the Aldwych.

*

At the Stratford main house is a Twelfth Night that takes its title seriously. The first half is played against a bare and wintry treescape; at the height of the carousal scene Sir Andrew hangs up a paper-chain, which Malvolio, his puritanism outraged by such fripperies, promptly dislodges. We return from the interval, though, to find that spring has arrived; it seems to have taken the Illyrian tailors a couple of months to deliver Malvolio his yellow stockings.

Terry Hands's production trips itself up in other ways. Feste--acting presumably as a Lord of Misrule--oversees every scene; but why then should he, like everyone else, get the twins mixed up? And the lovesick absurdities of Orsino and Olivia are, not for the first time at Stratford, overplayed. Kate Nicholls's Olivia, emerging from behind a yashmak into unrestrained flapperhood, is a brave and intermittently delightful performance, but no actress should be required to giggle and gesticulate quite so often.

But there are virtues, and these become more apparent as the evening proceeds. One is Jane Downs's Maria, never quite sure of herself with Willoughby Goddard's heartless Sir Toby and most touchingly crushed when the news of their marriage is greeted with laughter by the juvenile leads--those who, according to the peculiar class-structure of comedy, think love their own prerogative. Another is Cherie Lunghi's Viola, no revelation but charming nonetheless. Above all there is John Woodbine's Malvolio, who makes more of the man's convictions than any I have seen.

His natural enmity with Feste (Geoffrey Hutchings) crackles with unusual force; so do his dreams of humiliating his kinsman Toby. Olivia's letter upsets his whole scheme of values; 'I will smile' is a grimly desperate statement of intent, whose achievement fully deserves its round of applause. The antic scene is, physically and mentally, the most daring in my experience; at the end, the Puritan again, he can utter his last line as a serious threat to everyone on stage: a threat which, as has been remarked before, history was to vindicate. This is a serious, foursquare and supremely funny performance.

*

Flowers for Algernon replaces Tommy at the Queen's, and has much in common with it. Instead of a deaf-and-dumb boy turned superstar, we have a retarded young man turned by brain-surgery to a genius. Where Tommy offered lasers, this has the play of coloured light on a vast steely maze lowered from the flies. (I name this style medical chic.) Flowers, unlike Tommy, is a work of decent professionalism, but if this story is not to be unbearably mawkish it has to be kept icy-pure, and musicalising it is no way to do that.

The numbers, quite naturally, beg for applause and everything turns cute. Besides, Michael Crawford in his retarded stage is indistinguishable from his usual backward hero. It is a pleasure to see him cope so well with the prodigy stage (I am doubtful whether a genius would sing these lyrics) and he is unsentimental when the experiment slips and the hero regresses. The hero, by the way, is not called Algernon. Algernon is a mouse. They do a song-and-dance together. It's a nice one (and Charles Strouse has supplied a couple of good ballads as well), but it demonstrates exactly what has gone wrong with the show.

Nothing much has gone wrong with the lavish revival of The King and I (Palladium), even though Virginia McKenna is an improbably demure, for a Victorian empire-building lady. Yul Brynner still makes an authoritative king; and his part is actually very well written. He's a real comic creation and, in his attempt to reconcile two cultures in his own head, a touching one as well.

The use of children may irritate you ('Annie' is a stringent comparison), but Jerome Robbins's choreography comes to the rescue, and the songs are still, mostly, lovely. Rodgers and Hammerstein at their best wrote well-made musicals the way Rattigan wrote well-made plays; we can't and shouldn't emulate them, but we can't deny that they still work infallibly.
