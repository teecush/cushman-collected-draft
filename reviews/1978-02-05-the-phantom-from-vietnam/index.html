Feb 5, 1978

The Phantom From Vietnam

Robert Cushman · The Observer

The phantom from Vietnam

WE HAVE been a long time coming to the plays of David Rabe; or rather they have been a long time coming to us. He is the prize playwright of the Vietnam generation in America; and Sticks and Bones (New End) is the central play of a trilogy inspired, though the word sounds inappropriate, by the war.

It fits into a genre that was fairly common in Britain after World War One: the returned-soldier play. On one side the disillusioned young veteran: on the other his blinkered family. Fifty years ago, the playwright would have contrived some rapprochement between the two sides, but it has become a cardinal rule of the American family drama that the generations will never get together.

The family unit here is the classic one of Death of a Salesman and Long Day's Journey into Night: Father, mother, and two sons. As in Salesman, Junior allies himself with his parents against the unconforming elder boy, who in this case has returned from the war blinded but in one sense clearer-sighted than anyone else on stage. One Vietnamese girl has forced him to realise the humanity of the gooks he was sent out to fight, and her memory still follows him around: a phantom in his mind, a presence on the stage.

His family can cope with the physical disability; they smother him with attention. If this were a Jewish play, they'd be drowning him in chicken soup; being Wasp, they run more to fudge and cookies. It's his enlightenment, his savage distance from them, that fazes them; like the truth-tellers from another strain of American drama, he has to go. He is persuaded, gently and affectionately, to slash his own wrists.

The play is one in which a fantasy action is given so realistic a background that the audience is given no chance to dismiss it. You can, of course, reject the whole play, but Mr Rabe writes too well for that to be an easy option. Premiered in 1971, in an impasse which showed no signs of ever being resolved, the piece makes great demands on a British audience.

The strain is intensified at the New End by the intimacy of the theatre; we are never afforded the release of a good old-fashioned shouting-match. But, though muted, the actors are very good, especially Don Fellows as the father, propelled through most of the theatrical traumas that the well-meaning, guilt-ridden, middle-aged American male is traditionally heir to.

The bill at the Glasgow Citizens always looks enticing; it could be a work you never thought you'd see or a new one with an unbelievable subject. That is the category for Summit Conference, written by the Citizens' associate director, Robert David Macdonald, and directed by its designer, Philip Prowse. The meeting takes place offstage between Hitler and Mussolini; the characters we see are Eva Braun and her Italian equivalent Clara Petacci. There is the promise, given this theatre's reputation, of what the film of Cabaret called 'divine decadence.'

There is some decadence on the premises, but it falls short of divinity; more seriously it falls short of originality as well. Like Peter Barnes the previous week, Mr Macdonald goes to extreme lengths to say perfectly commonplace things. (Maybe Fascism is a subject on which the commonplace remarks are the only ones worth making.) He flirts with the glamour of the terrain; or rather has Eva and Clara do it for him. After some brief remarks about the satisfactions and frustrations of living in the shadow of a Head of State, and some rather longer ventriloquising on behalf of their gentlemen friends, they turn to taunting their bodyguard.

It hadn't occurred to me that Eva, who must have seen a lot of Nazi uniforms in her time, would still find them a turn-on; but there she goes, feeling the poor boy up, calling him a girl, and, when that palls, declaring that he is a Jew and must be sacrificed. This is the cue for the lights to change, and for soldier-boy to deliver a sober monologue about the eternal search for scapegoats.

Mr Barnes, I must admit, was bolder, though Mr Macdonald is wittier and a dabber hand at psychological tensions. Ann Mitchell, a beefily Anglo-Saxon Eva, and Julia Blalock, a miniature Maginot, do all right, and there is the inevitable striking set from Mr Prowse: a high-rise bunker.

Many reviewers have claimed that the RSC production of The Way of the World (Aldwych) enabled them, for the first time, to follow the plot. I don't want to boast, but I got it last time round; and it still doesn't make sense. Narrative apart, this is a sad end to a generally glorious season. The play, to begin with, looks lost on the permanent set; Congreve's society may have been vicious, but it was coherent. Everything here comes in little bits.

That applies to the sound as well as the look of the thing. Even Beryl Reid, whose Lady Wishfort is by some leagues the outstanding performance, an elderly bourgeoise straining to be on the rampage without quite knowing how, is short-breathed; she delivers in lines what Congreve wrote in paragraphs.

Otherwise the best thing of the evening is the marriage contract of Michael Pennington's Mirabell and Judi Dench's Millamant: for once it seems the beginning rather than the end of a relationship. But the efforts of John Barton's production to set it up, making Millamant the less confident in the preceding action, are self-defeating. Mr Pennington has all of Mirabell's controlling intelligence, but not much of his controlling personality; and though Miss Dench certainly avoids the Millamant stereotype, she comes perilously near to establishing a Dench stereotype, characterised by hurrying feet and nervous, silvery laughter.

Nervous laughter also permeates the performances of Witwoud and Petulant; modern actors seem incapable of playing Restoration fops without it, and the effect is invariably ruinous. I should add that Mr Barton's production is proverbially improved on the run, and a last-night ticket may be an excellent investment.

*

The plot of An Ideal Husband is decipherable; not perhaps to its advantage. Either Wilde meant it--in which case it could be played to the hilt--or he didn't, in which case it could be burlesqued. I would defy any one to tell the difference. This version, like most, is tactful and discreet and rather small; even such energetic players as Frank Barrie and Anna Carteret are neutralised. The wittiest is Barbara Atkinson, as a minor duchess or countess or something, who delivers a full harangue with artful restlessness, leading inevitably to exit applause. The butler is played by Ewan Hooper, Greenwich's retiring director--more retiring, since all he has to do is announce everybody else, than such an actor should be.
