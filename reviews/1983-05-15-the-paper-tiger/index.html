May 15, 1983

The Paper Tiger

Robert Cushman · The Observer

The paper tiger

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Giraudoux at the National Theatre.

JEAN GIRAUDOUX'S The Trojan War Will Not Take Place (Lyttelton) is a title that gives fair warning. It invokes and inverts Greek mythology, it is darkly frivolous, and it hints at eternally important themes. It signifies, as does the play itself, a schoolboy's view of what constitutes great drama.

That is not a sneer; it is a confession. A schoolboy is what I was when I first read it, and a masterpiece is what I thought. That, admittedly, was in 1950, when it was already a 20-year-old play; Christopher Fry's translation, still in use, was billed in the melodramatic fashion of the time as 'Tiger at the Gates.'

The tiger signifies the threat of war creeping up on Troy since the abduction of Helen. It also signifies Hector the Trojan hero, anxious to prevent the next war. He finds the Trojan women on his side, the old men, besotted with Helen and glory, against him.

But he battles on to return Helen, even getting the theoretic co-operation of the lady herself and the Greek envoy Ulysses, though both are sceptical that destiny can be cheated. It wants a war, and Helen is its instrument. But so may Hector be. The violence in him is the indirect cause of the calamity. It is very nearly, as he comes within an ace of killing the Grecian Ajax who is insulting his wife, the direct cause.

This is less a pacifist play (its credentials in that line, coming to you direct from the age of appeasement, are pretty disreputable) than a fatalist one. It exudes the kind of fashionable pessimism that is never out of fashion, since the eternal cussedness of human history gives it every chance of being the truth.

At the end Hector says simply, 'The war will happen'; that moment, which shoulders the weights of past struggle and legendary future carnage, is immense. So is its sequel; the symbolic Gates of War swing open to reveal Helen kissing young Troilus. Sex, like warfare, cheapens itself even as it prevails.

I am beginning to convince myself all over again. So, to backtrack: the play's style is very dated. What once we called 'civilised' we now, disdainfully, call 'clever.' This is not to say that it could not be rescued; the scenes in which old civilians, the ones not at risk, plan their propaganda war, should still strike a topical chill through their jokiness.

Actually, most of the play is jokey. If the jokes don't work, nothing will. The Lyttelton version, directed by Harold Pinter, goes disastrously for grandeur. Eileen Diss's set is a scramble of white steps, which draw the eye up to a nondescript blue cyclorama; finding no rest there, it climbs further and meets the stage lights: I have never spent so much time looking at lanterns. (The Gates of War are more impressive, though reminiscent of the old lift-doors at Selfridges.)

Nobody would dream today of inflicting such Pearl and Dean designs on a real Greek tragedy, or on Racine or 'Troilus and Cressida,' but because Giraudoux is comparatively modern he gets the treatment. It cripples the actors; try acting conversationally while strutting up and down stairs in a short tunic. Martin Jarvis is trapped between heroic poses and a hopeful smile. He does the stinging Oration to the Dead as well, but he never manages to look the living in the face: no contact, no pulse, no play.

The same generalised Greekness afflicts David Rintoul's Paris and, to a degree, Barry Foster's Ulysses, though with him we do move to a higher level. (So, self-consciously, does the play; the arrival of Ulysses is a signal that we are to stop giggling and pay attention. Here Giraudoux unveils his metaphysics, and they have shrivelled over the years.)

The play's sinister lightness is best captured by Nicola Pagett as Helen; her seduction of Troilus (Julian Firth) is the production's high spot. Annette Crosbie's Hecuba is a highly skilled dispenser of acid drops.

*

The play still survives better than John Osborne's 20-year-old A Patriot For Me (Chichester), which tells us that a homosexual military officer in the diplomatic service, in this case Austrian, could be blackmailed into spying for a foreign power, in this case Russia. Whatever next. The play is competently written on the 'King of Hearts' principle: begin at the beginning, go on until you reach the end, and then stop. It takes a long time.

A drag ball is famously thrown in, though it tells us nothing about the protagonist; we are merely directed to look at it and think, gosh. People will tell you that the play presents a picture of pre-1914 Austrian society, but all they really mean is that it has a large cast, many in uniform. They are seen to best advantage slowly revolving at the curtain-call.

Otherwise the visual problems are scarcely solved in Ronald Eyre's production, which uses drab projections for decor. The total effect is very literary, giving a new meaning to the term closet-drama; Mr Osborne's most vilified works ('A Sense of Detachment' or 'Watch It Come Down') have more life in them than this. Alan Bates, in icy control, is worth seeing; George Murcell is flavourful as the bluff, brutal Russian, a role he has played in many plays, including the original production of this one.

*

'Why,' I have found myself humming this week, 'must the show go on?' Coward's brilliantly dyspeptic attack on showbiz sentiment turns up, as the acme of chutzpah, in Noel and Gertie (King's Head); the title, again, tells all. 'Is it quite fair/To ask us to share/Your dreary private life'; the assumption here is that we hunger for every crumb of the joint private and public life of Coward and Gertrude Lawrence, though both seem to have been meagre.

They did two shows together, and were friends for a long time. As Sheridan Morley bizarrely puts it in his narration, 'physically they were in constant contact even though 5,000 miles away.' Whatever magic Miss Lawrence may have possessed is absent from Joanna Lumley's frankly insipid performance, but Simon Cadell is an excellent non-imitative Coward.

Among other things he sings a first-rate 'Mrs Worthington,' which makes it doubly absurd that most of the vocalising should be entrusted to a couple of cut-glass singers. Whoever said that Coward and Lawrence had voices? The dramatic excerpts are growing over-familiar, though Private Lives itself refuses to turn dreary; topping and tailing the evening with it gives shape, and a piercing sense of evanescence.

I have grown sickly weary of the perspex sets for Much Ado About Nothing (Barbican); they dehydrate the action. But Terry Hands's production has otherwise gained in shapeliness; Sinead Cusack's Beatrice is a serious, wounded creature from the start, whereas Derek Jacobi's Benedick has visibly to grow up in the church scene where he circles her praying figure.

Remarkably catchy music (Shakespeare's greatest hits?) is supplied by Nigel Hess. His more solemn setting of 'Sigh no more, ladies' deserves to be more than a hit: a standard.
