Mar 21, 1976

Dame With A Difference

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Very late last Tuesday night the Australian comedian Barry Humphries opened his one-man show at the Apollo. It was called Housewife-Superstar, after his most famous creation, Dame Edna Everage.

Edna, on this occasion, lived up to the title, and several new stars cropped up in her orbit. They were not played by Mr Humphries. As a matter of fact they had paid to get in. They were called Dave and Leo and Meredith and Judy.

Dave, seated in a side-box, was just someone for Edna to say hello to, to get the act started. Leo was actually a Leo, who responded, from a vulnerable spot in the front row, when Edna asked him what sign he was. But Edna, once she has made a friend, does not relinquish him, and he was Leo, by name, for the rest of the night and (since we didn't even get started till 11) well into the morning.

And then Judy. Edna is clean and proud of it. "The bathroom," she explained to Dave, who hadn't asked, "is an extra room we have in Australia; like your English woodshed." She then a bit inconsistently, quizzed Judy, who did not seem to be Australian, about her bathroom. "What colour are the tiles? The tiles, Judy!" Edna can get very excited, not to say snappish, when answers on her favourite topics are not as rapidly forthcoming as she would like. Nor will she tolerate bad manners. "What's the matter?" she demanded of us as we rocked. "Haven't you ever sat in a West End theatre at midnight and heard a lady describe her bathroom before?"

It seems that when Edna's daughter Velma lived in London she knew a Judy and Edna wondered if she could have been the same one. Judy, by now understandably bemused, said that she was. "A reply which, it seems, Edna is not used to receiving at this point, and which sent the act deliriously spinning. But she had her revenge: half an hour later, in quite another context, came a quick, venomous reference to a girl called Judy "posing as a friend of my daughter Velma." Edna's natural mode is syrupy and benign, but she is a viper when crossed.

She is a patroness to the manner born, though I dare say her damehood encouraged her enormously. She was, all things considered, very sweet to a gentleman in the stalls whom she suspected of being foreign (she knew that words would be wasted on him so she waved a scarf--"they love bright colours"--to keep him involved) and she could not have been kinder to another whom she tagged as a Senior Citizens (or, as the hour grew later and friendlier, simply Senior). What an escape, she mused, a night at the Apollo must represent for him: what a change from the Twilight Home--a world, like the one into which she had cast her own mother, "of rubber sheets."

Sometimes one forgot Edna and remembered Mr Humphries: wondered whether he always flies as high and fast as this, or whether he was inspired unrepeatably by the lateness of the hour and the firstness of the night. Subsequent performances begin at normal times, but I may return if only to discover what happens in the second half. For, Mr Humphries having held forth for a staggering 90 minutes, the interval arrived well past my bedtime, and so I went home determined--since I had already enjoyed myself far more than critics are entitled to--not to tempt Providence. The insurance paid off, since next night I laughed again.

*

13 Rue de L'Amour (Phoenix) is the best production of a Feydeau farce since the National's A Flea in Her Ear. In some ways it even surpasses that dazzler since it eschews camp (an indulgence that Flea could afford, but which has fatally attacked the Feydeau productions which have infested the posh reps ever since.) Peter Dews's direction has other things on its mind besides unnatural speed; indeed Glynis Johns, as a Parisian wife who takes to adultery because she quite justifiably suspects that her husband has taken to it first, is notably poised and reflective. Probably she is too much so--her performance lacks daemonism--but there is compensation in Louis Jourdan's lover, sourly wounded in his self-esteem, and above all in James Grout's four-square husband, a compulsive bluffer who ends by deceiving himself.

This final scene, also prettily taken by Miss Johns, does more to justify the now standard comparison of Feydeau to Moliere than any other I have seen. After a sequence of comedies (by Coward, Lonsdale, Ben Travers) that have nearly come off, it is comforting to recognise the cool, merciless touch of a real master. The technical details delight as always; but so do such incidental touches as Mr Jourdan's declaration that he keeps a poem on Miss Johns next his heart--after which he tugs it out of his pants pocket. Then there is Margaret Courtenay, in the best scene-stealing performance in London; quivering with delight whenever a man raises his voice to her, and two well-contrasted ditherers in David Stoll and John Baddeley.

*

The best week that the commercial theatre has had in ages was only partly sustained by Mardi Gras (Piccadilly). If colour, rhythm and vivacity could make a musical, this one would be fully made. It falls down on melody and on architecture. Melvyn Bragg's script is transparently in love with the New Orleans of jazz legend, but he has not been able to deliver a story of more than conventional melodrama.

There is plenty of performing talent (Lon Satton, Gaye Brown and Marsha Hunt are notable), but no real star. Nicky Henson is supposedly the hero, and the opening number reveals him to have a good growling jazz-voice, but he is kept idle for most of the evening. (And though he can do almost everything else, he cannot, it seems, radiate.) The score (Alan Blaikley and Ken Howard) is, like all English musicals, desperately weak on ballads; the stompers are not as good as the real thing, but when the beat is going you hardly notice; the programme lists seven orchestrators, which seems to show where the heart is. Dancing is better than average; Abd'Elkader Farrah's shiny set participates in the choreography, and his costumes help. But by the second act these elements are being employed indiscriminately to stop the rot of the plot, and the result is monotony.

*

Back in the land of the subsidised, Yemi Ajibade's Parcel Post is a domestic comedy (school of Quiet Wedding and Sailor Beware) that the Royal Court would not have dreamed of presenting (at least, I hope not) if the author and the characters were not African. The paternalism only begins to be justified 20 minutes from the end, and even then interest peters out.

At the University Theatre, Newcastle, David Rudkin's The Sons of Light is a massive allegory (three hours in a cut version) which sways between inflammatory inspiration and sci-fi banality. It needs to be done again; though, since its action is confused and its characterisation non-existent (except in the stage-directions which are brilliantly written), it will probably always fail. It need not, however, fail as abjectly as in Keith Hack's production which reinforces all that is worst in the writing. A character written as a schizophrenic hermaphrodite emerges as a routine mad girl. That the one sequence of clarity should be a prolonged account of death by torture is a comment certainly on the director, possibly on the author, and confessedly on the receptivity of us in the audience.

*

The Chairman (Globe), a comedy about the PR department of a large company, whose members demonstrate a similarly mystifying standard of behaviour, is mildly amusing and--given its theme of the corrupting force of a totalitarian organisation--one which might have raised a few ripples outside its little frogpond. Most of the performances do better than the script, especially Tony Britton's smooth, peacock-pleased David and Peter Blythe's waterfall torrent of breathy giggles and hyper-kinesis as Peter.
