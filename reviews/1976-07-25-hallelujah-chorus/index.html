Jul 25, 1976

Hallelujah Chorus!

Robert Cushman · The Observer

SIX years ago, in his choreography for Company, Michael Bennett devised a new convention for chorus numbers in a musical. He did it by dispensing with a chorus; when massed song and dance were called for, he used the principals, a dozen actors whom we had already got to know as individual characters. It was a lot more fun than the multiple anonymity that is generally to be found treading vigorously just outside the star's spotlight.

In A Chorus Line (Drury Lane), Mr Bennett, now director and presiding genius as well as choreographer, has worked the same trick in reverse. Instead of making a chorus out of individuals, he has made individuals out of a chorus. The framework of the show is an audition session for an unspecified musical; we begin in the most electric opening sequence I have ever seen, by watching the applicants going through their paces in tap and ballet. Some of them are sent home, 17 remain.

The director says that he needs eight of them, and that they will have to do more than dance. They will have lines, so he wants to find out what kind of people they are. The audition turns into a trial by inquisition, a contest at which we have nobody in particular to root for; we can only love the 17 for themselves and by the end we love them all.

But the main reason we love them is not because they are cute or because they had difficult childhoods, though it transpires that they all had; it is because they are so exhilaratingly good at their jobs. And here the line between performer and role really ceases to matter; these are fine actor-singer-dancers playing fine actor-singer-dancers, American specials.

Good though they are, it does not follow that they would all be equally suited to the roles they are auditioning for. In effectively pretending that they would be, Mr Bennett cheats a bit. Seeing the show for the third time, I sagged slightly after the first number, when the dancing stopped and the confessional began. Part of the appeal of that beginning is its documentary quality; we are seduced into thinking that we are witnessing a dance-audition as it really is. What follows seems artificial.

The slump, though, is momentary, since we are soon given a bright succession of songs about professional hazards: about a girl who can dance but not sing, about another who stayed out of work until she got her breasts siliconised (the refrain of this song is "Tits and Ass," though the programme politely refers to it as "Dance: Ten; Looks: Three"), about a third who suffered appallingly from an actor-teacher who ridiculed her earnest attempts to impersonate an ice-cream. This one, called "Nothing," is the show's best song, but Edward Kleban's lyrics for all three are funny and sympathetic.

Marvin Hamlisch's music is serviceable, though too much of the score is pure Radio Two middle-of-the-road; not really theatrical, not really pop. The show's official climax is an appallingly vague ballad called "What I Did For Love," a detachable hit. It is supposed to be about performing, but takes care never to say so.

By this time we are far, but not very deep, into the characters' hang-ups, though these are usually dealt with in speech rather than song. Talking about why they are in the business, they never mention Applause. I capitalise that word since it was the title, a few years back, of a much inferior musical which, however, latched onto a point which A Chorus Line neglects. At least it neglects it explicitly; in style and performance, of course, it is a hugely effective machine for pleasing audiences.

As an epilogue, after the story is done and the winning eight selected, we get a unison number called "One," the cast of 19 (the director and his assistant thrown in) high-kicking in gold suits and glorious unison while the practice-mirrors revolve to turn a stark set into a sumptuous one. (Many of the most exciting things in the theatre are done with mirrors.) This finale also serves as a curtain-call; and it sends us wild. There are hints in the dialogue earlier that Mr Bennett despises this kind of Hello Dolly extravagance, and that he hates the idea of the chorus as an exploited centipede. I do not believe though that any audience in the world is going to leave the theatre seeing in that number what he wants us to see. It is simply too enjoyable. And, as in Company, we enjoy it the more for having got to know the performers beforehand.

Leonard Bernstein recently deplored the "phony vitality" of musicals. The achievement of A Chorus Line is to make the vitality genuine again. Obviously the performers believe in the material. A montage called "Hello Twelve, Hello Thirteen, Hello Love" (puberty recollected in rhythm) is the show's epitome: commonplace in substance, but conqueringly alight in delivery. Mr Bennett's choreography, as distinct from his direction, can be surprisingly uninspired; a solitary number not only compromises what the show is supposed to be saying, but fails in itself, being only a series of angular turns. I carp since it is difficult not to snipe at a myth, and in the best and worst senses that is what A Chorus Line is: not the best musical ever, not even the best of the last few years, but an insight into the essence of musical, the biggest theatrical high since the RSC's one-day marathon of The Wars of the Roses.

*

Chichester came back to scratch last week with a revival of Maugham's The Circle, which Peter Dews's production showed to be the kind of searching laugh at conventional society that only a conservative writer could bring off. (A radical wouldn't bother.) Bill Fraser and Googie Withers find the silliness and the pain in a raddled pair of elderly lovers. Actually Miss Withers scants a bit on the silliness, but she more than compensates with her virtuoso delicacy when counselling her daughter-in-law against repeating her own mistakes and eloping to the FMS (Federated Malay States).

Coincidentally, the FMS may actually be glimpsed, not looking at all pleasant, in Ben Travers's Banana Ridge (Savoy), just pre-war, spiced with Mr Travers's eccentric verbal wit, but never really sparking. Robert Morley and George Cole are a disappointing duo as members of a sort of paternity syndicate, blackmailed into supporting the offspring of a Miss Sue Long, from whom many must have departed to the strains of "Sue Long, It's Been Good to See You." The play blows its top too soon, leaving the last scene an anti-climax.

This could not be said of Michael Frayn's Donkeys' Years (Globe) which, having got all possible comic mileage out of a group of Cambridge graduates (now employed from the Ministry of Education downwards) returned for a college reunion, very sensibly turns itself into farce, among the most inventive, hilarious and scrupulously motivated to be found in London, even in this new heyday of the genre. Common to both the play's moods is the double theme of the turning worm and the jangled university belle; a brilliant pair of performances by Andrew Robertson and Penelope Keith. Her recoil from him is particularly memorable. Michael Rudman's production is blessed by an excellent cast, and you leave the theatre liking the author: not the highest dramatic gift but a rare and good one.
