May 4, 1975

Bertolt Out-Brechted

Robert Cushman · The Observer

DAVID HARE'S Fanshen (Joint Stock Theatre Group at the ICA) is a play in the Brechtian manner on a Brechtian subject, though it hews far closer to the master's theories than he himself ever did in his practice.

I was reminded of this by the television production of The Caucasian Chalk Circle which, for a wonder (theatre critics not generally being allowed home in the evenings), I caught on its repeat showing last week. This play, you may remember, begins with a group of peasants discussing the proper use of a piece of land; but the discussion is not prolonged and is used only as the basis for a moral fairy-tale. Nor, along the way, are we left in much doubt of what the moral is, nor of the author's attitudes towards the characters. A storyteller is provided to make it all clear to us; and Brecht's vaunted objectivity has gone where it usually goes into abeyance.

Mr Hare's play also begins with a discussion among peasants; it ends with another and consists of little else in between. But there is no danger of Mr Hare sliding into obvious partisanship; if alienation was a discipline which Brecht had to invent in order to keep his feelings under control, for Mr Hare it appears to be a perfectly normal way of writing. His previous plays have been cold-blooded satirical comedies, surveying their characters and the society enclosing them with dispassionate loathing. Fanshen by contrast regards its characters with dispassionate approval and is not a comedy.

It does raise the occasional sour laugh: "Under the nationalists too many taxes, under the Communists too many meetings." Fanshen (based on a book by William Hinton) is the story of how one village in post-war China passed from the first state to the second. "Fanshen" (meaning a turning-over) is the name given to this process, and also to the many smaller changes, both within society and the individual, which it involves. As the only Chinese word in a script otherwise composed in good plain English it takes on mystical overtones as the evening proceeds. The Party becomes revered as the organisation that gave the people Fanshen: now and everlastingly. And it comes as no surprise (though still something of a culture-shock) to hear one conscience-stricken recalcitrant lamenting his probable fate: "I'll be thrown out of the Party--that's worse than being dead."

In one sense this is political drama of a rigour we rarely experience; the Joint Stock actors, under the direction of William Gaskill and Max Stafford-Clark, present and clarify arguments. They do not characterise. Once the landlords have been expropriated (and the production seems to take some joy in literally putting the boot in) all is sober dialectic. Each revolutionary step is scrutinised, the villagers agonise over their own decisions, and Party secretaries--themselves impeccably self-critical--pay periodic visits to keep them up to the mark; or to inform them where the mark now is. Mr Hare offers no authorial comment on the struggle, merely the assurance that it is worth waging--an assurance made explicit in the very last speech and implicit in his devoting three hours of dramatic time to it.

Granted that these hours are informative and occasionally gripping, doubts arise. Under the Communists too many meetings; so many that one wonders what time could be found actually to work the land so exhaustively discussed. Perhaps this is simply a reflection of what can and cannot be efficiently represented on a stage. But its effect is to provide both for the presenters and the audience a major exercise in wish-fulfilment; there is (especially if you actually live in an urban industrial society) more fun and more intellectual glamour in discussing agrarian activity, revolutionary or otherwise, than in actually performing it. Politics, divorced from any visible effect other than the humiliation of an unpleasant landlord or two, becomes an end in itself; for many people this is apparently an extremely seductive prospect.

Some years ago at the National Theatre Mr Gaskill gave us some extremely effective eighteenth-century comedies with Brechtian trimmings. It is commonly supposed that these productions showed us, once for all, the correct way to handle the genre; and it has become regulation critical drill to shoot down any director foolhardy enough to try another method.

Ian McKellen has directed Colman and Garrick's The Clandestine Marriage at the Savoy for H. M. Tennent; and has predictably, but groundlessly, been accused of eliminating the play's social comment. Now the comment in this play, as in so many others, is concerned with money, and it is particularly directed against the nouveau riche. The chief butt is Sterling, a merchant who, having made his pile, wishes to augment it with prestige by marrying his daughter into the aristocracy; the aristocracy, being impoverished, is willing to co-operate, but collectively rejects the merchant's plainer elder daughter in favour of his pretty younger one. She, however, is secretly married to her father's clerk. The merchant's lines are full of explicit greed. (He rejects the clerk because he has "no stuff, no money.")

In Mr McKellen's production Sterling's mercenary vulgarities are positively roared out by Ron Moody, and the landscape garden, which is his pride and joy, is as elaborately tasteless as the severest critic would desire. I do not see how Mr McKellen could have exposed the man more mercilessly without descending to the kind of over-emphasis (dressing the servants in rags for example) that we would surely all dismiss as vulgar. Mr Moody may even push Sterling a trifle too hard, but then he has a soft and exquisite foil in Alastair Sim's rheumaticky Lord Ogleby, contemplating with intrigued disbelief, slowly melting into charmed surprise, the unnatural sights that adorn Sterling's domain: "a spire ... without a church ... halfway up a tree." The gradualness is all.

For all this Mr McKellen's production is not very good: Messrs Sim and Moody would probably be much as they are (and neither is absolutely at his best) whoever directed and the other funnies lack inspiration. The straights (Kay Barlow, Peter Blythe) are better, but much of the play is tepid and it finally comes to the boil without our noticing. The last scene finds most of the cast assembled outside a bedroom. The man they suspect of being inside enters from the opposite direction. A comic climax, everyone aghast? Not a bit of it; the intruder sidles unobtrusively onto a reaction delayed, diffused, defeated.

*

The Stratford Love's Labour's Lost of two seasons ago has come to the Aldwych newly-charged. The dynamo is Ian Richardson, in virtuoso form as he has not been in Shakespeare these seven years; careering through the verse full tilt and full tremolo, able to brake or deepen on the instant. He would be invulnerable if Estelle Kohler's Rosaline were not part-mistress of the same weapons; she unseats him with lazy ease. There is, too, a new boy Moth (Martin Lev) who knows what he is about and why.

At Stratford itself Alan Howard, having scaled the Harfleur ladders as Henry V, slides down a snake or two to become Prince Hal in Henry IV, Part One: a moody but not moony lad driven to bad company (Brewster Mason's Falstaff, on my visit, was laryngitic) by the oppressiveness of the good. As represented by Emrys James, Henry Part One is the vile politician excoriated by Hotspur; he is perhaps storing up his nobler qualities for Part Two.
