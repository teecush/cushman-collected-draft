Dec 4, 1983

The Girl From Iowa

Robert Cushman · The Observer

The girl from Iowa

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Jean Seberg.'

AT the very least Jean Seberg makes an interesting bridge in the National Theatre's repertoire between Tales from Hollywood and Saint Joan. For those of us with a romantic streak it was pleasing to discover that the rumoured disaster at the Olivier was no such thing. It has in fact some striking merits.

Peter Hall has delivered a polished, logical production. John Bury's designs get us from Iowa to Hollywood to Paris through exquisite use of back projection. Barring a couple of lapses, Marvin Hamlisch's score is the best he has written for the theatre. (It is an indication of Broadway's decline in quality and quantity that we should already be talking of Mr Hamlisch, who has written only three shows, as if he were an old master.)

Two other New York professionals, orchestrator Bill Byers and vocal arranger Buster Davis, give the music the best possible chance. The Olivier is a marvellous theatre in which to see and hear a musical, and this is in many ways a slicker, sleeker presentation than Guys and Dolls.

It is nothing like as enjoyable; and that is not just because it has a serious subject. It traces Miss Seberg's career from small-town drugstore-counter girlhood, through her disastrous film debut as Joan and her subsequent success riding the nouvelle vague, to espousal of the Black Panthers, persecution by the FBI, and suicide.

That is a lot of ground to cover; music speeds us along, rather too fast. It also imposes some conventions of its own. Hopful Minds audition for Otto Preminger by singing a bit of text. This is bizarre; Saint Joan was not a musical. It also signals desperation: almost inevitable in a musical show about a non-musical performer.

Jean Seberg has to go looking for hooks on which to hang its song-and-dance. Some are all too obvious: the Panthers get a juiced-up number to which they caper belligerently while chanting Black Revolution. Some are crazily far-fetched: at one point Jean puts on view the body of her dead child, in order to prove that it was not black. A suitable song for such a moment is hard to envisage, but what we get is a homogenised lament about loneliness, shorn--like the egregious "What I Did For Love" in A Chorus Line--of any reference to the dramatic situation. When Mr Hamlisch's pop instincts come out, there's no holding them; I wish there were still a Tin Pan Alley so I might call him Hamlisch, Prince of Denmark Street.

Somewhere between these extremes comes a wisecracking female duet for two of Jean's Hollywood enemies. It is much in the manner of Bosom Buddies, from Mame, and is presumably trying to transcend it. But it ends, for lack of wit, not using the musical genre, but being used by it. Julian Barry's book has similar problems. It is one device after another.

Jean at the point of death watches and comments on the mistakes made by her younger self. Within this framework is another: Jean as Joan, on set and on trial, with J. Edgar Hoover doubling as Inquisitor. Gamely Mr Barry touches all the bases--puritan upbringing, father-fixation, early social conscience, black boyfriend, dreams of stardom--without exploring them.

In a musical that may be the job of the songs, but it places on Christopher Adler's lyrics a responsibility they cannot sustain. They are, on the whole, painless, but they never dig and they never glisten; they merely paraphrase the obvious. All we discover about the heroine is that she was a victim; and victims are not interesting.

Our pair of Jeans are an appealing Kelly Hunter and a stylishly ravaged Elizabeth Counsell. There is solid work from Joss Ackland (spending yet another evening getting sung at) as Romain Gary, and from Michael Bryant as Hoover. He doesn't sing, but Jean-Luc Godard does. So does Otto Preminger, a fabulous-monster role fully relished by John Savident.

At the end these distinguished British players relax into their native accents to deliver an epilogue. It is a dignified moment, and it takes some of the curse from the experience of being lectured, by a British company, on the failure of the American dream.

*

Our luck is holding with the Spanish Golden Age; Calderon's Life's a Dream works finely in Stratford's Other Place, staged by John Barton in an enclosure that looks like a corrida. (My stock reaction to Spanish sets.) Adrian Mitchell's translation swings, taking in enormous narratives and short jokes.

This is an amazing play, a tragedy in which only the clown dies. The tiresome platitude adumbrated in the title is brought to life through ceaseless twisting narrative energy; how lucky is a theatre that believes in romance. A Polish prince, kept confined since birth, is allowed a testing day as king and blows it by turning Caliguloid. Persuaded that it never really happened, he achieves maturity. In counterpoint, an enthusiastically wronged maiden learns to snap out of her own dream of revenge.

These roles are enterprisingly played by Miles Anderson and Barbara Kellermann, though Mr Anderson is too spryly earthbound to realise the play's full scope, and bright-eyed Miss Kellermann occasionally flags. They have fine support from Anthony O'Donnell, Charles Kay, and David Waller, who has now attained a state of grace that enables him to play wisdom.

The play, by some extra-continental coincidence, wakes echoes of half-a-dozen Shakespeares. On the first night a steed made a notable appearance underneath Mr Anderson, but cannot, it seems, be afforded in future. Obviously Equine Equity drives a hard bargain, but here is an opportunity for anyone wishing to put money on a horse.

*

Songs in Wedlock/Deadlock (King's Head) weakens an already ailing fabric. This is a concatenation, by Fidelis Morgan, of three eighteenth-century comedies, one called--the third time this year--The Custom of the Country. It plays as a collection of roistering Restoration jokes about marriage, camped up into a Caribbean musical. There are imaginative touches, though, in Miss Morgan's direction, and witty performances from Paola Dionisotti, Sian Thomas and Celia Imrie. All these ladies are former Glasgow Citizens. Their presence is missed back at the ranch, where a tragedy of similar vintage, Southerne's Oroonoko, suffers from lack of acting. It does not of course lack design; it is set in a West Indian slave-port, which Philip Prowse has envisaged as a ghostly shanty-town.
