Jul 1, 1990

In The Languages Of Star-Crossed Lovers

Robert Cushman · The Independent

For the last 30 years, consciously or not, most productions of Romeo and Juliet have been competing with West Side Story. They may emphasise sex, like Franco Zeffirelli's Renaissance epics of lust and dust on stage and screen, or violence, as in Michael Bogdanov's spirited RSC attempt to pin the blame on Verona's capitalist system. Here is one Shakespeare play - about teenagers, for God's sake - that should be able to realise the fantasy of every populist director and hit the kids where they live.

If where they live is Canada, there are other possibilities. This is a riven country, maybe on the verge of splitting apart. So a homespun company called Shakespeare on the Saskatchewan has pitched tent - literally - at Harbourfront, which is where Toronto holds its annual international theatre festival, with a bilingual production. Anglophone Montagues spit their hatred, in the original text, at Francophone Capulets who retaliate in translation. When they want to get really nasty, either side is liable to adopt the other's speech. Appropriately, there are two directors: Saskatchewan's Gordon McCall and the Quebecois Robert Lepage, whose massive original creations are too dynamic to be called performance art, and who is one of the world's best directors.

Characters enter by car or, more commonly, truck. We are in a car-town, and the lovers meet at the Capulet barn dance. Some oddities cannot be overcome. The man reverently referred to as the Prince is more like the local cop. Gee, Officer Krupke, fancy meeting you here.

At first the concept seems to have got stuck in the traffic. It is impressive to see just how many vehicles can force themselves into a space, and exciting to watch them and their occupants bashing one another. The opening brawl is as mean as it should be ("Do you bite your thumb at us, sir?" is the first line in clear English). Juliet's balcony is the top of a pickup truck: no deterrent to Romeo who is on top of her - literally - before they have exchanged so much as a sigh. This is presumably meant to be fantasy, but the heavy-handed staging has no magic. The first half is brutalist Shakespeare at its most reductive. After the interval all is superbly changed. A tiresomely coarse Mercutio becomes witty just in time for death. The traffic thins; the lovers wheel a bed on for their farewell scene, with a rope attached. When they part, Juliet pays the rope out and Romeo crawls along it. Somehow we are convinced this horizontal flight is a vertical one. This means Lepage is in charge. Every moment seems right, and most of them reverberate. When Juliet takes the potion we share her fearful vision of the corpse-strewn family tomb; this time we are not confused, and we do believe.

Unlike most directors praised for command of imagery or the ability to create events, Lepage is sensitive to text and acting. From the start the two leads show unusual promise: Romeo sturdy rather than moonstruck and Juliet's accent enchanting enough to justify the language experiment on its own. They grow remarkably; and the other characters give them a belated but illuminating context. (A young, coldly frightened Lady Capulet explains a lot about her daughter). The last speech, done by the whole cast, with alternate lines in French and English, returns us impressively to the concept. But anyone can have a concept: Lepage has peopled his.

The importance of basics has been further demonstrated by Kenneth Branagh's Renaissance Theatre Company which wound up its six-month world tour in Toronto prior to a British tour leading to the Edinburgh Festival. Branagh's King Lear and A Midsummer Night's Dream are disfigured by a unit set that looks dingy under lights and too many performances of matching shabbiness. But they survive as fine pieces of story-telling. Richard Briers's Lear has unexpected authority, somewhat at the expense of his own actor's identity; but his madness has a lucidity and immediacy I have never known bettered. The Dream has lively lovers and brilliant mechanicals, among whom Branagh's Quince is a real original: a smug village intellectual, resentful of Briers's buoyant Bottom. Clearly, if you are going both to act and direct in the Dream, Quince is the role to choose. In Lear Branagh plays Edgar, another character who tries to control peoples' lives. It must work off all kinds of tensions.
