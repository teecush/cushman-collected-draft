Nov 30, 1980

Fooling About With Hamlet

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Fooling about with Hamlet

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

THE Footsbarn Company sounds as if it ought to be a band of German strolling players.

In fact it does stroll and is popular on the Continent. Its base though is Cornwall, and holidaymaking actors who have encountered it there have declared themselves so impressed that they were tempted to throw it all in and go footsbarnstorming themselves.

Last week Footsbarn's Hamlet marked the company's London debut (at the Golden Lane Theatre in the Barbican) and its first tussle with a received text. Generally they work up their own, and what they offered here was not exactly Shakespeare; rather, an acrobatic paraphrase. The favoured method of entry, on to a circular platform apparently covered in green baize, was the graceful pole-vault. Costumes veered from the Orient (Rosencrantz - there was no Guildenstern - looked like a down-at-heel mandarin), through Araby (Hamlet in his mourning state being sternly yashmaked), to Turkey, at which point it met up with the music which, though skilful and effective, recalled the sensation of being seated up by the juke-box in a kebab house.

The performance lasted only two hours, so much went. The first scene was mimed, crowned by the appearance of a horrifically bony ghost. To be or not to be was cut entirely (cheeky) and the other soliloquies pared to the bone. All other speeches were made easy. "Will you listen," said the Ghost, huffily re-interpreting the original "List, list, o list."

Some scenes were re-extended to give scope to the actors' talents for tumbling and funny voices: the gravediggers were upped to three, with a whole new routine; and the players became a collection of Chico Marxes. Melodrama was strong and so were visuals; at the close the bodies were covered by a great dust sheet, on which the lighting picked out a pattern of skulls.

Hamlet made little impression beyond some enthusiastic gibbering and cock-a-doodling when insane, but there was an Ophelia with a will of her own and a Polonius who, for the third time this year, came close to walking off with the play: an inveterate political shover, whether briefing a quintet of spies ("you follow my son Laertes and see what he gets up to") or boasting about his past ("I was in a play once, you know") and ending up spectacularly impaled on Hamlet's spear. In the end, though, I was left wondering what it was all for. There was enough of Hamlet left to arouse our interest in the story, not enough to maintain it.

*

Francis King, novelist and critic, has turned playwright with Far East (Belgrade, Coventry), based on his own experiences as a cultural representative in Japan. I have to take his word for the surprising innocence of a 21-year-old Japanese student-cum-houseboy living in with a British Council couple, tainted by their weary worldliness, seduced and then disillusioned by an English mission-girl. Having taken it, I was touched by the writing and by Richard Rees's performance; similarly, by Renu Setna (who is becoming our theatre's all-purpose Oriental) as an elderly Japanese Anglophile, quaking with politeness and declining a visit to England on the grounds that the country now won't be much like Jane Austen. Mr King is very good at marking out and exploiting cultural differences; but his British couple, structurally at the centre of his play, never rise from stereotype.

*

A male virgin twice the age of Mr King's is the hero of Rattle of a Simple Man (Savoy), Charles Dyer's West End success of 20 years ago, revived as a vehicle for Pauline Collins and John Alderton and belatedly collected by me. Innocence again comes off best, Messrs Alderton and Dyer (and the audience) empathising mightily with the Mancunian football fan who blunders into a night with a London tart and at curtain-fall is still deciding whether or not to go to bed with her. But if he is real, she is a creature of fantasy: in two senses, since she has to lie continually to make life bearable. She reeks of old psychological theory and dramatic practice, and not all Miss Collins's technique and general lovability can make her believable.

*

Nicholas Nickleby (Aldwych) is back in town, and everything else pales. The RSC has accustomed us to marathons: this, though, is their first comic epic. Over the past five years Trevor Nunn has been breeding a richly human company. Dickens seems, in retrospect, their logical goal.

Paradoxically, David Edgar's adaptation, which gets everything in, has more of an individual theatrical identity than more timidly selective versions have done. It is massive but also supple. Each group, each episode, painted in loving detail, is balanced against another and can be whisked away to make room for it. The closing stages of Part One - in which the Crummles' production of Romeo and Juliet is juxtaposed with the attempted seduction of Kate Nickleby, whose fate is momentarily much like Juliet's - is the purest theatrical wizardry.

Formally, indeed, Mr Edgar may well improve on Dickens; he makes the plot not an encumbrance to be stoically borne for the sake of glamorous interludes, but a source both of excitement and meaning.

Only in Dotheboys Hall do we feel the production straining to make points rather than making them. Maybe the use of grown-up actors instead of boys masks the real casual cruelty of the system; certainly Fulton Mackay's Squeers, like Ben Kingsley's before him, is oddly puritanical. Some performances (Jane Downs's Mrs Nickleby and John McEnery's Mantalini) have greatly improved.

Performances I praised before have matured with staggering assurance: Roger Rees's Nicholas, Edward Petherbridge's Newman Noggs exuding pent-up excitement from every clenched joint, David Threlfall's Smike, Bob Peck's double of Hawk and John Browdie (the latter incarnating all the qualities in the production that make one want to cheer), above all John Woodvine's Ralph, a smooth dark villain who greets disaster with an awesome change of gear. Watch how these actors, with the minimum of fuss, simply take and hold the stage - as, on a smaller scale, do Patrick Godfrey's crawling Kenwigs, Timothy Spall's face-stuffing junior Squeers, Janet Dale's painfully refined Mrs Wititterly, Nicholas Gecks's young lord and a dozen others.

Add an imposing double by Lila Kaye and a tremendous treble by Suzanne Bertish (whose sister Jane Bertish, by the way, is good in the play at Coventry). Welcome various newcomers: Christopher Benjamin a juicy Crummles (with the most affecting line in the piece) and Emily Richard and Harriet Walter bringing the heroines fully to life. Acknowledge the whirlwind energy brought to the beneficent Cheeryble menage by David Lloyd Meredith, Hubert Rees, Griffith Jones and Christopher Ravenscroft, while admitting that the flaw they represent in the logic of novel and play alike can never quite be glossed over.

Celebrate the set by John Napier and Dermot Hayes, a drama in itself, and the immense contribution of Stephen Oliver's music. Call down blessings on Mr Nunn and his co-director John Caird. Admit that you have still not done justice to the occasion, and decide that, in all probability, nobody ever will.
