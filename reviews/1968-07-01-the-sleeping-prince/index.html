Jul 1, 1968

The Sleeping Prince

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

The Sleeping Prince

Meanwhile there is perturbation but not much music in West Street where countless plays have loped in and out of the St Martin's in the sixteen heart-stopping years that The Mousetrap has stood immovable guard next door. One of these nights, confused by the rapid turnover, I shall wander into the Ambassadors (what can it be like?) by mistake. On occasions, I have felt very tempted.

The Sleeping Prince just misses being one of those occasions. When, a few months ago, I commanded in these pages that some Rattigan be revived forthwith, this play was not quite what I had in mind. Still, the West End is not so rich in good boulevard comedy that we can afford to sneer at even a second-rate example of the genre. The Sleeping Prince is a second-rate example of the genre, and I shall try to bear in mind that we cannot afford to sneer at it.

A Grand Duke, in London for the 1911 Coronation, picks up an American chorus-girl. Had he fancied an English one instead life would have been easier for most of the actresses likely to play the part and for those forced to hear them at it, but she has to be American for the sake of some wide-eyed lines about free speech and democracy. He entertains her to a champagne supper in the Royal Suite of the Carpathian Legation in Belgrave Square, London. At first she takes a dim view of his blandishments (I quote Walter Kerr quoting heaven knows what) but gradually the dim view brightens until, within hailing-distance of the crucial moment, it suffers total eclipse. She passes out (first-act curtain). I know this is predictable; so do you; so, luckily, does Rattigan, and most of the laughs derive from our mutual awareness.

But in Act Two the gurgling has to stop. Showgirl awakens in love with prince and hangs round his neck like a nubile albatross for the rest of the play. This and a spot of consummation apart, the relationship remains static and the play has to be fuelled by diplomatic complications involving the teenage king of Carpathia and determined mainly by the necessity of ensuring a decent time-lapse between interval and curtain-call.

George Baker, an actor who excels in exploring the nuances of stolidity, gives a workmanlike performance as the Grand Duke, which is fine if you like your Grand Dukes to be like workmen. His comic climaxes lack their final éclat but most of the time he has only to express various degrees of exasperation, which he manages with suspicious conviction, though as director and impresario he only has himself to blame. Anyway, the play depends on its leading lady, so sweet, so dumb, and yet so diabolically clever. Susan Hampshire is very sweet, but she is far from dumb, and her skilful endeavours to pretend otherwise weigh down what should be the most enjoyable portion of the play. In the second act, effortlessly disentangling the politics of Carpathia, she is far nearer her element. All the same, her stage appearances are too infrequent to be squandered like this.

The Grand Duke has a Grand Duchess whom Kenneth Tynan once aberrantly described as “the first exceptional human being Rattigan has invented”. She is, in fact, a traditional stage eccentric, absent-mindedly awaiting the services of some grande dame with time to kill. Martita Hunt created the part; it goes now to Jean Anderson, whose sturdy Scots good sense renders it effectively redundant. Two players are in key with their material: David Hutcheson whose English ADC is fortified by years of experience at this sort of thing, and Roger Davenport, the boy-king, who in the nature of things can hardly have been at it so long but never betrays the fact. If someone slips you some free tickets, I should go.
