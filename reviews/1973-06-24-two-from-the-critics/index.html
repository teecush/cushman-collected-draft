Jun 24, 1973

Two From The Critics

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Two from the critics

Theatre

by Robert Cushman

I CLOSED last week with a promise to drag this column into the twentieth century. I was not rash enough to specify the 1970s, but I had hopes. They were pinned to two of my fellow reviewers with plays of their own about to appear. Broken reeds. In The Houseboy (Open Space), Irving Wardle offers sensitive transportation to the nineteen-fifties; in Sing of the Times (Vaudeville), Jeremy Kingston exhibits some of the typical humours of the sixties.

Mr Kingston's is the lighter play but the more serious offence, since it clearly suffers from delusions of contemporaneity. (This despite a pianist in the pit tinkling out the message that this is the dawning of the age of Aquarius: Hair arrived here in 1968 and it was already receding then.) Mr Wardle at least matches form to content, and period to both.

His play has the feel of autobiography, like most works about gauche, serious young men. John, 25 years old, recently down from Oxford, currently a music student, works as a scullion in a London hotel run by two middle-aged homosexuals. Their relationship is fraying ("two old hot-water bottles wrinkling up together"), and John, foreseeably, serves them as a battleground. For the first time he finds himself possessed of power; he begins, not altogether amiably, to grow up.

Mr Wardle easily makes us free of John's mind--too easily since the play works best when he remains a sounding-board for his patrons. John is most satisfying when most opaque; Philip Donaghy's silences are far more compelling than the glimpses we are clumsily offered of his inner life. When he quotes Shakespeare, at moments both frequent and inopportune, we can--unless I am being fanciful--observe a drama critic in the making; Mr Donaghy makes this eventual metamorphosis seem a very likely prospect. But the real interest resides with the rival queens. Mr Wardle has given them some neat dialogue and a ration of emotional depth; Nicholas Selby (who explodes) and Timothy West (a slow fuse burning beneath an ominously level surface) handle them efficiently.

The play's period flavour has one piquant aspect. No actual English play of the fifties ever managed to treat homosexuality so unflappably. The club theatres of the period (which would surely have been patronised by Mr Wardle's odd couple had he not drawn them as ballet-fanciers) were full of shrill, martyred approaches to the subject. The West End was as rife with plays whose authors, by way of obeisance to the Lord Chamberlain, disguised or discarded any homosexual relationships among their characters, and gravely impaired their credibility-rating as a result. One of the last playwrights from whom this homage was extracted (over a play called No Concern of Mine) was Mr Kingston. In his new piece he makes sacrifice to another of the theatre's antique deities, the god (surely not Eros, despite his convenient location) of West End sex comedy.

Before word spread of sexual revolution, this genre had a certain point. The author's ingenuity, and the audience's presumed censoriousness, combined to keep the bedroom door closed, and the heroine outside it. Now she is allowed in but, since nothing else in the play has changed, her emancipation seems remarkably incongruous. The artificial, asexual ambience is not to be transformed merely by allowing Kenneth More to make jokes about virgins. It appears, if you please, that he knew one once who worked for The Observer.

A lot of the jokes concern newspapers. So, in a way, does the plot, which presupposes--with more confidence than is strictly justified--a universal craze for all things astrological. Even The Times gets itself a stargazer, in the comfortably crumpled shape of Mr More. But since he only takes the job in order to influence the ladies in his life, both of them avid horoscope-heads, the comedy of gullibility dies at birth, and another evening is spent hovering round the bedchamber without so much as a maidenhead at stake. Sometimes people come in and tell us about the exciting things happening in the outside world, like in Greek tragedy.

The sad thing is that Mr Kingston does have some comic gift (he goes for the smart rattle where Mr Wardle is more your sombre ironist) and that he must have known, from his own critical experience, the kind of stereotype to which his idea was being reduced. Mr More, who has brought the art of passive comedy to a very high pitch, is a continual consolation. He is that treasurable phenomenon, the straight man who gets all the laughs. Not even Frankie Howerd is more adept at thinking of an entendre and then doubling it.

*

Strictly speaking, there are more laughs to be had at the Vaudeville than in Nuts (Theatre Workshop), but the latter has a cleaner smell. This show defies criticism. Songs and sketches by Joan Littlewood's regular troupe alternate with personal appearances by real East London journalists, whose speeches--whether attacking property developers or just hawking the Socialist Worker--are the best things in the evening, and the only occasions on which those delivering the material display any faith in it. As community theatre, this is totally honest. There is no attempt to pass propaganda off as drama, and none of the masturbatory quality of actors trying earnestly to act local.

But the ineptitude of most of the numbers passes belief. Some of the sketches might be salvageable, but there is no telling since few of the performers are bothering anyway. Well, at least they're relaxed. Kent Baker does Mae West to a nice turn ("Where's your Danny La Rue this noo") and Bill Wallis, a bearded roly-poly pudding of an actor, alone displays the anarchic offhandedness which presumably is the goal throughout.

*

At the Young Vic, an evening of Beckett Shorts (presumably to be worn with Tom Stoppard's Jumpers) is most noteworthy for a performance of Play which discovers a vein of humour missing from the original National Theatre production, and for the transformation of Krapp's Last Tape into a videocassette. How could he possibly have afforded the equipment?

*

Back to the Aldwych, to round off the World Theatre Season, come the Zulus in Umabatha. The dancing is the thing, to be relished without much reference to the Macbeth storyline, though the ceremonial does make you understand why a man might kill to become king. The original is closely followed, so don't bother to hire a translation receiver. I picked mine up to check out the hero's state of mind and found him wondering if that was an assegai he saw before him. As I suspected.
