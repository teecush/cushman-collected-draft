May 2, 1982

Just Camp

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Just Camp

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

ROBERT DAVID MACDONALD'S Summit Conference (Lyric) is a Glasgow Citizens' play and production, fitted out with a couple of West End stars. Glenda Jackson appears as Eva Braun and Georgina Hale as Mussolini's mistress, Clara Petacci, in an antechamber done up in battleship grey and looking like a lofty mausoleum.

This set was designed by Philip Prowse, Glasgow's resident genius, who also directed; and one of its fascinations is the amount of massive detail it crams in while remaining spacious. Even the sofa is monumental, while the red-swastika cushions that adorn it are the embodiments of Nazi chic. The scene is Berlin, the date 1941: offstage, with the war going against them, Hitler and Mussolini are conferring.

Their ladies take tea, cakes and liquor, and then begin to squabble. They slide into the identities of their masters and exchange rancorous generalities about the character and the war-record of their respective nations. One of the stunning insights that emerges--at some length--from their debate is that Hitler used the Jews as a scapegoat. This seems to be the subject Mr Macdonald is working towards; in the second act the girls turn on an attendant soldier and tauntingly pretend first that he is a girl prostitute, second that he is a Jew. There is a flash of light and he steps out of character to tell us that death-camps are horrible.

George Steiner once remarked how sinister it was that "camp" had become a vogue word for a flippant semi-style. I thought at the time that he was being both over-obsessive and over-ingenious, but Mr Macdonald has unwittingly proved him right. His play is camp in its details (Eva and Clara turn out to be movie-buffs) and in its general effect; it is a humourless joke. You might respect a dramatist who tried to put Hitler and Mussolini directly on stage; however painfully he failed; to do it at this remove is merely to add a layer of titillation.

You never believe in these characters. Possibly you are not meant to and the ideas are the thing. But Mr Macdonald says nothing new, and says it aridly. The pedagogical tone is relieved--no; it's reinforced--by the occasional Lady Bracknellism (Eva to Clara: "I advise you to find a persecutable minority by Christmas at the latest."). As for the performances: Miss Hale survives through a prima-donna accent and a tottering high-heeled gait; Miss Jackson, having established Eva's commonplaceness, has nowhere to go. As a fan of the Citizens, I have to admit that this production is a fair example of their style. This is how their shows look, this is how Mr Macdonald writes. It's also how he translates; but when he can spill himself over a great and usually inaccessible script, the staged results--though with some of the same self-admiring flavour--are very different.

At one point Eva-Hitler says: "I've done more for the Jewish consciousness than anyone since Arthur Balfour." This, to give Mr Macdonald his due, is something one can just imagine Hitler saying. It is utterly different from the "defence" advanced in Mr Steiner's Portage to San Cristobal, whose whole point was to confront us with subtleties and horrible historical ironies out of the Fuhrer's mental range. Steiner challenged himself to keep his sense of the Holocaust as something uniquely horrible; and he succeeded. It was a necessary and honourable book for him to write, and the dramatised version--while adding nothing--preserved the essentials.

*

C. P. Taylor's Good (now at the Aldwych) is a believable, and theatrically ingenious, picture of a man stumbling into genocide, and is the one Taylor play that justifies its author's loquacity; its hero has to keep talking to stop from thinking. Both these works have absurdly been accused of trying to whitewash Nazism. Orwell once wrote that an atrocity is worse than an atrocity story; it actually happens. These words imply a grim test. Steiner and Taylor survive it. Summit Conference collapses giggling.

*

Nigel Williams's W.C.P.C. (Half Moon) is a farce about police, corruption and cover-ups, with a sideline in Gay Liberation. The two themes don't quite cohere, and in the finale, enjoyable in itself, the second gets clean away from the first. It is a strange play: that keeps threatening to collapse and then recovering. After the first five minutes you wonder if Mr Williams can have anything left to say after establishing that all coppers are bent, except for one necessary innocent who happens to be a bigot. He hasn't; but he keeps inventing, and even throws in a few really funny lines, though more would be welcome.

Oh yes, the title. The play treats of a subdivision of the Vice Squad detailed to haunt public lavatories in order to arrest men engaging in homosexual activities, but also indulging itself in said activities on a large scale. These are not the circumstances in which you would expect to find Robert Stephens giving one of the best performances of his life, but there it is, and there he is.

He seems newly jowly, playing a police commander who combines a voracious sexual appetite with a public-school sense of propriety. He functions as both tempter and tempted, a combination that allows Mr Stephens a double ration of double-takes, all executed with consummate authority. He has always been good at bewilderment; now he has weight as well. Pam Brighton's production is very racy.

*

Beth Henley's The Miss Firecracker Contest (Bush) is, though never staged there, very Louisville Festival: that is, it takes a loving look at a stretch of the American hinterland which it has peopled with eccentrics. A mild word that. Miss Henley actually runs to screwballs, loonies and--let us not mince words--nutcases. But then her territory is Mississippi, and her style is southern Gothic.

In the small town of Brookhaven the event of the year is a beauty contest. The aspiring Miss Firecracker, first seen practising her tap routine (should she, she agonises, have chosen a recitation instead?), once known as Miss Hot Tamale, on account of her promiscuity, but she wants to live that down. Around her are her female cousin, herself an ex-beauty; her beloved male cousin who's had a chequered career and can do this trick where he wiggles his ears; the shy adoring girl next door who does her dresses; and the balloon salesman to whom she once gave the clap.

The dialogue is fine-spun but resilient; though the total effect--as of a soft-focus cartoon--does not engage as deeply as the author apparently wishes. She wants to touch as well as amuse; to her credit, she draws self-indulgent people without indulging them herself. Simon Stokes's production, sumptuously set and costumed, has especially good work from Sandra Dickinson as the contestant and David Beames as the beautifully off-the-wall dangerous relative.
