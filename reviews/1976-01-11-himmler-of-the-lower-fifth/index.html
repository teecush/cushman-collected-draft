Jan 11, 1976

Himmler Of The Lower Fifth

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Not unexpectedly Terence Rattigan's The Browning Version, 28 years old, stands up very well in revival at the King's Head. It has, it must be confessed, a fair amount to stand up to; Rattigan's dialogue may be understated, but his play is made up of confrontations and these need more space than the huddled stage of a pub theatre can afford.

The rhythm of Stewart Trotter's production is not firm enough to compensate; or at least it was not on the first nervous night. This was characterised by a succession of timorous exits and entrances, as the actors manoeuvred between a small door and a large folding screen. This object was prescribed by the author and his executants on this occasion may well have cursed him for it.

It is characteristic of Rattigan, though, that having ordered the screen, and made one of his characters remark on its kindness to eavesdroppers, he makes very little use of it. This is not carelessness; it is sophistication. Rattigan finds space, within the confines of a long one-act play, not merely to tell his story, but to comment on his own means of telling it: on what he doesn't use as well as what he does. The title, you may remember, refers to a translation of the Agamemnon, a story, as Rattigan's schoolmaster hero remarks, of a murderous wife and cuckolded husband.

This is precisely the situation of his own marriage, though it is spiritual rather than physical assassination that his partner has in view. About to depart from a school where he has been at worst loathed and at best taken for granted, Andrew Crocker-Harris, known as "The Crock" and worse, finds the courage to insist that, for once, he will not be passed over. He, and not a more popular master, will make the closing speech at the end-of-term assembly. It may be an anti-climax but, as he says, an anti-climax can be effective.

The elegance of this closing line is rather spoiled in Mr Trotter's production by a thunderous delivery from Nigel Stock; we are given no chance to judge the effectiveness of anti-climax because what we are offered is a climax, perhaps to make up for the excitement we have been denied in earlier scenes where the text provides for it. Mrs Crocker-Harris's most venomous action is to cast doubt on the sincerity of the boy who has presented her husband with a copy of the Browning version as a farewell present; it should chill the blood, but it passes by quite casually here.

Perhaps it is the fault of the play, or more precisely of my previous acquaintance with it. Rattigan's method of progressive revelation is particularly subject to the law of diminishing returns; people may keep their power to surprise, but not incidents. And apart from the Crock, the characters here are thin. The boy Taplow (a tenacious name in school fiction, as will be recognised by students of Professor Jimmy Edwards) veers quite arbitrarily from scorn to affection, and though Rattigan is careful to make his protagonist fair to his wife, he cannot manage to be quite that generous himself while she is on the stage. The result is that though The Browning Version compels attention (it is quite remorselessly watchable), it does not always reward it.

Mr Stock as the Crock is pretty good, but not really right. He is pudgy and crumpled not only in soul but in body, and so gives his author's game away from the start. He is not merely soft-centred (as the writing finally proves him to be), but soft-coated; when he is identified as the Himmler of the Lower Fifth, it seems odd and amusing rather than sad and inevitable. The rest of the acting is undistinguished, or the best of it is; Barbara Jefford is an actress I am always glad to see, but I feel she would be more at home as the Clytemnestra whom the play invokes than as the small-time harpy it actually provides.

The play, being less than great, suffers at the moment from its concerti with a style and a milieu we have grown unused to taking seriously, but (to answer the obvious question) I would be puzzled to name a current play that would revive as well in another 30 years.

*

Played with it is Carol's Christmas by Frank Marcus, an afterpiece on the first night, but a curtain-raiser thenceforward. Future audiences will thus be spared a bathos which threw a harshly ironic light on Crocker-Harris's last line. Mr Marcus is the only writer known to me who still takes waif-heroines seriously. This one is a prostitute who receives a visit from Santa Claus.
