Jan 3, 1982

Good Company

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Good company

Robert Cushman on Hull Truck and the RSC's pantomime.

I do not of course believe in decades, but I am prepared to suspend my disbelief in considering Mike Bradwell's 10 years as director of Hull Truck. He has largely devoted himself to showing us the flotsam of the Sixties surviving through the Seventies, succumbing to prosperity and disillusion. It has been an enjoyable trip, but now, a year into the Eighties, Mr Bradwell may be right to quit. He is leaving the company; Still Crazy After All These Years (Bush) is his valedictory.

The fans -- I speak as one -- will rush to see it; and so they should. They should in fact rush faster than I did; I arrived late, and could not be admitted until after the interval. Mr Bradwell, though, seemed quite agreeable to having me judge the show on its second half; and even provided me with a fast and entertaining rundown of the story thus far. (He is, as those who saw such early Truck shows as Oh What may recall, quite a performer.) I didn't feel that I had missed much plot.

What I saw was a four-sided conversation. There was a producer of TV rock shows and his wife, both of the above-mentioned generation. Then there were their juniors and house-guests: a French textile student with whom the producer has slept, and a new musician, hyping what he calls the new music by means of spiel and video-cassette. He arouses the enthusiasm of the young lady, a success and/or novelty worshipper currently en route to an Alternative Miss World shindig, and the mistrust of his elders. Somewhere, they seem to feel, rock sold out.

Scenes like these in Hull Truck shows inspire in me a malevolent delight that may be in excess of the company's intentions. My problem is that though I too am of the right age, I have never, from 1955 till now, felt any emotional allegiance to rock music. When confronted by a fight between two rock factions I can take no sides; I merely enjoy the discomfiture of both. I suppose I might concede that the electric guitar is a nobler noise than the synthesiser... no, I wouldn't. My sympathies are actually with the new boy; his music is terrible, but he never claims to be in it for anything but the money. That is what rock has always been about; and after the stultifying niceties of the previous generation it is a relief to hear it admitted.

The bile flows freely, and entertainingly. Thirzie Robinson, as the wife with reason to despise everyone else in the cast, conveys her disdain with witty rearrangements of haunches and eyebrows; Jonathan Kydd as the young prodigy displays a confident gaucherie that again confirms the power of improvised drama to evoke paradoxes and modes of speech no playwright could contemplate. The show presumes too much on previous acquaintance to rank with Hull Truck's best work, but as an entertainment, or whatever, it may well be forgiven that. Where Mr Bradwell goes next I don't know, and neither I believe does he; but he has behind him 10 years of the richest, sharpest and funniest work in the British theatre.

*

Hull Truck has attracted audience loyalty despite there being very few actors one instinctively associates with the group. (Rachel Bell and Alan David are the only ones who come to my mind.) The RSC has got itself a supporters' club audience by stressing permanence, and its pantomime, The Swan Down Gloves, which ended its run at the Aldwych last night, traded quite shamelessly on the punters' loyalty to company stalwarts. These included the house-playwright, impersonated with entrancing bashfulness by Michael Fitzgerald. Some more familiar players blossomed remarkably; Judy Buxton has never done anything as good as her Principal Boy, nor has Jonathan Hyde ever been so superbly stylish as here, playing a down-at-heel Puss-in-Boots.

Terry Wood, a vast actor noted for playing wrestlers, was positively the best Dame I have ever seen; and though I might have found the silently wandering Dragon just as touching if I hadn't known that Alan Howard was hanging in his scales, I wouldn't like to take the chance. Darker forces included David Suchet as an accident-prone Demon King, and Billie Brown as a treacherous baroness for whom I can identify no traditional model.

Mr Brown also wrote the show; as an Antipodean, he must view the English panto routine with bemused awe. That anyway is what emerged here; on an invented story concerning some magic gauntlets manufactured by the Bard's glover bruvver he hung just about every pantomimic ornament. He managed to celebrate and satirise the tradition at the same time; this admittedly is what often happens these days in official pantos (songsheets are seldom lowered without implicit or ribald apologies), but the fact that it was the RSC doing it undoubtedly added edge, as well as charm.

A lot of solemn guff is talked about company feeling; the main purpose of companies, as most RSC shows imply and this one proved, is to make audiences feel happy. This is not much different from the cosy feeling bred in seaside reps, nor should it be. It is something that the RSC, partly due to skill in public relations, has always been good at (in the Sixties anyone criticising David Warner risked lynching by schoolgirls); and it is something that Peter Hall, its original architect, has been either unwilling or unable to repeat on the South Bank. You go to the National's Christmas show Hiawatha for the staging and especially the set; the actors are all right, but they could be anybody. No accident perhaps that in their diplomatic show, The Oresteia, they have neither names nor faces.

Maybe the size of the complex imposes anonymity; things were not thus at the Old Vic. Maybe the Barbican will exert a similar influence. But I really believe that the RSC's secret weapon, the reason why it seems constantly to renew itself, is its patron, even though, with a couple of brilliant exceptions, it has not done too well by him this year. He deserved his starring role in The Swan Down Gloves. What other dramatist would stand being sent up for the best part of four hours?
