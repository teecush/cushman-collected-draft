Jan 22, 1978

Basho And The Tidal Wave

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Basho and the tidal wave

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Bond's 'The Bundle.'

MORE than likely Edward Bond's The Bundle will turn out to be the best play of 1978. It is certainly the best its author has written since Lear.

Like Lear, it has the force and the onward movement of a tidal wave. It communicates narrative joy; a complicated tale is to be told, spanning many years, and the playwright's craft in keeping us aware of our bearings is immaculate. Necessarily he writes many scenes, but he gives each one a shape and a dynamic. Where previous plays in the Warehouse season have reflected the staccato fragmentation of television, Mr Bond returns us to the weight and rhythm of epic theatre.

Talking of returns: The Bundle takes off from the same point as Mr Bond's 10-year-old Narrow Road to the Deep North. The seventeenth-century Japanese poet Basho sets out in search of enlightenment. He begins by crossing a river, bilking the ferryman ("I go to be reborn. Does the midwife charge the child?") and refusing to succour a baby left wrapped up on the bank by poverty-stricken parents. In Narrow Road the baby punished him by growing up and turning into a tyrant. In The Bundle the baby, for whom the play is named, does the same thing even more effectively by growing up and turning into a liberator.

Basho is in the grand time-serving tradition of Bondian poets: he becomes a judge and a ruthless servant of the established order, thus putting himself several strikes ahead of Mr Bond's Shakespeare in Bingo, who was merely a passive collaborator (and whose previous impersonator at the Warehouse, Patrick Stewart, now turns in a suitably flinty performance of Basho).

The territory is ruled by "the landowner" (unseen) and his main ally is the river; the iron fist of the one and the floods of the other keep the peasants in indigent subjection until Wang (the ex-bundle) teaches them how the landowner may be overthrown and the river tamed. His agents are a band of outlaws whom he has turned into guerrillas. Before this he has learned the enemy's ways by spending a period as Basho's servant: a post into which he was forced to sell himself to ensure the survival of his foster-parents, the ferryman and his wife.

The image of Wang shouting "Buy me, buy me" is a powerful one which somehow escapes self-consciousness; of all our current playwrights Mr Bond is the only one who can write large enough to bring this off. There is an even stronger moment later when the knives are out, and the ferryman has been killed by Basho's soldiers. His wife, half-senile, has been bound and gagged; she frees herself, but not to do or say anything: merely to scream. More than once Mr Bond seems to be learning from Brecht, and bettering the instruction.

Add to all this the bite and flavour of the language, a faithful production by Howard Davies and a performance of Wang by Mike Gwilym that releases all the strength, assurance and suppleness that have been welling up in this actor in the past few years, and we can get down to the play's flaw, which is one-sidedness.

One very discerning reviewer has claimed that the author maintains a measure of sympathy for Basho, but the drift of the play is against him. At the end Basho is still looking for enlightenment; and he is mocked, not honoured, for it. He is typed as the selfish aesthete (or holy man) seeking the good of his own soul at the expense of the general welfare. (He is pretentious too. All Mr Bond's characters speak with vigour and imagination of the river that rules their lives, but they see it only as a river. Basho alone goes on about it, in traditional literary fashion, as a source of spiritual regeneration.) And with him go all the humane scruples artists are accustomed to express if not to exemplify on the subject of means and ends.

"We have not," says Wang, "earned the right to be kind." He too has the chance of saving an abandoned child, but he throws it into the river. There is certainly irony here (where would he, or indeed the revolution, have been if Basho or the ferryman had done the same?), but it is not followed through. Wang's area of choice narrows from the moment he becomes a guerrilla. (His merry men, incidentally, speak in strange short phrases reminiscent of Mr Alfred Jingle or Big Chief Sitting Bull. Rather like the outlaws in The Two Gentlemen of Verona, they appear to choose Wang for their leader because he can talk grammar.) We are never made aware of the cost to Wang of revolution; or, except in the most general terms, of the cost to anyone else.

The violence in this play, compared to that in earlier Bond, is discreetly handled; but it is evident for all that. Evident, at least, when committed by the landowners; the process of Wang's revolt is unconvincingly bloodless, just as its outcome is unconvincingly idyllic. Unconvincing to me anyway; the fault may be in my temperament or conditioning but I believe it lies in the conditions of drama. We have spent years telling our radical playwrights that we know what they are against, but what are they for? Mr Bond shows us, and we must complain, ungratefully, of bathos. But it is more important that he has suppressed evidence and broken his own rules. In theory he endorses cruelty in a good cause; in practice he fails to present it.

The argument may be murky, but the style and the story are limpid. Most refreshing when you have to cope with the week's other offerings: both (to adopt a phrase I invented a couple of years ago and which inexplicably failed to catch on) transported Irish plays.

*

Ron Hutchinson's Says I, Says He (Theatre Upstairs) follows a couple of Ulstermen to the building-sites of London, and then back to Belfast, where they fall in, and then out, with the militant loyalists.

One reviewer has puzzlingly reported that they end up happy; actually they end up dead. What is more, they are foredoomed. Though the play has vigorous spurts of humour, boozy sexual or scatological (all fuelled by desperation and all excellently put over by the company, especially the swagger-bellied James Duggan), it is drenched in the most timelessly topical kind of Celtic gloom. It also mixes fact, fantasy, and flashback, which makes it difficult to follow.

It is simplicity itself, however, compared to the Caribbean-Irish musical comedy Kingdom Come! (King's Head), which recreates the Ulster situation on a tropical island, whose destiny is disputed by three rival groups, each consisting of one white person and one black. Power goes in the end to the people: quite distinct from their self-appointed representatives in the rest of the cast.

Kingdom Come! is a musical; much vital information, intended to be conveyed in the opening number, is lost in transit and never recovered. It is curious how prone the British musical is to return to its salad days, even when the basic subjects are as tough as this one, but Stewart Parker's lyrics are admirably turned if taken in isolation (I am in no position to judge their relevance to the action) and Shaun Davey's music, more Caribbean than Irish, is infectious, as are the performances. The costumes are good, too; though the lady responsible, whose name happens to be Maggie Smith, has been given what I could call unscrupulously large billing outside the theatre.
