Bewitching Much Ado

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Bewitching Much Ado

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Stratford and London.

THE STAGE for Much Ado About Nothing (Stratford) is literally divided by a wall of perspex. There is a door cut in it, through which you may glimpse a couple of chairs. The effect is so dazzling that I thought at first I was seeing them in a mirror. The director, Terry Hands, does his own lighting, bathing the set in gold, then sepia, then gold again. The final fade-out leaves Beatrice and Benedick dancing and bantering in creamy-white, like the primal Fred and Ginger.

One of the best Astaire-Rogers pictures, 'Swing Time,' was originally to be called 'Never Gonna Dance'; the central conceit was the length of time it took to get the predestined pair into each other's arms. That is also the pattern of this production; the story of two wallflowers lured into the sun. (A red orb actually flames above them at the close.) The Messina they inhabit is one in which everybody else dances at the slightest provocation. Even old Leonato essays a few delighted steps when hearing of Don Pedro's victory. The minor villain Borachio, talking of 'what a deformed thief this fashion is,' suits action to words.

Thereby he clues us in to the more dangerous qualities of the play's surface: merriment. Claudio (Robert O'Mahoney) is the very essence of fashion, ready to woo Hero, reject her and regain her with equal sincerity and equal shallowness. There is a disturbing tinge of melancholy to Don Pedro's role and a quality which return Derek Godfrey to his best form: at the end, celibate and alone, he is as much an outcast as Jaques or Malvolio. Or as his own bastard brother, elegantly portrayed by John Carlisle; there is a real family likeness here, with both black-clad siblings taking a delight in plotting.

In this atmosphere it is not surprising that Derek Jacobi and Sinead Cusack should present Benedick and Beatrice as vessels of mutual detestation rather than affectionate raillery. The theory is fine, but the result is initially little fun. Mr Jacobi fares the better of the two; an experienced comedian, he can use his expertise to set off his rancour. The scene of his gulling is enlivened by a running gag concerning the boy who, sent to bring him a book, keeps returning with it at inopportune moments. (Mr Hands may be the first director ever to have found a rationale for this character.)

The protagonists have to wait for their flowering until the church scene, where--glowingly fitted out, like the whole company, in white glad rags--they find love as a reaction to their shared distaste for the world around them. There is a violent tension to this scene, sustained in Benedick's confrontation of his former friends, whose callousness is for once allowed full rein.

Terry Woods's Dogberry is similarly shown to be a bully as well as--or rather than--a clown; and there are charming glimpses of a laid-back pipe-smoker among the Watch. The last scene is a brilliant medley of moods, with the stars prolonging their brinkmanship to the end. This is not the funniest or most touching 'Much Ado' I have seen, but it is among the most intelligent; while in its trappings, Ralph Koltai's sets, Alexander Reid's cavalier costumes, Nigel Hess's music--it is in a bewitching class of its own.

*

Now for austerity. The Prince of Homburg might be the title for a play about the deputy drama critic of The Times, who has a dashing line in headgear. There is little dashing about the show at the Cottesloe. To suggest what is missing from it, let me quote the first stage direction of Kleist's text (James Kirkup's translation): 'Fehrbellin, a garden, in the old French style. In the background, a castle with a balustraded slope leading down from it. It is night. Early summer . . .' We then read of the Prince 'lying on a garden seat', 'half-sleeping under an oak-tree', plaiting himself a wreath. Other characters 'steal quietly out of the castle and look down upon the Prince from the balustrade.'

A modern production does not have to reproduce all those details, but it had better find suitable magical equivalents. John Burgess's production contrives an impressive grouping of spectators, but reduces setting to a bench and a nondescript backcloth.

Stripped of its dream elements, and of any sense of its Prussian nationalism being either challenged or confirmed, the play becomes a slow, bewildering melodrama about a military hero who leads an unauthorised cavalry charge like Sergius in Arms and the Man, but unlike him is subjected to imprisonment, sentence of death, and eventual pardon at the whim of an inscrutable Elector. We are given no reason to care; characters merely line up and say the words--in the case of Patrick Drury's Prince, with very little expression.

You might claim this to be a plain-text production, but how can that be true when Kleist's madness is missing? Whether it can ever be released in performance is another question. On the strength of this version and the one that opened the Royal Exchange (better-acted but visually a mess), I am inclined to doubt it. But I would be delighted to be proved wrong. Whatever the play is, it is not an earnest pre-Brechtian military chronicle. At one moment when we hear of the prince seeing his own grave--some power is released.

*

The best thing about Rents (Lyric Studio, Hammersmith) is that since writing it Michael Wilcox has gone on to other plays. It was first staged at the Edinburgh Traverse, and if I had seen it there I would probably have roared at the local jokes; even here, some of them still tickle. They are attached to the story of two part-time male prostitutes; one is otherwise engaged as a drama student, the other as a shop assistant. Into their joint orbit comes a college lecturer; just outside prowls a crazy queer-basher.

As a pattern of jealousy and violence emerges, we begin to realise that very little is happening that could not occur in heterosexual relationships; that we are in fact watching a thoroughly conventional play, chopped up in contemporary fashion into innumerable short scenes (plus monologues) which steer the play towards nothing but tedium. In Chris Parr's production, multiple playing-areas are handled with--for this director--amazing clumsiness.

*

There is more bottom-drawer reclamation in Stephen Lowe's Funny Pictures (Gate at the Latchmere), two one-act plays staged years ago by Alan Drury. The first brings Donald McGill postcard characters to life; to point the transition from old vulgarity to new porn: a bright and pertinent idea, but not, on this showing, capable of much development. The second goes back further for its nostalgia: to wartime Britain, where we meet a provincial cinema manager, his senior usherette, an aspiring junior and a GI.

Age attempts to get off with youth, with predictably disillusioning consequences. Mr Lowe seems steeped in the period, and though his elongated treatment invites tedium, his writing is mostly (not always) shrewd and sympathetic enough to avert it. The acting quartet is the same for both plays; but happier in the second, where Janet Dale has a good line in acerbic suffering.
