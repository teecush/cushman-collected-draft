Oct 30, 1977

Rampaging From Naples To Norwich

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Rampaging from Naples to Norwich

This week a play by the most important Italian playwright since Pirandello opens in London, directed by Zeffirelli. ROBERT CUSHMAN reports.

JOAN PLOWRIGHT and Colin Blakely are getting married again. They appear as husband and wife in Eduardo de Filippo's play Filumena which arrives in the West End (on Wednesday, at the Lyric) only a couple of weeks after Sidney Lumet's film of Equus, in which they are Peter Firth's parents.

Equus was shot in and around Toronto; and it was during one of the lulls in filming (since it takes time to turn the Canadian into the English landscape) that Miss Plowright first broached the subject of Filumena to Mr Blakely.

Her own acquaintance with de Filippo goes back to Saturday Sunday Monday, the penultimate production of the Olivier regime at the National Theatre, and one of its greatest successes. There was a warmth to it that took audiences completely by surprise. And indeed the audience response took the actors by surprise. As Joan Plowright tells it, after the first euphoric stage of rehearsal, "we got into a stalemate. We couldn't go further without an audience. There's no other playwright who needs audience contact so much." I suppose that's because Eduardo writes for a company of actors.

The use of the Christian name, by the way, is not green-room cosiness. De Filippo comes from Naples--he, in fact, personifies the Neapolitan theatre, and there he is, by his own desire, "Eduardo" to everyone. Apart from anyone else, it helps to differentiate him from his brother Peppino, who is, like him, an actor-manager. Both have been seen, with their companies, at World Theatre seasons in London. At one time, however, they acted together in Eduardo's plays, alongside their sister Titina--a magnificent actress, by all accounts, who created what we are coming to think of as the Joan Plowright parts.

Eduardo himself was once called by Gordon Craig the greatest actor alive, and his one appearance in London (in his own Napoli Millionaria) certainly revealed to us a formidable talent, with an amazing gift for making himself quietly at home on a stage and simply existing. He is also generally regarded as the most important Italian playwright since Pirandello, which adds up to as much prestige as any one man should be entrusted with.

Eduardo is now 77; Ben Travers, with whom he has recently struck up a great friendship, refers to him affectionately as "the old chap." (Mr Travers is 90). Though he speaks no English, Eduardo has been over for rehearsals of Filumena and was particularly delighted when the play, on its pre-London tour, seemed to go down well in Norwich. Naples to Norwich really augurs universality.

Filumena was actually the de Filippo play the National first thought of doing as its standard-piece in Italy (written in 1946), it was the only play of his that had crept into the English anthologies. Kenneth Tynan presented Miss Plowright with the only available translation, which she thought too bad to permit any judgment on the play. At this point, Franco Zeffirelli, who had wanted for years to introduce Eduardo's plays to Britain, announced the existence of the far more recent Saturday Sunday Monday. All de Filippo plays are rooted in family life and the family in this one is larger and more rambunctious than most, so it seemed a good bet for the National, then at one of its strongest periods as an ensemble, and so it proved.

The author gave his blessing, only remarking that Miss Plowright seemed too young for Rosa, the materfamilias (and virtuoso ragu cook) of the newer play, though she would be just right for Filumena. This bewildered the lady a little, since there's only about four years' distance between the characters, but she played Rosa to everyone's great satisfaction and, four years having indeed passed, has embarked on the other.

Once again Keith Waterhouse and Willis Hall have translated and Zeffirelli directs; this despite a well-publicised row between author and director after the Broadway failure of Saturday Sunday Monday, done with an ad-hoc cast, and resulting in pressurised caricature. Filumena, as it happens, is more of a star vehicle, and the weight rests on the two leads.

Mr Blakely plays a rich confectioner; Miss Plowright is his long-time mistress, an ex-prostitute who, just before the play begins, has tricked him into a deathbed wedding and then, infuriatingly, failed to die. ("A nice change to do a Neapolitan whore after that repressed lady in The Bed Before Yesterday.") Wife, whore or both, Filumena is emphatically a mother; and motherhood, rather than marriage, is the family piety invoked in the play.

Surprisingly, though they were both National Theatre luminaries over the same long period, the two stars have only been together in one play before. That was Saint Joan, in which Mr Blakely made a ghostly appearance as the English soldier in the epilogue. It hardly counts as a duet; he was able to stay home till nine, and made a civilised progress to the theatre while she was getting tried and burned.

Both players, by the way, are among those who have helped to make regional voices acceptable; he's Belfast, she's Scunthorpe. Part of the fun of Saturday Sunday Monday was hearing an Italian overlay on familiar accents; this was not, contrary to general belief, Zeffirelli's idea. ("You don't," he used to say, "put on Russian accents to do Chekhov.") But the actors found there was no other way to match up to the emotions. And anyway, there's a sense in which Zeffirelli asked for it; a sense in which Joan Plowright likens him to Sidney Lumet directing Equus. They both say: "I don't want any of this English good-taste acting. You can be appalling at the way you find yourself giving forth."
