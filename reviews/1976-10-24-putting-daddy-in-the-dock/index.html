Oct 24, 1976

Putting Daddy In The Dock

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Putting Daddy in the dock

by Robert Cushman

THE leading character of Denis Cannan's Dear Daddy (Ambassadors) is not always affectionately addressed as the title might suggest. Three of the cast are his children and they seem uneasy about what to call him. They seem uneasy about him altogether, and he knows it. His impulse is to blame it on them, or on youth generally. 'It's a sad generation that doesn't know what to call its parents' is his phrase for it.

He has a phrase for everything. His name is Bernard, and he is very verbal. In one of his aspects he bears out Howard Brenton's claim that the West End is the theatre of envy. I was certainly jealous of Bernard's collection of books; the towering shelves were enough to get me going. They dominate a set which bears witness to good things having come Bernard's way in the past.

Recently, though, they have stopped coming; and envy drops dead at the foot of the library steps. His brood, now in their twenties, have come to sign over to him money that was being kept in trust for them. Filial duty done, they spend the rest of the play remembering, in a long crescendo of bitterness, the details of life with father. Mother drops in to help them; she is Bernard's first wife, who was drying out at a nearby health-farm and just happened to be passing. (Mr Cannan is a bit cavalier about mustering his forces; the action presupposes an ever-open front door.)

All together they accuse him of surpassing, patronising selfishness; of subduing their personalities to his own, of always being ready to crush them with a quotation, of taking from them and never giving. Ex-wife claims that he laughed her out of her own career in children's publishing; present wife, who at first seems wholesomely inclined to avoid the battle, confirms that he has done the same to her talent for making clothes: 'It's known as "My sewing!"' As for the children, Charles, the youngest, a trainee teacher, is comparatively gentle but owes his survival to a girl-friend who is, by the family's standards, hopelessly unsophisticated; nothing exposes them more than their efforts to be friendly to her and Charles himself is shaping up to be as exploitative as his father. Brother Billy is a nondescript drop-out, Daddy's favourite butt; sister Gillian, who attracts and dispenses more venom than anyone else on stage, is an espouser of causes, and a ferocious victim, sexual and social, making more money as a typist than she ever could with her degree. (It could be the theme-song of a generation: women who are making it without men: divorcees, widows, unmarried mothers, etc.)

*

There is a poignant wedding of theatre to title: An' Me Wi' A Bad Leg Tae at the Theatre Upstairs. A Puerto Rican girl in A Chorus Line refused to change her name 'because I figured ethnic was in.' In response to Billy Connolly's first play, she figured wrong.

Mr Connolly is among the nakedest emperors to ride into London for some time. A soldier bound for Germany returns to the family hearth in Glasgow, and sets off some sub-O'Casey rowing, philosophising, boozing and raging, leading a clansman to a dead end, punctuated by spotlit monologues as a guarantee of seriousness. There is a rich and subtle portrait of a drunk by Bill Paterson, whose complexion sets you thinking of rubies or beetroot, and some good old jokes: 'I fought with Monty.' 'You could never get on with anyone.' There is even the one in which X says that he didn't come here to be insulted and Y replies as Y has immemorially replied. The innocent delight of most of the Press seems the innocentest thing on view.

*

Frank Dunlop's production of Antony and Cleopatra (Young Vic) is vigorous and fast. Delphine Seyrig's Cleopatra is vigorous and intelligent. Michael Graham Cox's Antony is vigorous. You will gather that all this, though welcome, is insufficient. Except in one scene, the soldiers' litany as Hercules, fares so well, the play is denied its wonder, hence its grandeur, hence its breadth, hence finally its point.

*

That a comedian as gifted as Ron Moody should have failed, for 15 years, to find himself a viable vehicle suggests a certain self-destructiveness. On the evidence of The Showman (Theatre Royal, Stratford) his fault may be excessive diffidence. In this musical of The Bells Mr Moody plays not the tormented villain Mathias, but the purely ancillary role of the hypnotist; his elaborate routines confuse the issue but cannot, unfortunately, stop the show. The score confirms that musical merit has nothing to do with memorability; though I fled at the interval two tunes pursued me pitilessly. Perhaps I should have stayed, but as Mr Moody said after a particularly strenuous exhibition of goodwill, Qu'est-ce que vous voulez, blood?
