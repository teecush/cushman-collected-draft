Back To New Orleans

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Back to New Orleans

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Tennessee Williams and Shakespeare

DO YOU KNOW what it means to miss New Orleans? Tennessee Williams does and over the years his public have come to share the feeling. We have all wished him a return to the quality of A Streetcar Named Desire and if the quality, then why not the locale? Vieux Carré (Piccadilly) returns us to both; and there has hardly been a more satisfying evening in the West End since the revival of Streetcar at this same theatre.

While we are harking back, we may as well recall Mr Williams's other classic, The Glass Menagerie. Vieux Carré inherits its technique of first-person narration. Indeed, however centrally placed, the Writer is less a character in Vieux Carré than an extension of the Tom, now come a reasonably long way from St Louis if not, I would guess, very far at all from Tennessee.

One calls the setting a rooming-house by courtesy. The landlady Mrs Wire professes to think highly of it but her tenants refer to cubicles rather than rooms. Their comfort is not increased by Mrs Wire's practice of sleeping on a camp bed in the hall, all the better to see them from. She disapproves, volubly, of all of them: of the tubercular painter Nightingale who seduces the Writer; of Jane the genteel youngish New Yorker shacked up with a stud from a strip-joint; even of the two pathetic old ladies who stray about a kitchen when they sniff home-made chicken gumbo. They do this, oddly, in the middle of the night, but as they explain, 'our light bulbs are burned out so we can't tell day from night anymore.'

The Writer gets more of her tongue than any of them but he also gets more of her indulgence. He even works for her for a time, rustling up customers for her 25 cent bargain dinners. The slogan 'eat for a quarter in the quarter' may count as his first published work.

Half-way through the evening Mrs Wire wreaks vengeance on her basement lodgers, who have broken the terms of their lease by holding orgies (she says), and pours down boiling water upon them through a gap in the floorboards. For this she is taken to court and fined. I could not see that this event, however centrally placed, had much effect on the rest of the action, but you do not go to Vieux Carré for its plot. You go rather to hear the author, frank and relaxed, raising self-plagiarism to new heights and admitting it in his title. The process has had a rejuvenating effect on his dialogue, which has never been funnier. (It is in fact the best comic writing in London.)

There is now a refreshing wryness about his treatment of sex, homo and hetero; it still permits passion but banishes melodrama. Jane is a miniature re-working of Blanche Dubois, but considerably easier to take; she does not pose, and she is afforded no convenient black-outs. You see her hating herself and her lover, and succumbing; you also see why. Mr Williams the black romancer is still in evidence, in a long speech about a beautiful woman -- 'the champagne girl' -- devoured by dogs, but it is a necessary hideousness: an emblem of the world from which Mrs Wire's establishment provides at least a kind of shelter.

Vieux Carré is also a tribute -- maybe an unconscious one -- to a form of stagecraft long in disuse but still potent: the theatre of Elia Kazan, where music faded in and out at the precise atmospheric moment, houses were seen in section (sometimes, as here, with the aid of a revolve) and characters moved effortlessly into pools of light with retrospective intent.

In Keith Hack's loving production (there's a phrase I never expected to write) it is served by actors obviously overjoyed to have, for once, something to act. Richard Kane is perhaps a bit fuzzy as Nightingale and Jonathan Kent's bedroom champ may try too hard, though his efforts are usually rewarded. But Sylvia Miles is a barbed and electric Mrs Wire and Sheila Gish was hauntingly bruised as Jane. (Her subsequent exit from the part greatly surprised me; she seemed perfectly in tune with it.) Neither lady, blessedly, is pathetic.

The Writer is Karl Johnson, hitherto known in the complete works of Nigel Baldwin at the Theatre Upstairs, and possessed of a curiously compelling face, ratlike but vulnerable, and of a personality to match: open and inscrutable. For Mr Williams's surrogate, moving towards professional detachment, he is a perfect and imaginative choice.

*

More riches at Stratford: John Barton's production of Love's Labour's Lost is the season's best so far (though his Merchant runs it close) -- not so much a discovery of unknown riches as a clear demonstration that those in which we always believed do actually exist. Mr Barton directs it as a play about four undergraduates who forsake the world because they are afraid to face it, are thrown by the arrival of four ladies, try to get the love-game together, are made ludicrous by the disclosure of their pretensions, try to put a good face on things, think they have succeeded and are defeated by the arrival of an unalterable reality, Death.

None of this, I am aware, is news, but I have never seen it lived so thoroughly before; never heard such delight taken in the scholars' affectations, felt the mood change so palpably at the last, or known a Berowne keep so precise a balance between badinage and lyricism, sense and surrender, as Michael Pennington. (He starts as a disembodied intelligence, merely accomplished, and grows.) Backing him is a delightfully timid King from Richard Griffiths, as hesitant as he has in former roles been solid, and opposed is a Princess (Carmen du Sautoy in a spectacular ginger wig) who though she can teach him something of life, is visibly learning herself.

The finest touch of the evening comes in the show of the Nine Worthies: one of the performing clowns lets his spear droop; the Princess comes forward to help him, and the King to help her; a three-way rapprochement. Allan Hendrick is a marvellously assured Costard, and there is a parkful of talent besides, the extremes of experience meeting in Michael Hordern's Armado, who is rather like Quixote, and Jo James's page, who is nothing at all like Sancho. There is a soothing arboreal set (most uncharacteristic of its designer Ralph Koltai) and the action, mostly taking place on an oblong forestage, gets closer to the audience than is usually believed, in this theatre, to be possible.

*

Ken Campbell's The Great Caper was claimed by some to be ahead of its time when first seen at the Royal Court. Maybe it was just ahead of me. Anyway I found the current revival at the ICA far funnier, even though Mike Bradwell's production is considerably less polished than the improvised work on which the company, Hull Truck, has made its name. Mr Campbell's mystical-farcical tirades are delivered with the right offhanded delight, though the actors sometimes seem foxed by the absence of anything much to say. Meanwhile. There is action, oh yes there is action, and it takes strange shapes, but it doesn't come round very often.

Michael Abbensetts's Alterations (New End) is also dependent on its verbals, which again are well dispensed. Twenty years ago it would have been a Jewish play; now the minority group trying to get a footing in and through the rag-trade are West Indians, and at their best they can be similarly salty. The central situation -- a man working himself and his colleagues and his marriage to exhaustion -- never takes proper root, but the sparks fly around it with reasonable frequency.
