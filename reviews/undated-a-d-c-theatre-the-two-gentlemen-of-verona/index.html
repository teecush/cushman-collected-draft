A.D.C. Theatre: The Two Gentlemen of Verona

Robert Cushman · Broadsheet

Undated Cambridge student-theatre review, probably early to mid-1960s. Searches for the Carey Harrison production and its named cast did not establish a reliable year or issue date. Robert Cushman is named on the surviving source.

A.D.C. Theatre: The Two Gentlemen of Verona

[Editorial note: The two surviving pages join without a textual gap. No publication date is visible on either page.]

The externals of this University Actors production exhibited a sureness of touch which, in the context of recent Cambridge theatre, was breathtaking. The stair-swept setting possessed an unforced elegance which set the mood of the entire evening. This was at any rate the best-framed production seen for some time. It was also the most exactly organised.

Carey Harrison directed the play as a progression from sunlit idyll to abrupt disillusion. John Taylor's superb lighting set the pattern, the cyclorama fading from the oceanic blue of everybody's tourist Italy into a dimmer, harsher shade. The ending was discordant—Silvia, given in marriage to Valentine, strode off obviously experiencing the severest doubts as to the happiness of the ending. And no wonder, since David Cheshire played her intended on a sustained note of peacock complacency which continually discovered new poses—passionate lover, reflective outlaw-captain—to replace his original one of the bluff scoffer at love. He did it, for the most part, very well, and his first interview with his witty lady was an object-lesson in the playing and direction of Shakespearean comedy—Cheshire and Jean Liddiard handled the scene impeccably, and the lines turned out to be really funny; if the director had drawn as much from the text in subsequent scenes the production would have been an unqualified triumph. But, in the final count, the interpretation misfired. A tenacious belief in psychological consistency (Valentine starts shallow and goes on being shallow) runs counter to the whole texture of this thin, immature play. It is like treating Romeo's love for Juliet as of a piece with his infatuation with Rosaline. The cocksure anti-romantic toppled by love is a fairly constant feature of the comedies; as Berowne and Benedick he develops a sense of humour of which Valentine is innocent, but the genuineness of the passion is never called in serious question by the mockery. And if the love-theme is slighted there is precious little in TWO GENTLEMEN to hang on to; the change of emphasis in this production emerged as a cunning but unconvincing attempt to convict Shakespeare of having written a more interesting play than he actually achieved—a dark comedy twelve years before the fact. Harrison took Silvia's silence during the last hundred lines as a sign of hurt pride; my guess is that her author just couldn't be bothered to write her in.

Proteus was left in an uneasy limbo, with the sympathy that usually goes to Valentine hovering just outside his grasp. Roger Gartland's performance was puzzling; he has the exceptional actor's ability to compel attention even when not doing anything in particular, but I felt he over-used it in this role. He played with a devout intensity which carried complete conviction in the early scenes, but lacked the mercurial touch necessary to make acceptable Proteus' chameleonic changes of front. His plotting was half-hearted; maybe this was the intention, but it robbed the play of its dynamic at the precise moment when the surface charm began to pall and fresh excitement was needed. The conspiratorial soliloquy was a piece of monotonous recitation spoken against a strumming guitar which was in constant evidence throughout. Immensely useful atmospherically; it irritated me when employed as a background to speech, distracting from the words and imposing a lulled rhythm which, given players as vocally active as Gartland and Liz Wrangham, was a pity. Her Julia was still the outstanding performance, the only one which entirely bridged the gap between the play's flippant and serious moods. Eric Idle's rueful Launce went some way along the same road, though, like, I should think, every other Launce in theatrical history, he was outmanoeuvred by his dog.

I saw this production on its first night when its beguiling leisureliness spilled over, towards the end, into a slightly soporific calm. Later in the week it seems to have gained a good deal in firmness. It had a character, an identity; and for these rarities at least there can be only gratitude.

Robert Cushman
