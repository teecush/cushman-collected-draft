Dec 18, 1983

In Acton --And India

Robert Cushman · The Observer

In Acton --and India

THEATRE by ROBERT CUSHMAN

BRIAN THOMPSON last appeared on a London theatre programme as author of a National West End comedy called Tishoo. He now has a play at the Bush called Turning Over, and it is a beauty. The play is exotically set in India and Acton. Not that the Acton shown here is the Acton I remember, rather it is the new featureless could-be-anywhere Acton as colonised by the BBC.

In a Shepherd's Bush theatre, Mr Thompson's play must almost count as community drama. Half the stage represents a cutting-room, where editor, director and writer in that order of importance are toiling over a documentary that was shot on the other half, back to which we constantly flash. It is part of a misbegotten series entitled I Could Be Happy Here. The writer, a children's author new to television, decided that he could be happy in India. So they took him there.

Happiness, rather than India, is what the play is about. It is, as the bumper-stickers have always proclaimed, different things to different people. It is also, as the bumper-stickers forgot, unattainable, especially if you go looking for it. This is not a revolutionary idea, but ideas are less important in plays than the way in which they are fleshed out, and here Mr Thompson scores highly.

He distributes his theme right across his cast. The chippy editor in Acton, the lugubrious sound-man in India, mainly want to get their respective jobs done; they stand a chance. The director is pushier; he wants a finished film and a divorce and the opportunity to look down on the writer, who was brighter at school than he was.

The writer is the most muddled of all, increasingly conscious of his presumption in projecting his own dreams onto a society of which he knows nothing, and grabbing frantically at an illusory permanence with an expatriate married lady on the basis of -- if I counted rightly -- a two-night stand. Mr Thompson also throws in a P.A. girl, rushed out from England at short notice, and projecting her dreams on to a middle-aged Indian tour-guide with problems of his own.

The casual way in which Mr Thompson brings this young lady into the centre of a picture to which she had seemed peripheral, is quite masterly. Indeed all the parts are worth acting, and all in David Hayman's production are well played; especial mention for Gary Waldhorn's puzzled ache ("I have no influence; I write for children"), Charles Dance's sparky spikiness, and Geraldine James's placid radiance.

Mr Thompson stands with Stephen Fagan (The Hard Shoulder) among a new breed of acute social comedians, the heirs of Alan Ayckbourn and Peter Nichols. And though he has Scarborough training, it is Mr Nichols (who is earth to Mr Ayckbourn's air) that he more resembles. He also has television training, and much of his play's appeal comes from the hilarious accuracy of its ear for jargon and its eye for the boredom and indignity of filming. Note how those at home are always accurate when speculating on what may have happened sexually on location. And why has nobody thought before of acting out the way that sight and sound get distorted when a film is run backwards.

*

As it happens, they have thought of it again in Abbacadabra (Lyric, Hammersmith) which, in conception at least, is a super-pantomime, corralling characters from four or five different fairy-tales and turning them loose in a staged video-game, with three modern children to show them the works. The title, which you had better look at again, is occasioned by the musical interruptions: hits by Abba fitted out with new lyrics, which, since they don't belong, are even worse than the old. This is music that rots the teeth and stops the action, what there is of it.

The show's central conceit, pleasant and accurate, is that kids are far more at home with technology than are grown-ups, but they cope here so easily as to deprive David Wood's script of danger and finally of interest. Nor, apart from the odd mechanical wonder, is any supplied by Peter James's production; he seems to have used up his current stock of energy on his ace direction of Blondel.

The stage is occupied by actors given too little to do and non-actors given too much, though in the latter category B. A. Robertson comes through as an engaging Beast (as in Beauty and). Dexter Fletcher is a masterful schoolboy wizard and Elaine Paige as the wicked fairy tries hard, but fruitlessly, to send up her own Eva Peron. But she is unexpectedly convincing as a frumpy teacher in prologue and epilogue.

The mixture of myths, though, is a tempting economy offer, pre-empting both the West End's Aladdin and the National's Cinderella (Lyttelton), on whose first night a child in the audience stopped the show by shouting, as Buttons improvised a ball-dress for Cinders, "she hasn't got a belt." This was the best line of the evening, though it was closely followed by those which Tony Haygarth, a charmingly bashful Buttons, had to invent to save the situation.

Anyway, some contact with the target-audience was obviously achieved. (Of course you may doubt whether National Theatre children are real children. Give them another year and they'll be on the mailing-list.) To this particular Scrooge, however, the evening seemed supine. The script is "adapted by Bill Bryden, Trevor Ray and the company" from what sounded to me, with my limited experience of panto at the Players', like a remarkably sub-standard Victorian original.

The couplets, which are almost constant, are not gorgeously awful, but just execrably limp. They imprison the actors; you can feel Derek Newark, Robert Stephens, Jack Shepherd and the others pining to get out and be funny, but to no avail. The only escapers are Janet Dibley in one song as the heroine, and two guys who emerge from within the pantomime horse to perform a highly professional whitewash routine.

Susan Fleetwood's Prince, William Dudley's backcloths, and the transformation scene (with real ponies) are all worth gazing at; but the total experience makes you feel nostalgic for the commercial product. I may of course regret having said that before the week is out.

*

The School for Scandal (Duke of York's) was, at its final preview, in a semi-comatose state, certainly as far as the scandalous college itself was concerned. Things improved when the plot veered towards domesticity, with Nicola Pagett a Lady Teazle of great comedic skill, if hardly a country girl, and Donald Sinden a Sir Peter who made all his humorous and emotional points, even if he did deposit them too firmly in the audience's laps.

There is too a ripely fruity Sir Oliver from Michael Denison, a Joseph from Clive Francis of amusingly stylised primness, and in Julia Josephs a Maria who bounds on as if to announce that she knows this is a thankless part but is not going to be defeated by it, and, as it turns out, isn't. John Barton's actual staging, on Christopher Morley's silver-gilt sets, is often admirable; the screen scene, for once, almost lives up to its reputation.
