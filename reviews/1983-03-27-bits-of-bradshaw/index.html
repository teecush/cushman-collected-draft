Mar 27, 1983

Bits Of Bradshaw

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Bits of Bradshaw

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Howard Barker's new play at the Royal Court.

There is a current form of play that proceeds by seizing a small historical moment, and imagining around it. An example is Howard Barker's Victory (Royal Court), which begins with the exhumation of John Bradshaw.

Bradshaw was president of the judges who condemned Charles I to death; at the restoration of Charles II he was one of those whose corpse was dug up, gibbeted and exhibited. Bradshaw's wife, or widow, is the central character of this play. Described in text and programme simply as Bradshaw--either for feminist reasons or because Mr Barker did not want to remind us of the Mrs Bradshaw who featured so prominently in early soap-powder commercials--she goes through the play searching for the remains of her severed spouse.

By the end she has assembled all the bits, including the head: a collectors' piece, since the current monarch has been using it as a mascot. Bradshaw's quest thus serves dramatically as a means of bringing disparate characters together. This is Mr Barker's shapeliest play; his penultimate scene is as comprehensive a reunion as could be found outside a Shakespearean epiphany or a pantomime finale: less cheerful though.

Apart from Bradshaw and the monarch (Sellar and Yeatman laid it down that Charles II, being merry, was less a King than a Monarch, and Mr Barker's Charles likes it that way) those present include the founder of the Bank of England, a semi-discarded royal mistress, a court poet, a cashiered Cavalier bully who has spent the play in lubricious pursuit of Bradshaw, the atheistic Puritan who has accompanied her on her flight, and her long-lost son, a medic now specialising lucratively in venereology. They make up a ripe slice of post-revolutionary old England: a state-of-the-nation play in wigs.

Victory is Bondishly subtitled Choices in Reaction. Main title and sub are both, of course, loaded with puns. There is the royalist victory, which leads to acts of revenge on dead bodies and a reign of terror against the living carried out by pox-ridden old soldiers. There is Bradshaw's victory in coming through this landscape with herself, if not her husband, in one piece. There is her reaction to her situation, which is to keep her head down and her pockets, where possible, full; and there is that of her male companion, who blunders outspokenly into martyrdom, but happens to be consumed by guilt at having revealed the dead leveller's grave in the first place.

Bradshaw thinks this man is a fool, and she is probably right. Her own course is not heroic, but it is not despicable either, and any blame can be attached to the times rather than to her. Some of her actions may be reactionary; as are certainly those of the king, trying to hang on to his prerogative, and of the new financiers, trying to limit it. Reactionary of course meaning right-wing; by what romantic-semantic convention do we accept that the left never reacts to anything?

A primal banking conference is cartooned in Mr Barker's best brand of deadly knockabout, and he is very good on sex: from Charles's compulsive fumblings to Bradshaw's enforced composure after rape to her assailant's disappointment: 'I come off so miserable. I think love would be to come off and be happy.' That is the best moment in the play. On the other hand Charles's melancholia is over-extended, and his courtiers are grotesques assembled so that the author can tell us at length how grotesque they are.

The movement from one set piece to another tends to be clotted. But the dialogue can be off-handedly funny and is modern without obvious anachronism. Continual expletives do a lot to link the two periods, though I had an old-maidish sympathy with the odd character who wished they would stop from time to time. Bradshaw, in this Joint Stock production, provides a superb role for Julie Covington, who excels in commonsense and bewilderment, the last reserved for people--the majority--who do not see the world as clearly as she does. Also notable are Eleanor David as two strumpets and a maid, David Lyon and Kenny Ireland. Nearly everyone in fact.

Clegg, Mr Barker's poet, says that he has written a tragedy and been promised production 'for what a promise is worth, not a lot in the theatre.' That little in-joke gets us to Michael Rudman's Short List (Hampstead), which is about the deliberations of a committee judging a playwriting award apparently modelled on that given annually in memory of George Devine. At least, the Harry whom this one commemorates, and whom the characters obsessively remember, was a producer much given to encouraging new talent.

His heirs are a shifty but engaging bunch: rather frightening emblems of the radical impulse gone prosperous, and furious at the idea of rebellion going on without them, and clutching a young writer yearly to their collective breast as a prophylactic. A young male playwright who turns up and insults them (they interview candidates as well as reading their plays, which seems a daft idea) is obviously one of their sort; a shy, resentful female playwright is obviously not. Morally, if in no other way, she has the last laugh.

I got a great deal of pleasure merely out of trying to identify the characters. Three I was certain of; and even if I am wrong, it's too late now, I've had my fun. Mr Rudman's prowess at keeping a stageful of characters verbally active and alive should not be underestimated, even if at times he coasts on bitching. He did not direct the play; Mike Ockrent did, with a wonderful cast.

He has Ian McKellen, fur-coated and duffel-bagged as an international director of carefully cultivated feyness; Bernard Hill nearly stealing the show as an acidic exhibitionist; Philip Voss as a cheery old academic, patient except when he loses his temper; Glyn Owen as a professional northern minimalist ('I challenge you to read my play as quickly as I wrote it'), who jerks laughter every time he brusquely breaks a sullen silence; Maxine Audley as Harry's gracious female relict and spiritual executrix; Susan Engel up from the country; Michael J. Jackson about whom I can think of nothing to say, which is not his fault; and Barbara Flynn who takes the whole thing down in shorthand. Towards its close the play says something about success. And it is no more incestuous than Mr Barker's play. It merely comes from a different family.

Romantic Comedy (Apollo) by Bernard Slade also looks inward. Fearing himself to be old fashioned, Mr Slade shows us two playwrights who have a similar problem and probably write the same sort of play. Note the tiny defiance of the title. The collaborators are Pauline Collins, odd accent but fine presence, as an innocent, and Tom Conti, whose croaky presence as a sophisticate makes the thing look more than ever like They're Playing Our Song, retitled They're Doing Our Play. I cannot lie: I wanted to see if they would end up together, and I laughed sometimes. But the humour, like everything else, is thinly derivative.

Last week, reviewing The Provoked Wife, I invented an actress called Christine Braddell. She is Valerie Braddell: I portmanteaued her with Christine Bishop, who is also in it. Both, in excuse, are charmers.
