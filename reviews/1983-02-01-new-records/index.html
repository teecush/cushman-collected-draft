Feb 1, 1983

New Records

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

New Records

Though I enjoyed Andy Capp in the theatre I had doubts as to how it would sound on record. But Key Records' cast album (KEY 4) is excellent. It shows off Alan Price to advantage, of course, and his opener "On My Street" comes off all the better as a prologue for our not being bothered by the show's rather desperate attempts to integrate him later. It's unfortunate that his incursion into the main action, duetting with Tom Courtenay on "The Trouble with People", is one of the weaker numbers. Its recurring punch-line 'you really bore me' is meant to carry more dramatic weight than it can bear, and it's also a tough one to rhyme.

Actually there's a fair amount of sloppy rhyming in these lyrics, but the musical forms are usually loose enough for them to be bearable. Mr Price wrote the music, Trevor Peacock the book, and they collaborated on the lyrics, or more likely alternated. At any rate it's Price I hear in the musing pop numbers, Peacock in the music-hall ones. Andy's credo 'I Ought to be Ashamed of Myself' is shades of Formby, and mostly delightful. I don't think I've ever liked Tom Courtenay as much. (Dees anyone remember Courtenay singing Mr Peacock's masterpiece Mrs Brown You've Got a Lovely Daughter' before Herman's Hermits got to it and ruined it?) The number for the women (Val McLane as Flo, Nicky Croydon as Raquel planning her marriage to Elvis) get the exact note of cartooned domestic bite.

The weakest numbers, as on stage, are the sentimental When You've Lived in Love with Someone' and the cluttered, supposedly celebratory 'Mr Scrimmett'. The best, as on stage, is 'Good Old Legs' sung by Andy and Chalkie (John Bardon) as they reel home from the pub. Its sodden euphoria, reflecting its performers' uncertain gait, is something new in musicals. There are two records of the RSC. First Poppy (WEA 250000-1), Peter Nichols's pantomime, Terry Hands's musical. (I only know what I'm told.) I can't see that much separates the two forms today; a Palladium panto and Annie have much the same production values, and either is available for imitation or subversion. If a pantomime storyline required an opium dream or an Anglo-Chinese battle I'm sure the result would look much the same as at the Barbican.

And it isn't as if all pantomimes were alike; Aladdin is not the same bag of goods as Dick Whittington. The claim that the revivalist number The Blessed Trinity, not in Nichols's original script, weakens the anti-imperialist message, can hardly be taken seriously. It positively blares it out. The real objection to this number is that it comes off as the RS's pale answer to the National's 'Sit Down You're Rockin' the Boat'. That the same man, David Toguri, should have choreographed both of them is almost incredible. The decisive objection to the show is that the pantomime convention is not a good way of telling the story. I don't know much about the Chinese opium wars but I do know they weren't fought by characters called Dick Whittington and Obadiah Upward and I got fatigued by the pretence that they were.

Maybe pantomime, in its Victorian heyday, summed up complacent British jingoism but it doesn't any more; as Hands's showbiz spectacle unwittingly proves. The show's attempts to drag the story back to its panto base becomes increasingly desperate. Robert Cushman On record, of course, it becomes a musical anyway, with a competent pastiche score by Monty Norman; though the title song gets as tedious as the Beatle Indian epics it is supposed to be parodying. Nichols's lyrics, though, fulfil the promise of Privates on Parade. The radio critic who called them 'the wittiest since Cole Porter' was going a bit far, but as he then added 'or John Lennon' he obviously didn't know what he was talking about.

In fact they show a feel for rhythm as well as rhyme that is extremely rare among lyricising playwrights, though they frequently try to dispense more historical information than they or the audience can cope with. The highlight is undoubtedly the chanson duet 'Nostalgie de la Boue'. The cast, notably Stephen Moore and Julia Hills, come over very well. As a record it is, surprisingly, a better buy than the National's Guys and Dolls. I, who taped all nine hours of the television version, can hardly object to souvenirs of Nicholas Nickleby. But the album (That's Entertainment TER 1029) is fairly pointless, It gives the main themes from Stephen Oliver's score, of which the first - billed here as 'Overture London' though I think of it as Main Title Theme - revives happy memories.

Others I don't remember at all, and something called "The Farewell Waltz' is interminable, The cast are heard in choruses whose words are a bit more distinguishable than they were in the theatre. Mr Oliver's words for the 'Patriotic Song' that concluded the Romeo and Juliet travesty are excellent pastiche, as his snippet of Rossiniesque opera, with John Weedvine doing what sounds like the heavy father. A few scenes, or bits thereof, might have made the album indispensable. But then I don't understand why people buy LPs of film background music Courtney Kenny is an Irish pianist and opera executive who also performs programmes of arcane popular songs. His album Let Me See You Smile (Erase EO697), recorded before a live but muted audience, presents 14 of these.

He is best with British material, notably a pair of charmers by his great-uncle Percy French, and a gem called "Rheingold" (from The Girls from Gottenburg, an Edwardian show of which I had never heard) by Lionel Monckton and Adrian Ross which sends up Wagner - lyrically rather than musically - very nicely. There is also an Auden-Britten collaboration "Tell Me the Truth About Love' which ranks as a curiosity but sounds like an etiolated imitation of Cole Porter. Sondheim's 'Losing My Mind' is not his meat but he is the only singer I have heard make 'Home Sweet Heaven' (a mechanical list song from the musical version of Coward's Blithe Spirit) sound tolerable and even witty - and that includes Coward himself who didn't write it.

Two real rarities are "The Ragtime Pipes of Pan', very early Porter and very bouncy, and the Gershwins' party song 'Mischa, Jascha, Toscha, Sascha' which is about Russian composers and has very odd lyrics. Pace the sleeve this has been recorded before, on an American collectors' label, by a singer whom that sleeve declares to be Bobby Short but who is actually Arthur Schwartz, another of Mr Kenny's heroes. He does both verses and all three choruses of 'I Guess I'll Have to Change My Plan, Mr Schwartz's first (1929) hit and just about my favourite song. Mr Kenny has taste.
