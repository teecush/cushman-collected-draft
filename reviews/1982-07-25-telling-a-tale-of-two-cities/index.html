Jul 25, 1982

Telling A Tale Of Two Cities

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Telling a tale of two cities

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Danton's Death' and a new musical.

THERE are very few plays that capture the movement of history. Shakespeare's Coriolanus is one; Büchner's Danton's Death (Olivier) is another.

Büchner is the most notoriously youthful of playwrights; he died in 1837, at the age of 23, with three plays behind him, of which Danton's Death is the first. Its energy and its profligacy with scenes and characters mark it as a young man's play; but it also seems strangely apt as the work of a man two years away from his coffin. This chronicle of what Lady Bracknell called the worst excesses of the French Revolution is as much about death as it is about Danton.

He happened to be in mortal danger when he wrote it: a known subversive with the authorities on his trail. But his was a compressed life, and he had already gone through his agitational phase and come out the other side. Danton's Death is a play desperately critical of revolution, though it cannot be said to be against it. Robespierre's reign of terror, which kills Danton (once the initiator of the September massacres, now of all ironic things a moderate), is a phenomenon: something to be observed.

Büchner was also a medical student who won a doctorate through work on the nervous system of fishes. Organisms, processes, things fascinated him. Danton walks out into the street and scents lechery among the passers-by. "I can't see," he says in Howard Brenton's new adaptation, "why people don't just stand still in the street and laugh in each other's faces." Life brings him to a gaping standstill and, logically enough, he throws it away. Despite the urging of his friends, he doesn't fly or defend himself. He bellows when he comes to trial, but he doesn't expect to get away with it. He just enjoys it. It's his nature.

That is the key to Büchner's Danton. "Men," he says to Robespierre in an earlier translation that has stuck in my mind, "are epicures, coarse or fine. Christ was the finest." Robespierre, the virtuous exterminator, latches on to the same analogy: "He redeemed them with his blood, I redeem them with their own." They are like the tramps in Waiting for Godot, who all their lives compared themselves to Christ. This is the play in which, 100 years ahead of time, historical drama meets the theatre of the absurd.

The sense of teeming, vein-bursting life that the characters express is also present in the action itself, especially in the crowd-scenes, which derive bookishly from Shakespeare (Büchner gives us a clowning lynch-mob out of Julius Caesar), but have a fragmentary identity of their own. Meanwhile we get cameos of the politicians intriguing, orating, surviving.

Danton's Death is many kinds of play and Peter Gill, in his best production yet at the National, makes a fair bid to give us all of them. On a set by Alison Chitty of constantly reforming wooden slabs he transports us from brothel to courtroom to guillotine. Every character is allowed his say, and takes it lucidly; while no director is better than Mr Gill at the instant collection or dispersal of crowds. He gets fine central performances from Brian Cox's Danton, a bull who thinks, and John Normington's Robespierre, a machine who feels.

Below this line there is cause for complaint. Saint-Just and Camille Desmoulins are colourless young men, and Paul Moriarty as Lacroix imports vigour at the cost of treating every scene like a public meeting. Real life stirs in the reminiscences of Frances Viner as Danton's whore, whose philosophy of pleasure parallels his own, and in the suicide (another Caesarean section) of Elizabeth Bell as his wife. Their love is one of the mainsprings of the play. "I in love you," says Danton at the start, "like the grave."

From Paris to Chicago, the Windy City of the new musical at the Victoria Palace. This is based on The Front Page, greatest of American comedies, and is patently the work of men who love their material. For a British show it looks and sounds remarkably authentic. Dick Vosburgh, adapting, has got the flavour of Hecht and MacArthur into his own dialogue ("Duncah," dictates a telephoning reporter, "as in coffee and doughnuts") and I liked what I could hear of his lyrics, especially one called Perfect Casting.

This is sung at scheming editor Walter Burns and ace scribe Hildy Johnson by Hildy's fiancee (here redrawn as a scion of Hollywood nobility) and is about how they two are company and she's a crowd. ("Svengali and Trilby you'll still be, I know.") A number entitled Long Night Again Tonight draws together all the characters who have been scuttling over Carl Toms's magnificent rabbit-warren of a set. (It shifts elegantly from side to side, revealing reporters' room, prison cell, sheriff's office and assorted staircases, plus heavenly glimpses of Chicago's elevated railway.)

Tony Macaulay writes good ensemble-music, though his ballads have the anguished anonymity of current pop. There is one which might as well be billed as Diane Langton's Big Number. She sings it as Mollie Malloy, tart with heart, to Earl Williams, the convicted murderer who is her only friend (and vice versa).

This song is a puzzler. It sounds like love-interest; but, as per the play, none develops. So it merely gets in the way. And that is the show's problem. The Front Page cannot really be enhanced. It could perhaps be changed (as Pygmalion, with fewer alterations in dialogue, was radically changed--romanticised--to become My Fair Lady), but the authors of Windy City love it too much for that. We get the same wonderful blend of farce, melodrama, verismo and social comment, but diluted and slowed down. Pace is the one element that Peter Wood's generally admirable production does not supply.

Dennis Waterman's Hildy lacks daemonism and sings anachronistically, though he looks well. Anton Rodgers's Burns has a Groucho-air (in moustache, delivery, hairline, even walk) that suits Mr Vosburgh's writing (his last show was A Night in the Ukraine) down to the floorboards. Victor Spinetti is funny as the dainty poetaster Bensinger, here supplied with a rollicking pastiche of Hiawatha; and Robert Longden is inspired as Earl, being really and amiably mad. That at least is a creative addition. A larger one, the rewriting of Hildy as a woman, made the movie version His Girl Friday into one of the best films ever. That added romance without sacrificing speed. I wonder.

In Regent's Park, under the stars and the Jumbos, there is a Shaw double-bill, both halves chippings from Shakespeare. The Admirable Bashville is a very jolly blank-verse spoof about a boxer and a lady that might benefit from a firmer sense of parody in the performances. Oscar James, looking like a cannibal, has the manner best. In The Dark Lady of the Sonnets Shakespeare (as Shaw always spelled him) breaks into Queen Elizabeth's court and pleads for increased arts subsidies, a course I recommend to modern intruders. (Start with the Theatre Museum.) He also includes All's Well That Ends Well among his favourite plays. The Barbican production almost proves him right. I shall return to it--on the page, that is--next week.
