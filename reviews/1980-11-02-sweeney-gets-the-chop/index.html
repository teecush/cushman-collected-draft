Nov 2, 1980

Sweeney Gets The Chop

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Sweeney gets the chop

ROBERT CUSHMAN looks at the implications of a musical's demise.

YOU had better go to see Sweeney Todd. I hate writing that kind of sentence. I think I should be able to convey enthusiasm for a show without issuing instructions to readers. But this isn't a review, and the show has only two more weeks to run.

Quite possibly you will have seen Sweeney Todd already. It opened at Drury Lane in July, and though a four-month run for a major musical represents a commercial failure of ominous proportions (ominous because it will discourage further adventure), it still means more performances than are enjoyed by the average production at the National or the RSC. Maybe the number of the discerning has already been exhausted. But the feeling remains that there is an audience for this show that has not been reached.

I visited it a couple of Saturdays ago and there was a football crowd in. They listened raptly and cheered at the end. Whatever its subtleties, this is primary-coloured theatre with a strong story and a compelling score -- with these two qualities more than usually interdependent. The performance, with Denis Quilley as the demon barber and Sheila Hancock as Mrs Lovett slicing her pies and her syllables with manic precision, was more cohesive than ever.

According to Miss Hancock, houses, if less than full, have been young and enthusiastic. There may be some wishful thinking in the customary equation of youth and enlightenment, but the fans have not been the customary patrons for "Musicals at the Lane." Most London theatres lost their identity long since, but now Drury Lane seems to retain one, composed of memories of Ivor Novello, Rodgers and Hammerstein, and My Fair Lady. Hence the indignant, nostalgic letters to the London Evening Standard about this bloodthirsty tuneless show.

Tuneless? Let us grant that Stephen Sondheim does not write autonomous melodies in the fashion of Kern or Rodgers and that it might be a nicer world if he did. But where exactly is the competition? Evita? Annie? They're Playing Our Song? The current hits can hardly muster a distinguished tune between them. But they are forgiven -- by critics as well as audiences -- because at least they let you know that they would like to sound like the good old stuff. It is the shows with individual aspirations (On the Twentieth Century? is another example this year) that get shot down in flames.

Instant memorability is no criterion. (We can all, I imagine, remember tunes that we would rather forget.) There is a famous Broadway story about a critic in the Thirties who complained, on his way out of a new Jerome Kern show, that the songs were forgettable, especially the one about smoke getting in your eyes. Jule Styne once told me that it took him several hearings to grasp a Cole Porter song. Since the pop music business was then geared to the theatre, the necessary repetitions were available; the songs became hits and, eventually, standards. That connection has now been severed: pop means rock or muzak, and neither is dramatic.

Actually I think that if the music business could rid itself of the superstition that the only good show song is a love song, somebody might have had a hit with "The Ballad" of Sweeney Todd, whose function and impact in the show are analogous to those of "Mack the Knife," in The Threepenny Opera. Since it closes the show and has an insistent rhythm, audiences probably come out thinking they know it; since it also has some tricky modulations, they're probably wrong.

But what is important in the theatre is that the music should excite you when you hear it and that it should enhance the situation. Sondheim's music does this in spades and, besides, provides the experience, rare in any kind of theatre, of a writer developing while retaining an individual voice. And if you hear it a second time, either in the theatre or on record, you will not forget it.

Before the show even opened it was put down by John Mortimer, who complained in the Sunday Times that "show music" wasn't "either real classical or real pop. Why music should have to fall into one of those two categories (jazz, of which he must have heard, doesn't either) Mr Mortimer never explained. Compared with Porgy and Bess, the obvious test case, Sweeney Todd is musically informal. It isn't opera, and it's unfortunate that the amount of music it contains caused critics and publicists to bandy the word about and to prophesy a future for the show in the operatic repertoire. (If I were feeling really pugnacious, I would mutter something about the frivolity of a form that uses words while taking great pains to render them indistinguishable.)

Sweeney Todd is a dramatic theatre piece and -- since the subsidised theatre has yet to take musicals at all seriously -- a commercial theatre piece. It threw everyone into confusion. The popular reviewers, instead of saying whether they had enjoyed the show, speculated on whether it would be a hit. (That -- trying to make up the public's mind instead of your own -- is the real critical treason.)

I've heard it said, rightly I think, that it might have done better had it come to us from the Roundhouse or the fringe rather than from Broadway, dripping with Tonies. Evita (same producers and director) was able to survive, even thrive on, publicity overkill through the appeal of native authors and a pre-sold score; it's still the hottest ticket in London. Sweeney Todd fell foul of all our double standards -- at one end the Daily Mail daring its readers to like the show; at the other Time Out, whose initial review was favourable but who obviously felt it would not do to recommend a Broadway-West End product week after week. Pal Joey, being a transfer from the Half Moon, gets the nod.

Now there's a show with the right credentials. It shocked the New York critics in the Forties and the London critics in the Fifties. Now its cynicism -- which to its credit is unostentatious and ingrained -- feels like home. Critics who were only vaguely aware of the existence of Rodgers and Hart are understandably bowled over at discovering them.

Pal Joey is so enjoyable that it seems unkind to point out that it represents the team at their second best (Hart, the supposed sophisticate, is actually less at home in the milieu than Rodgers, the real toughie) and that the director, who at one point permits the words from one song to be sung to the tune from another, must have a tin ear. It's interesting that Pal Joey received a far better press than the revival of Oklahoma!, which really was, and remains, a landmark musical, even if its fresh air is currently less to our taste.

When critics don't expect to take a show seriously they imitate the skipper of the good ship Hesperus, and bring their little daughters along to keep them company; so it was on the first night at the Palace. But the show didn't need our good opinions (though it had mine). These revivals -- Oklahoma! and My Fair Lady and The King and I -- are invincibly and deservedly popular. They answer a real need; they dispense a genuine glow. In later shows -- from Hello Dolly! to They're Playing Our Song -- that glow has become synthetic: hence the importance of Sweeney Todd in offering a new satisfaction, sending you out not warm but tingling.
