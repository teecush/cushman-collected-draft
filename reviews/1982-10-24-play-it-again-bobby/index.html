Oct 24, 1982

Play It Again, Bobby...

Robert Cushman · The Observer

PLAY IT AGAIN, BOBBY...

ROBERT CUSHMAN calls for a revival of cabaret

AT THE moment London seems to have a jumping cabaret scene. I'm thinking especially about singers. They're raising their voices again in restaurants: at Langan's Brasserie, for example, where the emphasis has been on the more cute or flippant songs of the Thirties. Even the old tradition of the thé dansant has been revived; a lady will warble to you, with Palm Court backing, over scones and cream cakes in the Waldorf Hotel.

The leading jazz places, the Pizza Express in Dean Street and the still-new Canteen in Covent Garden, sometimes put on singers, even devoting special evenings to the work of particular lyricists or composers. And of course, for years now, London's premier jazz joint, Ronnie Scott's, has presented star vocalists from Ella Fitzgerald down.

All the same, for singers and those who want to hear them, it can still be an uphill struggle. After all, people go to jazz clubs primarily to hear instrumentalists, and to restaurants primarily to eat. In a huge and popular place like Langan's, the combined clamour of knives, forks and conversation could drown any soloist. For singers of Porter, Coward, Sondheim -- singers who want you to hear the words -- these are not the right places. What we need is what they have in New York: supper-clubs.

Every so often somebody tries to start one. Country Cousin's in farthest Chelsea made an attempt, but it failed to pay. Quaglino's, which had the tradition from before the war, also went dark after a period of grovelling to the coach-parties like a small-scale Talk of the Town (which has gone, too). The Playboy Club had a few tries. Mabel Mercer, the doyenne of city singers (English-born Paris-bred, New York-based), the lady who taught them all, including Frank Sinatra, played there in triumph, and was followed by Bricktop, the legendary Paris-American hostess who taught even her.

The singers were accommodated in a ground-floor room with a view of Hyde Park; too elongated for proper audience contact but still pleasant. But the Playboy then showed just how we go wrong by putting Bobby Short, the reigning male singer-pianist, into a deafening subterranean disco, to the mystification of its regular dancing public, and the frustration of his own fans.

Thankfully, amends have been made these past two weeks with a highly successful season of Mr Short, in the far more sympathetic ambiance of the Pizza on the Park, which has an elegant piano room in its basement. Short's stamping ground back in New York is the Carlyle Hotel -- and ideally hotels are the places, especially if they can steel themselves a) to provide an intimate room, b) to keep the catering simple and c) to admit listeners for the price of a drink.

This formula is currently being proved at the Algonquin Hotel in New York, a place much frequented by actors and Englishmen, where they have installed Steve Ross, a young man with a clear, high baritone, a whimsical face, and a standing repertoire of 200 songs, many of them little-known and often hilarious show numbers. (This kind of singer inhabits a rarefied but rewarding limbo between jazz and the theatre.)

Ross recently made his London debut, to much applause, at the Carlton Tower Hotel: a ritzy one-night stand. They are thinking of asking him back. If they don't, some other establishment should, arranging for others to follow him. The thing will never work on a one-off basis.

There are American artists we should see, and British ones we should encourage. The material is too vast, and too rich, to be neglected. And it has nothing to do with the present vogue for nostalgia. Nostalgia is about liking a song because it's old. In cabaret, you like it because it's good.
