Jul 17, 1983

Sin Of Fawnication

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Sin of fawnication

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

DIRECTORS may talk about approaching classic plays as if they had just received them in the mail, but audiences, who are more important, know different and -- of course -- better. We approach old and new plays in totally distinct frames of mind.

Most attempts to put a classic into modern dress, or otherwise make it relevant, are jokes -- conscious or otherwise -- at the expense of the play. I am not against this; I am all in favour of jokes. But a joke has to be shared, and those of us who have never seen the play before are in no position. The desire of any audience, meeting an old play for the first time, is to get a good look at it. Once we know the territory, we can all enjoy defacing it.

This is of course the preamble to a consideration of two revivals. The good news first: at the Cottesloe John Marston's The Fawn is given, probably for the first time since its premiere in 1606. Marston's critical stock, low for centuries, has risen dramatically; damned by the Victorians as a malodorous cynic, he has been accepted by moderns as a satirist, a sexual obsessive, in fact one of us.

But The Fawn was Marston's last comedy, and it is remarkably good-tempered. It has a lovely artificial structure. The elderly Duke of Ferrara seeks marriage with the daughter of the Duke of Urbino, and sends his comely but unromantic young son as ambassador. He himself follows disguised, and is soon devoting himself to forwarding his son's match instead of his own. He also observes the sinister follies of Urbino's courtiers, and encourages them. This makes him very popular. They call him Faunus, because he fawns.

This is a great English comedy, in the line of Love's Labour's Lost. The court fool proclaims 'a figure called in art Ironia,' and irony is the whole technique of the play, almost its subject. Marston grasped that there is nothing as funny as self-satisfaction, especially when coupled -- as it logically must be -- with self-deception. Faunus watches the characters blowing bubbles, and he hardly has to prick them; they explode of themselves. Most of their offences are against women; in the end, they are all found guilty by a Court of Cupid, and consigned to a Ship of Fools. Urbino's Duke himself proves the biggest fool of all, complacency steering his daughter into marriage with the wrong man; an old device but a perennially good one.

Giles Block's production gets the play exactly right. We watch it, as it must have been written, with a constant smile that erupts into frequent chuckles and occasional belly-laughs. Poppy Mitchell contributes a fine and soothing courtyard: a paved floor with matching wall, a green-leaved tree begirt with cushions, a blue sky alight with stars and fireworks. (In a just world The Trojan War Will Not Take Place would have been done in this theatre, on this kind of set and with this kind of wit.) The ensemble work is the best at the National in moons; the actors seem to enjoy the play, and they make the dialogue dance.

Bernard Lloyd, playing the Fawn with acerbic joviality, sets the tone. He is seconded by Peter Needham's bitter fool, and countered by Basil Henson's ducal one: a delightful study in sententiousness. His second is an ostentatiously silent flatterer who in Roger Gartland's performance looks a bit drunk as well.

The most spectacular of the gulls is James Hayes as a pathologically jealous nobleman; he has a great scene hanging from the wall by his fingers, agog to observe his wife's infidelities. Karl Johnson, who woos all available women, and Edward de Souza, who claims to bed them, make a good duo, one piping, the other booming. Jessica Turner heads a posse of hurt and sensible ladies; as the princess Dulcimel, Miranda Foster is the most delicious thing to arrive on a London stage this year.

*

Now then. John Gay's Polly (Cambridge Theatre Company on tour, seen by me at Oxford Playhouse) is a sequel to The Beggar's Opera. It has more plot than its predecessor which, in view of Gay's talents and limitations, is no good thing. The epigrams remain ('tis only for poor people to be brave and desperate, who cannot afford to live') but they have turned mechanical. Polly Peachum is now a sentimentally virtuous heroine, still in pursuit of her Macheath who has turned pirate, following on transportation to the Caribbean.

The Caribbean. That gives the fatal cue for updating. The songs are buried beneath a calypso-and-reggae beat; the story is turned into an anti-colonial tract. Where The Fawn lets us observe a timeless pattern of human conduct, Polly belabours us with liberal sentiments we all share anyway.

I don't know if Polly is a viable piece, but she is stillborn here. The prevailing note of the performances is aggressive slovenliness; Martin Connor, in drag as a handbag-swinging Mrs Ducat, is the only actor to combine attack and precision. Gay's funny prologue ('Musick might tame and civilise wild beasts, but 'tis evident it never yet could tame and civilise musicians') is replaced by a representation of his funeral, supervised by a bored and venal priest. Doubtless there was corruption among the eighteenth century clergy, but I don't see what call we have to be so smug about it. David Pownall did the rewrites.

*

Joseph George Caruso, an American, did the rewrites for Pinero's The Magistrate which he has re-titled Little Lies (Wyndham's). He has cut some characters, changed a male hotelier to a French bawd (substituted, that is, a cliche for a cipher) and added a thin thread of judicial corruption. You can see why he bothered -- it's money for old rope -- but the management's motives are more obscure: what they save in actors' salaries they must cough up in royalties. Every so often I heard a racy line I thought might be new, but it always turned out to be echt Pinero.

Mr Caruso has weakened Pinero's ending by way of giving greater prominence to the magistrate's wife, she who lops five years off her child's age in order to conceal her own. This is ill-advised since Connie Booth in the role is as out of place as nuts in the drawing-room.

John Mills, as the beak lured into dissipation by his strangely pubescent stepson, is a sweet and agile old party, without much dignity to lose. Tony Tanner's production seems to think the late Victorian period funny in itself, and the solemn love-play of Malcolm Sinclair and Angela Scoular is some vindication. The unsinkable original provides some fun.

To come back to timeliness: if Wednesday's vote had gone differently there would, I guarantee, have been a queue for the rights of Brendan Behan's anti-hanging classic The Quare Fellow. Now is the time for somebody (Bill Bryden at the Cottesloe would be ideal) to demonstrate his integrity and do it anyway.
