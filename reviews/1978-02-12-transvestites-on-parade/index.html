Feb 12, 1978

Transvestites On Parade

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Transvestites on parade

Our author offers in these motley scenes / A slight remonstrance to the drama's queens. (Prologue to Sheridan's The Critic?)

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

FAR be it from me to remonstrate, but the past week's motley scenes have focused on two homosexual heroes, exhibitionist and introvert by turns, both happiest in women's clothing.

The Elocution of Benjamin Franklin (Mayfair) is a problem, triumphantly solved. The management may have that as a quote, but I'm actually talking about the plot: Benjamin's elocution is a problem since he's a 12-year-old with a terrible stammer; and the solution is proved triumphant by the fact that at 20 he's a popular actor.

For this transformation a price must be paid, and the man who pays it is Benjamin's speech teacher, a pudgy middle-aged transvestite ex-actor named Robert O'Brien. Accustomed to solitude and to a life of what he calls "adequacy" (meaning bare emotional survival), he wakes up one morning and, horror of horrors, he's in love.

Set in Sydney, the play introduces us to a young Australian playwright, Steve J. Spears, and an established Australian actor, Gordon Chater: a fitting combination for a piece concerned with the impact of youth on middle age. Since Robert is the only character we see, the shares of author and performer are more than usually difficult to distinguish. (An obviously competent director, Richard Wherrett, is also in there somewhere.) Between them, anyway, they have fashioned a masterful first act, a rueful celebration of getting through the day, peculiarly appropriate for a man who has conversations but no relationships (the telephone operator is almost his closest friend) and it is sustained through a variety of moods: edgily confidential with another amateur of drag, a stockbroker named Bruce whose wife helps with his costumes, hilariously professional in his dealings with his infuriating pupils, and the best sequence of the evening, more desperately conscientious as he tries, for both their sakes, to warn off Benjamin, who is jail-bait.

The law comes for him all the same, and Act Two finds him, eight years on, still serving his time in a mental hospital. We see an ageing man on a bed; we hear, via the radio, of the transvestite terror of Double Bay, a Cause or a Menace depending on your prejudices. The contrast is a good one, but the play contracts here; it needs to be exploratory and instead is melodramatic. But the actor survives; Mr Chater is spell-binding throughout, a clown forced into tragedy and fighting it.

*

I suppose that, as an actor, Robert's ideal role might have been Captain Terri Dennis in Privates on Parade (Piccadilly); indeed, since Terri himself is a pro, he might be his own ideal role. He is the drag-queen star turn of Peter Nichols's dramatised concert-party, detailed to entertain the troops in Malaya, 1948. Nobody, though, is likely to improve on Denis Quilley, who parodies just about everything, Terri himself, his Vera Lynn-Carmen Miranda-Noel Coward routines (parodies to begin with), and even the idea of Mr Quilley himself playing the part, and still ends up believable.

Jungle warfare apart, Terri is in a softer spot than Robert O'Brien and wears his mask more becomingly, but it still slips a bit. There is the same slight dislocation of role in Nigel Hawthorne's straight-backed blundering major, whose well-meaning incomprehension is an amazing example of a joke that stays funny however hard it's pressed. Joe Melia, Ian Gelder, Tim Wylton, Simon Jones, Emma Williams make as strong a back-up team as could be found.

These are all the same actors as launched the play at the Aldwych last year. There are changes, however. Formerly the first act ended with the mock-death of the villain of the piece; now it's a real death, which effectively removes all traces of plot from Act Two. Since we all thought originally that the story merely got in the way of the fun, this seems sensible enough; certainly there are no longueurs this time. Moment to moment Michael Blakemore's production is hugely enjoyable, but the play seems to want to mean more than it does.

At the end we get a long-range squint at the birth of the Welfare State, and it seems an age of innocence. Terri, in pantomime couplets, refuses to look into the future; so we look instead and find the sequel bleak. The (Under) the title of The National Health, Mr Nichols has already written it. But if this is the underlying motive of the play its framework is too unwieldy for mere autobiography. Everybody loves the songs, but to hear Mr Nichols at his best listen to the virgin soldier's letters home, masterpieces of gawky pretentiousness, affectionately caught.
