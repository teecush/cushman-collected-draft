Jun 4, 1987

Casting A Critical Eye Over A Miller's Tale Of The West End

Robert Cushman · The Independent

Jonathan Miller, announcing a brave new season of plays for his first year at the Old Vic, took the opportunity to inveigh against the rest of the British theatre. "Bureaucratised" was his word for the subsidised wing. The West End, where four plays closed only last week, was a wasteland, governed by the untutored whims of "Attila the tourist". Actually the situation is even bleaker than he describes. I am not at all sure, though, that any individual, even Attila, is to blame.

Nobody could accuse Dr Miller of pandering to the Philistines. Planners looking for untried classics to fill up their seasons, or even fans wondering what theatrical cadavers they would most like to see resurrected, are apt to throw up their hands and fall back on the same old Chekhovs, Ibsens, and Shaws. Dr Miller has come up with seven titles of which only one could possibly be called familiar. And that one, being The Tempest, hardly counts. Shakespeare is allowed to break everybody's rules.

For the rest, we have a musical, Candide, which I have seen in New York and Edinburgh, but never in London; and plays by Racine, Ostrovsky, Lenz, Chapman and N. F. Simpson, that I have never seen at all. And I've been around.

It's a cheering catalogue, made more so by Dr Miller's assertion that the season only needs to play to a modest sounding 60 per cent of capacity to break even. "There is," he said, "no way we can make ourselves millionaires out of this theatre." But then his backer, Ed Mirvish, already is a millionaire. He rescued the Royal Alexandra, which is Toronto's most beautiful theatre, and is doing the same for the Vic, which is ours. A few years ago he saved its body. Now he's working on its soul. Dr Miller's tribute to his "pragmatic philanthropy" is, if anything, an understatement.

Some of Miller's pronouncements are more questionable. To declare in advance that none of your productions will transfer to the West End sounds to me like giving hostages to misfortune. True, a West End management is not that likely to bid for Bussy d'Ambois - not, at least, without Michael Caine in the leading role - but it is possible to imagine the Shaftesbury Avenue boys knocking on the door at Waterloo Road and asking if Candide can come out to play. If they do, and if they ask very nicely, will Dr Miller really say no? And if so, why?

One reason may be that he needs something to fight against. He has to construct a demonology. I imagine that he sees bureaucracy and commercialism as aspects of the same phenomenon and would point to the National Theatre and RSC's string of West End transfers as proof. Some years ago Lindsay Anderson started a classical repertory company actually in the West End and used the occasion to proclaim the virtues of the commercial theatre (against which he had been inveighing all his working life) and to deplore the feather-bedding of the subsidised theatre (which he had always supported). Funny how your view of other people's work is governed by the conditions under which you yourself happen to be operating at the time. As it happens, Mr Anderson's company speedily found itself playing a straight run of a Ben Travers's farce, and then expired, thereby confirming everybody's worst fears about the West End. A question-mark hangs over the more recent attempt to buck the system of another distinguished director, John Dexter.

On the other hand... the success of The House of Bernarda Alba, a play of which few West End punters could previously have heard, has been hailed as a sign that a play of quite menacing seriousness can survive in the West End. And so it can, if it has rapturous notices and Glenda Jackson. Miss Jackson can sell almost anything. Television stars, the West End has been discovering, lack the magic, maybe because on their own turf each of them is only half a star. Peter Bowles doesn't sell without Penelope Keith, or George Cole without Dennis Waterman, or Mel Smith without Griff Rhys-Jones. Les Liaisons Dangereuses, the remaining class contender, has no stars but it does have rapturous notices, plus sex. It was also, for more than a year, an acclaimed play in a small theatre that nobody could get into. One of the subsidised theatre's attractions for a commercial producer, greater even than the money it saves him, is the expectation it creates.

That leaves the mega-musicals about which there is nothing to be said except that they run because people enjoy them. This may sometimes be perplexing, but it is not sinister. I cannot see that anybody's pleasure in Les Liaisons can reasonably be damaged by the existence round the corner of Les Miserables.

It all means, though, that there are few categories of shows with any chance of West End success. Dr Miller blames it on the tourists ("the Huns of modern Europe"), but this is unfair. Tourists can only patronise the shows that producers offer them and most producers would be far happier to play to a more reliable indigenous audience. The problem is that it does not exist, or at least not in sufficient numbers.

Does it matter? Probably not, except to those of us hoping in one way or another to make a living out of it. The average theatre-goer does not care much about "the state of the theatre". He only wants there to be a few shows on that theme which he would like to see, and it cannot matter much to him whether they are located in Islington, Chelsea or the Strand.

Or, of course, on the South Bank or in the Barbican. The National Theatre's repertory is, all of a sudden, as varied and as well performed as anyone could expect. The RSC is known to be in desperate financial trouble, but this does not seem to affect the monotonously high standards it achieves on its smaller stages. The big ones are more problematic, apparently resistant to any play that is not either a set text or a vehicle for Jeremy Irons. The directorate has been publicly flagellating itself for its choice of non-Shakespeare plays at the Barbican, but they actually looked perfectly reasonable on paper. A few years ago there would have been a decent audience for them. The awful truth may be that there is now almost nothing that a large audience really wants to see. I do not like to think about this very much. Neither, I imagine, do most people in the theatre. But it must give us all the odd, unguarded sleepless night.

So Jonathan Miller's instinct - to retire to a promontory in the Waterloo Road and fortify it - may be the correct one. He will be putting on minority plays for short runs on, presumably, modest budgets. That may be the best that anyone can do. Unless they want to get rich.
