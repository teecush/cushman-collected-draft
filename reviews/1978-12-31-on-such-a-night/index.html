Dec 31, 1978

On Such A Night

Robert Cushman · The Observer

On such a night

ROBERT CUSHMAN on theatre in 1978 -- a good year for actresses and new plays.

TO BEGIN, as ever, with the National Theatre.

I lifted a sad eye to a noticeboard announcing the evening's attractions. The Woman was on in the Olivier, The Philanderer in the Lyttelton, and American Buffalo in the Cottesloe: two excellent new plays (one large, one small) and a first-class revival. On such a night the National is obviously doing what we ask of it: providing three of the best.

Some bitter fruit might have yielded me dustier answers, but the averages have got unsteadily higher over the year. There are just two cautionary notes. One concerns the output of new plays; the other is the regular appearance in the Cottesloe repertoire of mercilessly boring new plays. To Lost Worlds and The World Turned Upside Down must be added the current Christmas attraction, Herod, a brief and stunningly undramatic nativity piece. The occasional animal noise, and a group called The People Show, plodding at the back and at the end of the hall, somehow add to the depression. Good is being forcibly done to us, and pedagogy cannot save it.

The Woman is a post-Civil War panorama with Diggers digging and Ranters ranting and Levellers not getting level. It was an astonishing production of a difficult play. The language has a rough grandeur and the images are carved large; the theatre around it seemed momentarily to have recovered a public size. I can understand people not liking Edward Bond's play The Woman, though I think they miss something; I am baffled by the negative responses to his staging of it, which is powerful and also great to look at.

The National's plans for next year look exciting; I was always a sucker for Press-releases. As for the opposition, the Stratford season has been underrated. Love's Labour's Lost reaffirmed John Barton's grand mastery of Shakespearean comedy, Michael Bogdanov mounted a sparky Shrew, and Peter Brook's Antony and Cleopatra was at any rate the best production of the play most of us have seen.

The Aldwych's record with new native plays was watery--as usual. In nearly 20 years' occupancy there have been few of note: since Peter Hall departed, taking Harold Pinter with him, hardly any. The energies of the directorate have been focused elsewhere, Terry Hands having in his time directed everything which does not actually stand still and a group of actors were, as always, responding. The Caretaker, here given a tight and succulent production, packed much more bite than the Philanderer which, together with the slightly perchier Double Dealer, restored the approach which frankly is the national approach to middleweight comedy, i.e. two-thirds of the effective repertoire.

The rich performances, with Congreve, along with those of Robert Stephens in Brand and Susan Fleetwood in The Cherry Orchard, reminded us of another of the proper pleasures of posh theatre: that of watching a front-rank actor blow a supporting role up to his own proportions, enriching the texture of a play without damaging its fabric. Trevor Nunn may object, but the incidence of individual laughs and shudders is praiseworthily high.

Heroic acting--and this is serious--has been at a discount: Mr Bryant succeeded as Sir Paul but not as Brand, Albert Finney as Lopakhin but not as Macbeth. But the Olivier has seen one heroic production. The London and Stratford studios, by contrast, have bombarded us, and generally to good effect. In The Bundle the RSC had an even better Bond than the National; we assisted at the development of Pam Gems, Stephen Poliakoff and (turning creatively to adaptation) David Edgar; and new authors called Peter--Flannery, Whelan, Atkin--proved as thick (on the ground, I mean) as directors used to be.

This activity yielded some fine performances (Mike Gwilym as Basho, Peter McEnery as Albie Sachs, excelled themselves) and one, Jane Lapotaire's Piaf, for which the only word is formidable. It works even better if you italicise it. There was nothing quite as exciting in the main houses, though Alan Howard tightened his grip on Coriolanus and Jonathan Pryce came on strong. Ian McKellen, leading theatrical commando raids on unresisting villages, should have disarmed them with his Toby Belch.

As must be clear by now, male acting has been subdued; only Nicol Williamson, reliving the agonies of Inadmissible Evidence at the Court, lit rockets. But actresses were something else: Miss Lapotaire's outburst as Piaf has already been noted, and Vanessa Redgrave in Ibsen provided one of the strongest stage experiences of the year. Diana Rigg in Stoppard's Night and Day, Penelope Wilton in Betrayal, and Susan Fleetwood in The Cherry Orchard were different kinds of command. The year of the feminist? Not quite, but it was certainly the year in which actresses and new plays most often gave the theatre its charge.
