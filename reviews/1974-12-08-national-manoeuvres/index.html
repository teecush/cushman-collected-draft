Dec 8, 1974

National Manoeuvres

Robert Cushman · The Observer

National manoeuvres

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on A. E. Ellis's new play about Dreyfus.

THE original title of the National Theatre's new play about the Dreyfus case was "The Jew of Mulhaus." It now comes before us as Grand Manoeuvres, a phrase used at one point by Esterhazy, the adventurer actually responsible for selling French military secrets to German intelligence, and applied, by ironic extension, to the gyrations of the French high command in their hideously successful efforts to send the wrong man to prison and keep him there. In general, though, irony is not this play's style. It is a slam-bang clamorous affair which might more frankly have been re-titled "The Dreyfus Show."

In this enjoyably phantasmagoric epic, cast and settings vie with each other in number and mobility. The movements of both are witty, intricate and precisely timed; one knows within minutes of the opening that everything and everyone will click into place. Only a full-time theatre critic, perhaps, can appreciate how refreshing that knowledge is; and I must stress that it is never allowed to relax into complacency. The surprises, for three whole hours, keep on coming.

I am inclined to attribute them to the director, Michael Blakemore, rather than to the author, A. E. Ellis, but this may be prejudice since I have known and admired Mr Blakemore's work for years while Mr Ellis's is new to me. I have not read his one novel, and this is his first play. As such it should probably not, in an ideal world, be accorded a full-scale National production unless it were either a masterpiece or the likely precursor of one. It is neither, but most of the new plays offered this year by our three major theatres have been so dismal that this one may be tolerated, even welcomed. An occasion for a virtuoso production is at least an occasion.

On the evidence before me I would judge Mr Ellis to be more of an editor than writer. Many of the play's speeches are evidently verbatim reproductions of what was said and written at the time; other aspects are less obviously derivative. I thought it at first an excellent idea to dramatise the divisive effect of the affair by showing a bourgeois dinner-party turned into a shambles at the mention of Dreyfus's name. I was therefore a little dashed when I opened the programme to find that what I had admired was merely an animation of a cartoon of the period; I had accordingly to divide my admiration between the original artist and the director, designer (John Bury) and choreographer (Sue Lefton) who had revivified him. Very little was left over for the author, whose accompanying dialogue is no more than efficient. An encounter between Esterhazy and two moustachioed intelligence officers disguised as ladies of leisure is wittier, but here, too, the programme discloses a less direct debt to a contemporary draughtsman.

It is an admirably honest thing, this programme, and well worth consulting. It enables Mr Ellis to fill in the details that are wanting from the play, to tell us why the France of the 1890s felt itself to be vulnerable, why it needed a scapegoat and why that scapegoat should have been a Jewish Army officer. It tells us a great deal about Edouard Drumont, an author and journalist who seems almost to have invented French anti-semitism and whose newspaper, Libre Parole, hysterically led the cry against Dreyfus.

The way Mr Ellis goes on about Drumont you expect him to be the villain of the piece. In fact he does not even appear in it, though his influence is to some degree felt; the officers who are the play's chief rogues are first seen ensconced behind copies of the Libre Parole, whence they emerge to embark on grand and petty manoeuvres designed to ruin Dreyfus and to camouflage their own corruption and incompetence. Their spokesmen--written and played with considerable spirit--is a Major Henry, risen from obscurity and rising still; Paul Rogers, charting his progress, runs through about three-and-a-half different accents before a temporary crest in Dreyfusard sentiment brings his career to an expedient and suicidal halt. But sentiment prevails, and children for his orphaned children is soon heard tinkling into the colonel's cap (I told you he'd risen) of the unfortunate deceased. Meanwhile Dreyfus continues to rot on Devil's Island.

I liked that moment. But what with his twitting of the soldiery and the politicians, and the necessity of conveying the events of a dozen years, and the tendency of the characters to address one another by name, rank and title so that we may have some idea who they are, Mr Ellis has left himself with very little time to examine the significance of the case to France, or to the outside world, in general. I think he kept meaning to, and, late on, he at any rate shows us the shocked, derisive reactions to Dreyfus's family of a trio of yokels made up as circus clowns.

It might have helped more if we had been allowed to judge the inroads made by anti-semitism on recognisable people. Here, however, we come up against the play's own grand manoeuvre, which is to deny sympathy, humanity and even a normal speaking-voice to practically everyone except Dreyfus himself. When Alan MacNaughtan first comes on, the contrast of his dapper quietude to the preceding rowdiness is quite breathtaking. Where the others are allowed no nobility, he is allowed no flaw, the play taking the reasonable if limiting view that his sufferings were so great as to make any judgment on his character irrelevant.

At the end of the play Mr MacNaughtan gives us one final, overwhelming shudder. Vindicated and released, Dreyfus is grudgingly awarded the Legion d'Honneur. He turns to the audience and we find he has been decorated with a yellow star. The sheer theatricality is almost irresistible; but I began resisting when the figure of a Nazi guard loomed upstage and have continued ever since. These are the prime horrors of our time and they must not be too easily used. They become cheapened; so do the artists responsible; so do we. Since the play never really examines the French anti-semitism of the 1890s, it can tell us nothing of its relation to the German of the 1930s. As far as it goes, however, the narrative is absorbing and (unless, like some learnedly dismissive critics, you know it all already) instructive.

*

Much of the 1950s seemed gathered at Greenwich to watch John Osborne's The Entertainer. This play has weathered 17 years and will certainly outlast Grand Manoeuvres. But, at least in the author's own reverently uncut production, it contains an inordinate amount of moaning and I found that the celebrated equation of Britain with a decaying music-hall rang no bells; it belongs firmly to its period. The evening is nearly wrecked by the length of the transitions from hall to home, and saved by Max Wall's Archie Rice. He has to rein himself in on Archie's awful early routines; he has after all convulsed audiences with material even more tenuous (though I wonder if Mr Osborne intended Archie's lyrics to be quite as bad as they are). But he excels in the extension of the act that constitutes his family life; it is here that Archie performs best, simultaneously wooing and excluding his audience as Mr Wall has always done: his last great, dismissive monologue, actor, role and play come resoundingly together.

*

In Barrie's What Every Woman Knows (Albery) some harsh unfashionable views on marriage are compromised by a compulsion to appear as pleasant as possible. This weakness, already present in the writing, is greatly intensified in Clifford Williams's production. Peter Egan, who breathes sensitivity, is cast as an unimaginative careerist who does not know when he is insulting his wife. A self-made Scot, he falls for an English aristocrat; credible enough if the lady were not recognisable as a twittering nonentity a mile off. Meanwhile his wife suffers from her lack of charm--which, since Dorothy Tutin plays the part, is plainly (or beautifully) absurd. Though the miscasting is monstrous (and could the right kind of actress ever get the part in the West End?), it is almost justified since Miss Tutin, who has the most eloquent eyes in the world, acts subtly and superbly.

*

In Sheffield, the Crucible Studio is showing an early Athol Fugard, Nongogo, which is certainly very promising and contains a good flash performance by Alton Kumalo.
