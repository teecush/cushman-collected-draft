May 1985

Tom and Viv and Jumpers

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

The printed issue establishes May 1985; the publication day is unknown.

Tom and Viv and Jumpers

Robert Cushman at Tom and Viv and Jumpers / Royal Court/Aldwych

Two plays have recently returned to London in which Bertrand Russell plays a peripheral, unseen role. In each, he is the occasion of wit. In Tom and Viv he is T S Eliot’s early room-mate, ‘the most hated man in London’ who, despite his reputation as a seducer, signally fails to get anywhere with the first Mrs Eliot: a circumstance that fills all concerned with the gloomiest apprehensions for that lady’s future. In Jumpers he turns up in Professor George Moore’s speech on the possible existence and interdependence of God and morality as ‘my friend the late Lord Russell’, a phrase that George admits does not sound quite right, perhaps, as his wife Dotty drily points out, because Russell wasn’t his friend.

In short, we are among intellectuals. Tom and Viv is Michael Hastings’s treatment, unauthorised but far from unsympathetic, of Eliot’s wretched first marriage whose upshot, in Edith Sitwell’s phrase, was that ‘Tom went mad and promptly certified his wife’. Tom here does not go mad; he merely goes English and Anglican. Banker and publisher as well as poet, he wants his life to be tidy, and so there is no place in it for Viv. She dazzled him at first: an apparently free spirit with upper-class connections. In fact her family accept him rather more enthusiastically than they do her. Viv has menstrual problems which embarrass her family, her doctors, and herself; and which, ignorantly treated, drive her further and further into neurosis. Most of all do her illnesses, physical and mental, embarrass her husband. They have, it seems, no sex-life. Eliot hopes that Russell might actually seduce Viv, just to take the strain off him, and he is crushed and desperate when he learns the truth. The information passes between them in a gentlemanly exchange of letters. It is one of Mr Hastings’s best, most economical, strokes to have Viv discover this correspondence and read it to us.

I have no especial biographical interest in Eliot, and no more than a polite literary one, but the treatment of all parties seemed to me acute and sympathetic. Tom’s gradual withdrawal into English religion and society, his abandonment of his wife and his guilt over it, all seem likely enough. His growing rapprochement with Viv’s brother—an amateur Blimp whose stumbling good intentions, economic misadventures in Africa, and decline into shabby gentility make him seem like a figure from Anthony Powell—is especially well delineated. I believed it all. But it is not a very dynamic progress, and it is half the play. It weighs the evening down, and the second act in particular becomes dull.

The play seemed notably wittier when I read it afterwards than it had in performance. Max Stafford-Clark’s production is, after its own fashion, impeccable: elegant, icy and precise. But it is also insistently ascetic, ending scenes abruptly and changing them in full view of the audience. I know this is Royal Court style and has been for years, but I am beginning to suspect it. The assumption seems to be that the audience does not want illusion, and would be angry, even insulted, if it were offered. Or perhaps the idea is that we should take what’s good for us. The denial of illusion can overlap with the denial of pleasure. (This kind of staging bothered me particularly in the recent production of The Pope’s Wedding; it seemed a reflex, nothing to do with the rhythm of the play and actually harmful to it.)

I admired Edward Herrmann’s gradual suppression of his American accent as Tom sank into his chosen milieu. Julie Covington is one of my favourite actresses but her Viv, after all I had heard about it, (I missed the play on its first London outing) disappointed me. Everything is there—the 1920s brittleness, the defensive descents into mock-Cockney, the fraying nerves, the final desperate gallantry—but it adds up to the solution of a set of acting problems rather than to the creation of a person.

Another disturbed marriage is at the centre of Tom Stoppard’s Jumpers. How disturbed seems to vary from production to production. But troubled depends on which production you are watching. The three I have seen have all been directed by Peter Wood but they have varied considerably. The National Theatre original had Michael Hordern and Diana Rigg radiating in opposite directions; one wanted to believe that there was hope for them, because both are such likeable players, but there was little optimistic evidence. In the National revival Hordern and Miss Covington had some newly-scripted moments that hinted an underlying sympathy. Both were oral beings, in a newly amoral universe. The current version is the bleakest yet. Felicity Kendal’s Dotty is vivaciously vulnerable but, as far as her spouse is concerned, unreachable. Paul Eddington’s George radiates pain.

I am unsure how much the darkness is intentional, how much was due to the second-night performance I witnessed (which was very second-nightish) and how much, as some reviewers have claimed, can be blamed on the times. Certainly there was nothing like the effervescence of the original production. That came at a buoyant time, theatrically and even nationally. It was 1972, London was still vestigially swinging, and a rejuvenated National was specialising in luxury-pad plays; Jumpers, the modern-dress Misanthrope and Trevor Griffiths’s The Party were plays of ideas acted out in coolly luxurious settings. Mr Stoppard’s jokes were all wonderfully new, and newly wonderful. Hordern was playing his greatest role, Miss Rigg was beautiful, and the occasion oozed confidence. Even though the odds were stacked against Mr Stoppard’s well-meaning professor, even though one sympathised with him deeply when he stepped on his tortoise in the very act of mourning his slaughtered hare, the wit in his presentation (some of it his own, some of it at his expense) kept us buoyant. It seemed possible that some time, at some future date, George would invent God and win through.

Now we are less confident. England is gloomier, ruled by a government of a different complexion from Mr Stoppard’s Rad-Lib but equally convinced of its own rightness. The political acuteness of Jumpers, as of Travesties, has always seemed to be undervalued; it is not so much that it starts where orthodox political plays leave off as that it goes further back than they would dream of delving. Mr Stoppard asks why people go after power, what it is in themselves that they have to deny, and why the world makes it easy for them. George’s harried defeat is what Mr Eddington now captures; the fun of George’s monologue largely escapes him. He has not Hordern’s quintessential donnishness, nor his eagerness, nor his absurdity. To put it another way, the second act of his performance and hence of the production, is much better than the first, during much of which one sits lamenting lost chances.

Simon Cadell makes a smooth job of the omnicompetent Archie though he seems very young to have amassed so many credentials, even in a relativistic world. Andrew Sachs is an oddly manic choice for the plodding Inspector Bones, and it is very confusing when he turns up in the dream-coda as Archbishop Clegthorpe. ‘Will no-one rid me of this copper’s nark’ is the new link-line provided to carry off this identification; it seems, like most of the re-writes, unwise. The coda was much criticised at the time of the first production (epilogues nearly always are) but actually, it makes, on its own terms, perfect sense: an accurate tally of George’s bewilderments and of his failures. Tricked up as it is in this production, it merely confuses. The performance I liked best was that of Timothy Bateson as Crouch, the metaphysical caretaker (‘it’s just a hobby with me’): a gift role for the gentle mole-like character actor that Mr Bateson superbly is.

Need I add that the play still fizzes and that its ceaseless agility, its determination to leave no hole unstopped with laughter makes it the most delightful—or why not simply say the best—of post-war British plays? (Forget the stuff about its philosophic content flattering the audience’s self-esteem; you could say as much of the acclaimed political plays that seem intended to compensate critics and publics for the activist past they never had.) No playwright since Shaw has written comedy this sparkling, and Mr Stoppard is ahead of much of Shaw in clear-headedness and in honest good nature. You never feel that he is patting you on the head or that he thinks he has all the answers.

And you actually feel for George and Dotty: far more, speaking for myself, than for the central couple of The Real Thing where Mr Stoppard piles on the psychology without getting any nearer the truth. Better the pure fun of On the Razzle or Rough Crossing than a solemn Stoppard building a structure as complicated as that of Jumpers but without its aerating humour.

That The Real Thing should have been hailed as an advance for Mr Stoppard, or that Tom and Viv should have been taken more seriously than Mr Hastings’s hilarious Gloo Joo, is as sad a sign of the times as anything prophesied or adumbrated in Jumpers. We prize our playwrights, but not always for their best work. I could name other victims, laughing on their way to the bank while their audiences sit dutifully moping in the stalls.
