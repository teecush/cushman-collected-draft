Mar 22, 1981

Surrendering To Laughter

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Surrendering to Laughter

Robert Cushman on plays by Noel Coward, Joe Orton and Feydeau.

In the heyday of the West End Noel Coward first nights were, one gathers, like no others. Or rather they were like all the others only more so.

It was a moot point, especially when Coward was appearing in one of his own creations, whether there was more glamour at large on the stage or in the stalls. Even now a Coward revival brings out the black ties and long dresses like nothing else. For one evening, progress -- if that's what you want to call it -- can be held at bay. If the play misfires the effect can be grisly, but if it works you surrender to the dream. And Present Laughter (Vaudeville) is the dream incarnate.

It concerns, you will remember, a highly successful actor called Garry Essendine, king of the London stage immediately before the war. His is a world where a star is sure of a long run, unless he makes a really criminal error in choice of vehicle, and where all crises can be resolved over lunch at the Ivy.

The play is a long and leisurely affair that hardly bothers to introduce a plot until the second of its three acts. The first is devoted to showing us Garry at home, which generally means Garry under siege. Women throw themselves into his bed; a manically earnest young playwright arrives to upbraid and remains to worship.

Telephones ring, appointments are made and tours arranged at a giddy pace. Garry regards himself as a martyr to his friends and business associates, but in fact his life is ruthlessly arranged to suit his own convenience. He is the Man Who Came to Dinner caught at home; and if the play has less vigour than a Kaufman-and-Hart crazy comedy, it compensates with a finer sensibility.

Then the story begins. We are told -- though, reprehensibly, not shown -- that Garry's circle is a small oasis of tolerant, civilised values. Enter, to throw herself disruptively at Garry, Joanna, who is the wife of his producer and the mistress of his backer. Or maybe the other way round; I can never get those two fellows sorted out. It is one merit of Alan Strachan's production that they here appear more like men and less like sticks than in either of the two previous versions I have seen.

His is also the only production in which the play improves as it goes on. Instead of declining from witty discursive autobiography to desultory comedy of incident it ascends into rampaging farce. This goes with the casting -- in a strict sense, the miscasting -- of Donald Sinden as Garry. Coward, though apparently very generous, was an intimidating figure; Mr Sinden is approachable. Coward was glacial and Mr Sinden is teddy-bearish. Coward was feline; with Mr Sinden the play's homosexual dimension disappears.

Garry has a soul full of doubts, but mere events don't really ruffle him. Mr Sinden, as a farceur, has no time to worry about his soul, but events can destroy his day, if not his life. It is a joy to see him sinking under their weight until finally, with superbly-timed effort, he dislodges them; and to hear some of Coward's funniest lines surviving an entirely alien, though equally skilled, delivery.

An uneven supporting cast is topped by Gwen Watford, as Garry's aide constantly on the verge of tears -- of joy, misery or amazement. There are two lovely studies in idolatry from Belinda Lang and Julian Fellowes (whose scarved and corduroyed enthusiasm is beautifully in period) and a brisk valet from Colin Spaull.

*

Third time lucky again. Unlike the original and the Royal Court revival, Kenneth Williams's production of Entertaining Mr Sloane (Lyric, Hammersmith) entertained me, thanks chiefly, to Dave King, who has the easiest stage manner imaginable and whose gear-changes, arising out of such deceptive relaxation, are transfixing.

This performance, balancing respectability and lubricity on a knife-edge and with every crooked epigram taking the hearer by surprise, is classic Orton. This puts it ahead of the play itself, which, though it takes acute glancing blows at attitudes towards death, sex and legality, lacks the wider reference and frantic activity of Loot or What the Butler Saw.

The chintzy oppressiveness of the setting is part of the satire, but to spend an evening looking at so much pink is fatiguing; the more accurate the design (and Saul Radomsky's is excellent), the greater the trial. And Orton pushes his effects very hard; most of the first act is devoted to the same joke: primness equals nymphomania.

So Sloane the leather-boy is seduced by his landlady while she piles on (most heavy-handedly in Barbara Windsor's performance) the protestations of innocence. All the same, this early, minor Orton still has a shape, a style, and an identity; few other plays of the Sixties would prove so coherent in revival.

*

The new Hammersmith director, Peter James, leaves his previous perch, the Sheffield Crucible, with Blow Hot, Blow Cold, the joint work of Feydeau and someone called Hannequin, whose name for it was The Ribadier System. M. Ribadier's system is to hypnotise his wife so he can go out and be unfaithful. And that really is all.

Compared with the accustomed Feydeau, this piece is short on characters, on intrigue, on doors. What it has is social significance: Feydeau fans (myself included) have always proclaimed him a scourge of the bourgeoisie; well, here he is, scourging them. M. Ribadier, discovered, shrinks from the thought of a duel, and he and the man he has cuckolded come instead to a mutually advantageous arrangement concerning the wine trade.

Meanwhile the somnolent Mme Ribadier is unavailingly pursued by a long-time admirer back from Batavia. Two sides of the Sheffield triangle -- Bryan Pringle as hypnotist and Ronnie Stevens as hopeful hypotenuse -- are admirable; the third, Joanna Dunham, is more decorative than adroit, though, as aggrieved party, much of the fun should rest with her.

*

The best two numbers in the revue Up in the Eighties (King's Head) begin the second half: One is a song about actors (a natural opener displaced), the other a sketch about box-office staff. Add, perhaps, Maggie-vieta, which makes good sport of Mrs Thatcher, but rather better of Andrew Lloyd Webber. In fact this show's inbred style is only good for satirising the theatre.

The spoken material is awful, taking indifferent ideas and beating them to death. The cast are bright, especially Amanda Barrie, who has the rare gift of being funny and sexy at the same time.
