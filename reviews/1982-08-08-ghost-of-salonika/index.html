Aug 8, 1982

Ghost Of Salonika

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Ghost of Salonika

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a striking new play and revivals of Chekhov and Coward.

DARE I announce a new genre? Meet the retrospective beach-drama. Earlier this year there was Edward Bond's Summer, in which wounds from the Second World War were reopened on an island. Now, strikingly, there is Louise Page's Salonika (Theatre Upstairs), in which the memories stretch to the war before that. They are embodied in a soldier who, before our astonished eyes, rises from beneath the sand.

Mr Bond has taught us not to be afraid of ghosts, and Miss Page handles her apparition with great tact. He may not be a presence at all, merely an embodiment of the thoughts and emotions of four contemporary Britons visiting Salonika 65 years on. (Miss Page breaks this convention only once, when she ill-advisedly has one character remark to another on the strange people you meet in these parts.) If he is a ghost, the other characters treat him with remarkable calm. Calm, with turbulence beneath, is the play's great virtue.

The four survivors are the dead man's wife, now 84; their daughter, a spinster in her sixties; the wife's septuagenarian suitor; and a young beach-bum, who is stretched out sun-bathing when the audience arrives. He might be dead; and images of death recur throughout the evening. It is a subject much on the characters' minds. Even the young man survives by keeping the roll of honour at the local cemetery, supplementing his income by selling his blood.

Mother and daughter have to think of mortality, and it is the mother who is the sprightlier. Daughter, who has had no fun, fears the final injustice of going first. Sheila Burrell, one of our best actresses, conspires with the author to create a fine picture of bottled-up innocence: a mind that at times snaps shut like a handbag, at others reaches out wanly for help.

The frustration issues in unconscious (and very good) jokes. Of the mother: 'We can't put her in a home, there's not enough wrong with her.' To the suitor: 'Stop calling her kitten. She's 84. You might at least call her cat.' The reversal of roles between the two women is a constant source of dramatic energy. Gwen Nelson gives the elder a thin crust of jollity, and Richard Butler hits a note of surface joviality as the man in her life.

The young men of the present and past are excellently played by Garry Cooper and Christopher Fulford; the entrances of the deceased punctuate the play, an image of what the other characters discuss. The remainder of his fate, the bitter comparisons of then and now, overhang the bickerings, jealousies, frustrations and occasional kindnesses that are the play's substance. It is a dark but thoroughly undepressing piece; the best to arrive in London in months. (Cruel, though, to young actors: one has to lie buried in sand, the other to lie still while his naked body is washed all over.)

Directors have tried hard to persuade us that Chekhov can be a laugh a minute, but they have never really succeeded, and it is something of a relief to find Christopher Fettes's production of Uncle Vanya (Haymarket) reverting to the old-fashioned way. There is comedy, rich but short-lived, in Bill Fraser's waffling Waffles (a snorting-heavy instead of the usual wisp) and in some of Donald Sinden's early tirades as Vanya, but this remains a play to weep over, its ending less compromised by optimism than any other Chekhov wrote. When Sonya says 'we shall rest,' she means 'we shall die.'

There are some very fine passages in Mr Fettes's production. The second-act storm, raging, modulating and spending itself beyond the windows. The reconciliation of the two young women, making a ritual out of drinking from the one glass. A protracted moment when Vanya thinks he really has killed the Professor. The end, with Frances de la Tour taking off unforced into Sonya's last speech of hard consolation. Add some of Harry Andrews's laments over his professorial gout, and a general lucidity that finds particular expression in Sheila Gish.

Her Yelena is rather chill and matronly, but she has a clear attitude to every other character and unsparingly shows the lady's helpless distaste in the face of emotion, her own or anybody else's. Mr Sinden, unfamiliarly round-shouldered, crumples at the end into a very moving heap, but is short of the nervous energy that lends meaning to his downfall. (He may never have lived, as he claims, but there should have been a spark of life in him.)

A really crippling defect is that there is no communion between this Vanya and Ronald Pickup's Astrov, the two cultivated men in the district; in the end the same kind of failure. Astrov is said to have 'a fine sensibility,' coarsened. Mr Pickup gets the sensibility, tries too hard for the coarseness, and so misses it. Unhelpful all round are John Murrell's contorted translation and Daphne Dare's set, which looks like an ingenious answer to the problems of doing the play on an open stage. In this proscenium theatre it merely shreds the action.

*

I like a Gershwin tune, and I was happy when Design for Living (Globe) kicked off with a Thirties recording of, appropriately, 'Beginners' Luck.' We then plunged into the relationship of painter Otto, playwright Leo, and late-flowering decorator Gilda, interrupted at, and during, intervals by selections from Cole Porter ('What is This Thing Called Love?' when Gilda left one for the other), Harry Warren ('We're in the Money' when Leo had a hit, 'You're Getting to Be a Habit with Me' when Gilda changed partners again), Billy Hill ('Glory of Love' could have been any time in the mad, mad whirl), Rodgers and Hart ('Manhattan' when the action moved there; a bit obvious if you ask me) and Harold Arlen ('As Long as I Live' when the threesome finally got stuck with one another).

Couldn't we have ended with Coward himself? World Weary to underline their beastliness to everybody else, or I'll Follow My Secret Heart to show their egotism, or I'll See You Again as they inevitably would? I'm showing off; but so do the characters, and so does the play. Even if we grant the stars to be as fascinating as Coward thought them, they are shown to us for at least one whole act too many. They are taken seriously here by Maria Aitken and her two cohorts. Alan Strachan's production doesn't play for laughs. At least I hope it doesn't; it didn't, on the first night, get many, though Miss Aitken fairly timed a very funny line about salmon.

*

At the Cottesloe you may catch a workshop production, previously toured, of The Caucasian Chalk Circle. It's followed by a discussion on the environment, but it isn't that bad. In fact it's very lively, its eight players rising spiritedly to the challenge of improvising their own props while playing multiple roles. Peter Needham is a reduced Azdak, but Patti Love's Grusche is a love.

James Hayes, smirking and dark-glassed as a gubernatorial prince, Greg Hicks as his overbred bureaucrat nephew and Roger Gartland as a stormtrooper are all hissable nasties. Only the music grates; a melange of oversweet pop-styles, including a spot of Country suggesting someone has the wrong Georgia on his mind.
