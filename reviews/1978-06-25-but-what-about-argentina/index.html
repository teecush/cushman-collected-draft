Jun 25, 1978

But What About Argentina?

Robert Cushman · The Observer

But what about Argentina?

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Evita.'

IN its deployment of light, space and bodies Evita (Prince Edward, ex-London Casino) sets new standards for the West End.

It is not only recent British musicals that stand condemned by comparison: when, for example, did you last see a Shakespearean crowd that looked capable of shifting the weight assigned to it? The Argentinian masses in this production really look as if they could be politically effective -- which makes their gullibility the sourer. In the biggest of the show's big scenes they assemble beneath the Perons' balcony, cheering and swaying literally non-stop. One man, transfixingly, just keeps jumping up and down.

It is a glowing image of fan-worship, and when Eva herself glides on to the balcony in bridal white the theatre audience get in on the act, applauding as if she were Eliza Doolittle on her way to the ball. She stops in the centre, and at last -- for we are now half-way through the evening -- sings 'Don't Cry For Me Argentina,' which, haunting but meaningless as a pop hit, suddenly acquires all the irony that has been claimed for it. Evita, beautifully, is putting one over.

This is her moment of stardom. Earlier we have seen her working her way up to it. The wittiest number in the show revolves around her bedroom door; gentlemen are ushered through it in ascending order of influence by an increasingly glamorous heroine. Harold Prince is the director, Larry Fuller the choreographer; the designers are Timothy O'Brien and Tazeena Firth, and David Hersey did the superlative lighting. Together they have brought off a sustained feat of imaginative animation.

All the same the show, judged by the standards Mr Prince set a decade ago in Cabaret and reaffirmed in Pacific Overtures, is unsatisfactory. You come to the end of it not knowing why it is there. This is not merely because it takes no obvious party line: after all, to be anti-Nazi, as Cabaret was, is hardly news, and Pacific Overtures aimed its satiric shafts all over the globe. But they were informative shows, and you felt that their creators had a personal stake in the subject-matter.

Tim Rice's lyrics here are reasonably intelligent and Andrew Lloyd Webber's music more than reasonably melodious (though rather lacking, despite Mr Webber's own sumptuous orchestrations, in dramatic backbone), but all they tell us about Eva Peron is that she was ambitious. And there is no knowing whether she left Argentina better or worse than she found it; the place looks much the same throughout. Peron himself is given a cynically sallow performance by Joss Ackland (he even has an olive-skinned voice), but all we really learn to his discredit is that his henchmen made a habit of beating up David Essex.

Mr Essex plays Che Guevara, though he might actually be any percipient malcontent. His function in the show is to cut Evita down to size. He is the man who sees through the glitter, though his criticisms are more aesthetic than political. Everyone thinks she has style; he denies it. And you have to listen to him because he has so much style himself. A cigarillo-puffing dandy, relaxed as Perry Como, he has all the best lines and -- if you'll forgive the expression -- capitalises on them. He is in fact the star; Elaine Paige's Eva, with all her energy and her clarion clarity, is the talented featured player. And so the show, which is about stardom if it's about anything, comes out fatally unbalanced.

*

At the crucial moment of Flying Blind (Royal Court) no less than three couples are trying, and failing, to make illicit love. The scene is Belfast where, we are given to understand, the phallus has been superseded by the gun. That is not a new analogy, but it can rarely have been exploited with such spirit and determination as in Bill Morrison's tragi-farce.

The man of the house has been impotent for months, and has turned for consolation to the recorded sound of Charlie Parker. An amateur saxist himself, he is playing along with a favourite track ('Lover Man,' as it sardonically happens), when he is surprised by four Protestant gunmen, theoretically on his side. Later on he is visited by a couple of their Catholic equivalents, and it gets very difficult to tell the difference.

Mr Morrison may not yet be in the Parker class, but he too is a master of the unexpected modulation; he does not delicately blend his tones, he switches them -- ferociously and hilariously. His terrorists (one called Magoo, trying to balance spectacles on his balaclava) are as incompetent as his lovers; it does seem possible for a time that the gun may be less mighty. He stirs in a politician, himself pressured into buying a weapon, and an ineffectual knight-errant who has come -- as he proudly announces -- 'from England.' Only the play's last line -- rather too carefully prepared -- lacks resonance.

Alan Dossor, whose direction of Funny Peculiar established him as the master of the meaningful sex-farce, does an even better job here; and Peter Postlethwaite gives the play a fine bewildered centre. The divine Rachel Bell plays, with memorable asperity, a buxom neighbour experimenting with adultery.

*

This week's tour of the world's plague-spots culminates in South Africa and The Jail Diary of Albie Sachs (Warehouse). Sachs, a white lawyer, served two consecutive terms under the 90-Day Law in 1963 and steadfastly refused to answer questions. A couple of years later he was picked up again; times having advanced, he was subjected to sleep deprivation, and caved in after 24 hours. David Edgar's adaptation of Sachs's book is studiously anti-sensational; hence it concentrates on the first phase of his ordeal and gives a remarkably persuasive picture of the interior of a man's skull through six months of solitary.

Peter McEnery's performance gives little hint of physical deterioration, but his measured voice, remarkably similar to Sachs's own as heard on radio last week, is mesmeric. He is almost fanatically fair-minded, and Mr Edgar, who has a flair for playing devil's advocate, gives him an Afrikaaner keeper (himself in all senses a victim of prejudice) whose fumbling attempts at friendliness break through his own mental rigidity. (To keep our sympathy within bounds, the same actor, Edwin Richfield, later doubles as the notorious interrogator Swanepoel.)

*

Gratitude to Greenwich for The Golden Cradle, an investigative programme of five Irish one-acts: three Yeatses, a Lady Gregory and a Synge, all but the last made accessible through lithe direction by Siobhan McKenna. And welcome to Michael Bogdanov, who as the new director of the Young Vic is the right man in the right place. His inaugural production of Bartholomew Fair is done in modern dress, which makes for a swing-and-roundabout fair; you lose the Jacobean authenticity that is a great part of the play's appeal but at least you can tell the characters apart. (The text includes a puppet-play which puts a classical story into Jacobean dress. His puppeteer claims that otherwise his audience would not understand. Jonson himself seems to disapprove.)

A good half-dozen of the cast are clearly incapable at present of playing Jonson in any costume, but Michael Attwell makes a breezy civil-servant simulacrum of Proctor Littlewit and Bill Wallis's shy authority is invaluable in the pivotal role of Justice Overdo, who comes to judge the fair and finds himself condemned. This, the central point of the play, is clearly made and there is fun en route. A puppet's derisive laugh closes the proceedings: a good ending, and for the new regime a good beginning.
