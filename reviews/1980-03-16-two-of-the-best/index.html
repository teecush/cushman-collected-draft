Mar 16, 1980

Two Of The Best

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Two of the best

ROBERT CUSHMAN profiles a successful duo of American writers, whose latest musical, On the Twentieth Century, opens at Her Majesty's Theatre this week.

BY TRADITION the best American comic playwrights have travelled in pairs.

In the twenties and thirties there were Moss Hart and George Kaufman, Ben Hecht and Charles MacArthur. The latter couple wrote The Front Page and also the backstage saga Twentieth Century, which became a celebrated film with John Barrymore playing a megalomaniac actor-manager closely resembling John Barrymore.

Much later -- 1978 to be exact -- it became a Broadway musical, retitled On the Twentieth Century (this being not an era, or even a fox, but a train) and opening in London this week. Book and lyrics are by Betty Comden and Adolph Green, a writing duo who began working together during World War Two. Neither has ever written anything independently of the other.

They have carried over into the post-war years their predecessors' distinctive New York strain of madcap urbanity. The line of descent is quite direct; Kaufman and Hart's Once in a Lifetime must have influenced Comden and Green's own spoofing of the coming of sound to Hollywood in Singin' in the Rain, and that marvellous film has certainly bequeathed a few gags to the current London production of the play.

Two subjects, often overlapping, have preoccupied Comden and Green throughout their writing career: show business and New York. If Singin' in the Rain is the classic backstage, or in this instance offscreen, musical, their first stage show, On the Town, summed up wartime Manhattan. It's the one that begins with three sailors, on 24 hours' leave, singing 'New York, New York, a helluva town,' a chant that has become proverbial.

That was in 1944, when the authors themselves were still wide-eyed, as were their collaborators: a young composer called Leonard Bernstein and a choreographer, Jerome Robbins. Since then they have become part of the scene themselves. Both partners were born in 1915, and neither looks it. Green has a shock of white hair, an open face and a crinkly smile. Comden, a tall brunette, exemplifies the most sensible kind of Manhattan chic. I would guess that the fantasy in their work stems mainly from him, and the satire from her, but since they maintain that even their closest friends can never tell who wrote what, I would not press it.

They are not married; at least, not to each other. Green's wife is the actress Phyllis Newman, whom he met when she was Judy Holliday's stand-by in one of their most successful shows, Bells are Ringing; Comden was married for more than 30 years to art expert Steven Kyle, who died last October.

Actually they misled the world about their status when they wrote the film The Band Wagon, whose cast included Nanette Fabray and Oscar Levant as a husband-and-wife writing team who in other respects were clearly self-portraits.

Like their cinematic doppelgänger, Comden and Green are performers as well as writers. Indeed, like many others, they started writing because they couldn't afford to pay anyone else for material. They met when she was studying drama at New York University, and he was sort of unemployed. They teamed up with three other hopefuls, one of whom was Miss Holliday. They describe a perfectly true B-movie incident. Judy was caught in the rain in Greenwich Village and stumbled down some basement steps, and there was a man who turned out to be Max Gordon, who owned the Village Vanguard night-club and still does and looks exactly the same.

The new group were booked into the Vanguard under the name The Revuers, lampooning New York with particular reference to newspapers and theatres, and attracting distinguished and terrific audiences. S. J. Perelman, Robert Benchley and Lillian Hellman were fans, while Bernstein came to know their material better than they did themselves. He has been known to perform it from memory at parties.

The Revuers collaborated on words and music. 'Oh, we were both very musical. Betty,' says Green, waving at the piano in his partner's town house, 'plays four chords -- very well. Actually people still say "I love your music," and we have to disabuse them.'

After the Vanguard, the Revuers' career halted. They were out of work for almost a year, then made their way to Hollywood and break-up. 'The studios only wanted Judy. There was another B-movie scene; we had to talk her into deserting us.' A bedraggled duo returned to New York. Comden went first to join her husband, on a brief furlough from the Services. When Green followed, dejected, he was greeted on the station platform by his team-mate bearing a banner inscribed, 'Adolph Green Fan-Club.' ('I was a very gloomy fan club.') The incident was recycled in The Band Wagon; Fred Astaire, playing a washed-up version of himself, arrives at Grand Central to be greeted in similar fashion by, more or less, Comden and Green.

Their luck broke with On the Town. It was Bernstein's first musical and he, the city's favourite prodigy, insisted that they be commissioned to provide the words. They themselves insisted that they be included in the cast as Ozzie and Claire de Loon, sailor and anthropologist respectively. 'It wasn't so easy to sneak ourselves into the show. We had to audition for the director, George Abbott.' They haven't snuck themselves into any of their subsequent musicals, but they have appeared from time to time in a mélange of their own work called A Party.

On the Town was followed by Billion Dollar Baby, ruefully described as 'a succès d'estime.' 'It was a sardonic look at the 1920s and people weren't ready yet for a show with people jumping out of windows.' However, it planted in Hollywood's head the idea that they write about the Twenties, and they found themselves at MGM revamping the old chestnut, Good News. Then came the film of On the Town, and a trio of the studio's (and therefore Hollywood's) best musicals: The Band Wagon with Astaire, Singin' in the Rain and It's Always Fair Weather with Gene Kelly.

All three films took their tone from their funny, joyous, lemon-flavoured scripts. The duo fashioned theatrical satire that was neither inbred nor sentimental, but fastened a gleeful eye on cant or self-glorification. The pinnacle perhaps was the silent-movie queen (Jean Hagen) telling her adoring audience, 'If we have brought a little joy into your humdrum lives then it ain't all been in vain for nothin'.'

Back on Broadway they had one more triumph with Bernstein (Wonderful Town) and a string of shows with music by Jule Styne. On the Twentieth Century has united them with Cy Coleman, whose snazzy, jazzy style would seem to be a perfect match for theirs.

Unforeseeably, though, the collaborators have set this saga of battling egos as mock operetta. 'Cy played something and we said, let's hear that again. He said, you mean that bit of Rossini. We said that's it, and it became a song.' Having told me so much, they sang the song. Songwriters have hummed me the odd line on previous occasions, but this is the first time I have been treated to a full-throated duet.

They won't be at the London first night. They have been elected to New York's Songwriters' Hall of Fame -- an achievement if you are still alive and are generally thought of as theatre, rather than music people -- and they are staying in town for the ceremony. Coincidentally, Michael's Pub -- a jazz-spot that goes in for composer-tributes -- is running an evening of their songs.

If you are searching your memory for their hits, try 'Just in Time' or 'The Party's Over' from Bells are Ringing. But isolated songs, even isolated shows, are not the point. They represent some of the best of Broadway tradition, and some of the most essential.
