Jun 6, 1982

Judy, Billie & Co

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Judy, Billie & Co

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'A Star is Torn,' 'Queen Christina' and 'Talley's Folly'

ROBYN ARCHER does impressions. This is denied in the programme-note to her one-woman show, A Star is Torn (Theatre Royal, Stratford East), but the kindest word I can find for that is disingenuous. Miss Archer works hard and skilfully at recreating the sounds of Judy Garland and Billie Holiday. She does not generalise. She marks off the several stages of Garland's progression from the winning child to the driven woman with her voice in a state of terminal vibrato.

She gives us two periods of Holiday: the tragedy queen of the Fifties with her vinegar croak and the delicately barbed swinger, voice flavoured with alcohol but not yet coated, of the Thirties. Her interwoven medley of early Holiday standards is glorious, peaking in a 'Fine Romance' more pointed than the lady's own. (She, unlike Miss Archer, was no satirist.)

Other assumptions are less exact: Helen Morgan comes off too genteel, while Miss Archer loses the melody-line in trying to re-create the baroque edifices that Dinah Washington used to erect upon it. But for each of her ladies she creates a presence. She comes before us springy and white-faced (lighting as much as make-up) and immediately, with a gentle waddle and a pair of outstretched arms ending in upturned palms, she is Bessie Smith, black blues singer--though Miss Archer refreshingly emphasises the rampant bawdy vaudevillean.

She also does a good guttural Piaf. Indeed her mastery, if that's the right word, of differing styles is remarkable. You may guess her range from the fact that she is happiest in music-hall, where she does Marie Lloyd, and in rock, where she out-does Janis Joplin. You may also get the point, if the title hasn't given it away. These are all ladies untimely dead, victim figures, self-destructive. The latter would be the word generally used, but Miss Archer is out to lay the blame somewhere else or everywhere else: on the entertainment business, on audiences, on the male sex.

Marilyn Monroe, we are told, was destroyed by male chauvinism, which denied her talent its proper outlet. 'She could sing, she could dance.' Yes, but so could plenty of others, better. The charge in Monroe's personality and physique was precisely the element that appealed to male chauvinists. Without it she would have been nowhere: better off perhaps, but nowhere.

Miss Archer's thesis seems to be that what her heroines had to sell was vulnerability (wot, Marie Lloyd?) and that reality and fiction became confused, leaving them defenceless. Any kind of vulnerability will do, even vulnerability to traffic. When we were solemnly told, apropos the death in an air-crash of country singer Patsy Cline, about 'the physical reality of fast travel,' I temporarily lost patience with the show.

Wartime morale-builder Jane Froman is dragged in, because she was crippled in an accident, though as Miss Archer more or less admits, she was about as vulnerable a performer as Vera Lynn. I wish she had followed this up. She might have considered Ethel Waters, probably the most influential popular singer of the century, whose childhood exactly paralleled Billie Holiday's, but who survived--at a cost.

The contrast would have been instructive and Miss Archer could have had fun with the songs. (Waters sang everything.) It would have done more justice to her intelligence than handing out cliches about Holiday--'a visceral reality,' 'honey-and-molasses voice,' 'epitome of sex'--at a rate that would shame a sleeve-note, or retelling myths about the death of Bessie Smith (denied entrance to a white hospital) that doubtless should be true, but aren't.

Miss Archer's conspiracy-theories are possibly born out by the fact that the only male vocalist ever to trade on being lost was Frank Sinatra, and he snapped out of it. The doomed male vocalist had to wait for the rock age, though jazz was full of doomed instrumentalists: Parker, Beiderbecke, you name them. She has a case, but she spoils it when she talks--at least in her own voice.

She does a wonderful Joplin monologue ('it's not the having nothing, it's the wanting') that says everything about a performer who was really an artist in self-destruction, having learned from her predecessors who just fell into it. This even admits that Joplin, despite everything, had a better time than most. So did her other heroines; the satisfaction of applause may be transitory (what isn't?), but it is still very nice and they got a lot of it.

So does Miss Archer, deservedly; she is not very moving (you can't be when you sing heartbreak songs that the format forbids you to make your own), but she certainly excites. Her choice of material is admirably un-obvious; and a final medley in which she juggles her idols is sensational.

*

Pam Gems wrote a play about Piaf, and if her Queen Christina (Tricycle) had only sung she too would have made a great Archer subject. Raised as a boy, then placed on the throne of Sweden, she found plenty of reason to be contemptuous of both sexes. Miss Gems, with both logic and variety, shows her trapped but buoyant, bewildered but argumentative, brutal but agonised. She also endows her with wit, though in Chrissie Cotterill's performance this often emerges as bloody-mindedness.

Miss Gems writes heroic roles (in this, if in nothing else, she is John Osborne's successor) and Miss Cotterill, though chirpy and valiant, falls short. Apart from Peter Baldwin, smooth in a bunch of establishment roles, her colleagues amplify her faults. The prevalent note among younger British actors is unfocused sullenness: it distorts harmlessly neutral lines, while denying real anger. Pam Brighton's production is at any rate good-looking.

*

So is Talley's Folly (Lyric, Hammersmith) in Grant Hicks's boat-house set, moodily lit. Lanford Wilson's play is the story of a southern American girl wooed and won in wartime by a Jewish accountant; embodying the clash of cultures and union of personalities are Hayley Mills, strong and graceful, and Jonathan Pryce, fluent, versatile, funny, painful, alive. He starts the play with an ingratiating monologue explicitly designed to charm us. It succeeds. So does everything else.
