Nov 1, 1968

Close the Coalhouse Door

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

Close the Coalhouse Door

For me television drama is pre-eminently a dispensable alternative to whatever old movie is playing on the other channel, so all honour to Alan Plater who once hooked me for three successive Mondays with a trilogy called To See How Far It Is. At its best, which was somewhere around its middle, it displayed a notable talent for the elegant facing of painful truths and impressed upon the mind the necessity of judging its author's future work by high standards. Well then, judging by those standards, this particular example of its author's future work is quite disgraceful. It was also one of the half-dozen most entertaining evenings on offer in the London theatre. Let me argue myself out of that one.

It makes only limited sense to consider Close the Coalhouse Door as an Alan Plater play, or as an anyone play. It is the first example to reach London of the regional historical-documentary which is increasingly recommending itself to provincial reps as a means of proving that they are where they are because they want to be. It hails from Newcastle and traces, in the approved comic-strip cum music-hall manner, the fortunes and more especially, the misfortunes of the Northumbrian pit-man over the past two centuries. I wish that that were all it did.

But Mr Plater had to go and fix it up with a framework. He begins with (and rapidly drops) an oleaginous Southern expert who might have been expressly designed to bring out the worst in Ken Wynne; this talented comedian, presented with a weak script, can always be relied on to descend to it. By the quantity of his mugging shall ye know the quality of what he mugs. In this show he plays most of the Establishment-figures so he mugs much, though he does get in a not-bad Harold Wilson towards the end.

But I run ahead of myself. Mr Wynne's expertise is summarily dispensed with, and we are left with the real people who are celebrating a golden wedding, and taking the opportunity to reminisce. The writing here is amorphous and undistinguished, fit to reinforce anyone's prejudices about cosy TV dramatists straying beyond their proper limits. Then suddenly we are in the 1820s witnessing the birth of trade unionism in the pits. The treatment here is based starkly and sensibly on the proposition that a bare recital of the working-conditions of the time still makes the blood boil. Just as we are beginning to get stirred and interested, we are returned to the tedious 1960s, and soon afterwards the lights go up, leaving us to reflect on the wisdom of Shakespeare and Brecht and John Arden and all other dramatists who didn't reckon that the only way to make the past relevant was to keep cutting back to the present.

Occasional songs have helped Act One on its way; and in Act Two they become a good deal less occasional. The General Strike is the period covered, a source apparently of jolly good fun for all. This at any rate is the impression conveyed by the rather haphazard light-heartedness of the treatment, all fun and games and vaudeville. (Most would-be impersonators of Groucho Marx succeed with the walk but can't manage the voice; discuss, with special reference to Bryan Pringle at the Fortune Theatre, and attempt to explain what Groucho is doing in the show in the first place.) Of course the fun is meant to highlight the pain underneath but that would need a much subtler and more accurate counterpoint of theme and treatment than is aimed at here. We are thrown back on the fun as fun, in which capacity it is extremely successful. It only starts wearing thin in Act Three, where contemporary problems get on top of the play and leave it floundering. A paradox is (just about) adumbrated but never explored; if the pit is such a bloody awful job, why weep for its disappearance? The play stops being funny but can't seem to start being serious: the closing song (“It's only a story, a fanciful tale”—irony intended) tries to wipe from our faces a smile that has long since vanished but cannot summon up a scowl to replace it.

Meanwhile the family drama has limped on, partially sustained by an extremely strong performance from John White as a young pit-man half inclined to break out, and brought to the brink by the socially-conscious girl-friend of his college-bred brother.

There remain a mixed but largely enjoyable bunch of songs (by Alex Glasgow) and the riotous performances of three underrated character actors. Colin Douglas, previously known to me as the toughie of innumerable children's serials, turns the paterfamilias into a vastly endearing shambles of a man. He shares the burden of history with two mates who verge on the out-of-this-world. Of course I always knew that Bryan Pringle could gangle, but even expected pleasures sometimes acquire special intensity. I never knew that John Woodvine could sing, and years of watching him being dour, solid and invaluable at the Old Vic, the Mermaid and such had not prepared me for the warmth, passion and humour of his performance here. From now on I want to see him properly used.
