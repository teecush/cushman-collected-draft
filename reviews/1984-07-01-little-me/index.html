Jul 1, 1984

Little Me

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

Little Me

Not too many musical comedies are actually funny. Guys and Dolls of course, but even there the romance is meant to be taken pretty much at face value; laughter is a strong but not a pervasive element. Otherwise there are three, all clustered together at the beginning of the 1960s: How to Succeed in Business Without Really Trying, A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum, and Little Me. Forum, as an exemplar and a celebration of farce, is sui generis; a musical based on Plautus and set in ancient Rome would have to be. But the other two have much in common. They arrived in consecutive seasons, both produced by Feuer and Martin, both choreographed by Bob Fosse.

Both have fragmentary literary sources: suggested by rather than adapted from. Both are satires, dealing in a bland acceptance of the outrageous. Remember mediocricity is not a mortal sin sings Frank Loesseer's climbing tycoon, for the delectation of an audience largely composed of the mediocrities themselves. "So here's a good tiding, if men you are killing, a talent you're hiding, to be a performer' sings Carolyn Leigh's vaudeville booking agents to the innocent multiple murderess who is the heroine of Little Me, thereby saying in four lines what it took Chicago two laboriously cynical hours to establish. Both, How to Succeed and Little Me, are brilliant. Little Me is the story of Belle Poitrine, née Schlumpfert, a girl of extreme poverty and Four of the parts Russ Abbot plays in Little Me - top, Amos Pinchley, and Noble Eggleston.

Below: Fred Poitrine and Val du Val. ignorance, and of Noble Eggleston, a youth of extreme riches and accomplishment who is educated at Yale and Harvard, simultaneously, and in later years pursues a dual career as lawyer and surgeon, taking time out to be a World War One flying ace and to save the lives of all passengers and crew in a shipwreck of Titanic proportions. Noble's one disadvantage is his mother, who is unspeakable. She disapproves of Belle, especially of the way she smells, and keeps the two apart. To prove herself worthy, Belle, who has the kind of figure that used to be described as pneumatic, determines to acquire wealth, title and education.

She gets them through the good offices of a succession of mostly rich lovers (and even a couple of husbands), each of whom accidentally expires after giving Belle his all. The story is told to us as a showbiz autobiography, the mature Belle commenting from the sidelines on the adventures of her younger self. It is interesting to see this device again in the wake of its use in Jean Seberg and to realise that it could never be anything but funny, intentionally or otherwise. In its original (1962) incarnation, the story was told on the golf-course by a successful elder Belle to the ghost-writer of her memoirs. In the current version, deriving partly from an unsuccessful New York revival of a year or so ago, our narrator is on the skids, telling her tale as part of a seedy saloon act.

This means a new opening number, 'Don't Ask a Lady', to replace the original "The Truth' (which went, in part, 'as in a haze/I see a Doubledays/all shining in a window full of Arthur Miller plays'). The replacement is a better song but not as apt; and the show is robbed of some of its point if the sacred monster romanticising her past is nudged from her outrageous pinnacle. Indeed all the obvious structural alterations are questionable; instead of being a childhood admirer, Belle's gangster wooer now emerges from nowhere, does his big number and disappears until the ending, by which time we have forgotten him. The natty title song, an overlapping duet for old and young Belle, is confused by having their mother get into the act.

(For some reason, however loose a show's structure, reassigning numbers is always a mistake. Two more of the original songs have gone; one, 'Poor Little Hollywood Star', is unlamented, but deletion of the other, the aforequoted 'Be a Performer', not only robs us of a wonderful song dripping with callous insouciance ('unless you, God forbid/get hung for what you did/oh-ho you naughty kid') but leaves very cloudy Belle's crucial progression onto the boards. Having written a classic showbiz satire, Neil Simon has nearly taken the showbiz out of it. But a lot of great stuff remains. Some of Mr Simon's best work is in his books for musicals. Removed from its usual realistic confines, his imagination thrives; it expresses itself generally in jokes, and the audience is spared the discomfort of wondering whether any characters would converse so exclusively in one-liners.

These are not people; this is a cartoon with a laugh in every balloon. My admiration for the lyrics of the late superb Miss Leigh must now be manifest; her JULY 1984 rhymes are prodigious, her jokes as good as Mr Simon's and tougher. The score is actually a little more sophisticated than the book, but since this is not your integrated Rodgers-and- Hammerstein musical the discrepancies hardly matter. Miss Leigh and Cy Coleman were the premier song-writing combination of the postwar years, and superior pop songs witty, rhythmic and curvaceous - are what they offer here. The score takes in the jazz anthem of 'On the Other Side of the Tracks', the blatant, pounding slinkiness of T've Got Your Number', the hilarious Central European prance-lament of 'Goodbye' (subtitled "The Prince's Farewell'), the affecting waltz of Real Live Girl', and the tongue-in-cheek hoe-down morality of 'Deep Down Inside'.

Mr Fosse's reconstructed choreography makes the Western origins of this last one invigoratingly clear; the bums who dance it have been fitted out by Tony Walton in bucolic splendour, the gladdest of rags. His costumes and sets, especially the vast tilt of his sinking liner, are triumphs. The older Belle is well handled by Lynda Abbot as Eggleston with Sheila White as the heroine, Belle. Baron, and the younger is scintillatingly done by Sheila White. While two actresses share the female lead, the male star appropriates no less than eight individual roles. Traditionally this feat has been performed by a television comedian: Sid Caesar, Bruce Forsyth, and now Russ Abbot. Mr Abbot has not Mr Caesar's mastery (on the record anyway) of the wry aside, or the killer instinct of Mr Forsyth.

It is an index of his essential gentleness that his most successful assumption is that of Fred Poitrine, the appallingly vulnerable doughboy whom Belle marries in order to have a father for her child. As Mr Pinchley, a face-grinding landlord of 88 or as a Ruritanian monarch with a fatal case of National Debt, he lacks something in ferocity and in precision. But goodwill takes him a long way in his other roles, and each of his recurring appearances as Noble is more confident than the last. Little Me has never been a commercial success. Maybe musicals attract a sentimental public that resents being made to laugh. Maybe audience identification is thwarted by a split heroine and a subdivided star.

Maybe the show is imperfect; certainly this revival is. It is still the second most invigorating show in town....
