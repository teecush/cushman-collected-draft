Oct 26, 1980

Wood Fun

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Wood Fun

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Manchester.

VICTORIA WOOD is the greatest good fortune to have befallen the British musical in decades. Good Fun (King's Head), for which she wrote book, music and lyrics, lives wryly up to its title, but I don't believe in it. We need to know, from the start, just what conventions are being utilised, what kind of song Miss Wood is using, and why.

Her natural mode is confessional. Her characters explain and reveal themselves. These songs do not move the plot along: they halt it while amplifying - acutely and with a splendid ear for detail and for the humours of self-betrayal - the people in it. Liz, the heroine, tells us at curtain-rise that she is a middle-class do-gooder, working hard at benevolence in order to compensate for disappointed sexual and social ambitions. (Much of this, Miss Wood being skilful, is implied rather than stated.) Liz's perch is an arts centre ("an adaptable space," as she repeatedly explains) and her immediate project is a party with entertainment for 300 cystitis victims.

The preparations, which are not smooth, bring her into contact and conflict with a goofy librarian who fancies her and with her own adored, self-styled artist soiled with the last droppings of the alternative culture; not to mention a passing lady beautician, oppressively full of joie de vivre, a dolefully pregnant catering help (salads only), and an old school friend who, in Liz's words, has a much better time than ugly girls are entitled to, but seems jaundiced by it all.

Her number "I've had it up to here with men" ("I'd be happier, I know/If we could only go/From the foreplay straight to the cigarette") is already a standard in Miss Wood's own repertoire; that she also has a glorious store of one-liners may have something to do with Miss Wood having played the role herself in Sheffield.

I was the mug who complained, at that first production, that the male characters, not being allowed raw singing, were being given a raw and even prejudiced deal. Now the chaps do get to join in. The librarian is one half of a courtship duet which is a treat. He also joins in Liz's early morale-boosting routine about facing up to organisational problems ahead. So does his parasitic opposite number. This is disaster; the guy, as given to us, wouldn't be caught dead participating in well-drilled displays of enthusiasm. The assumption that any character may be dragooned into a number is part of our national musical-paralysis. When the author, like Miss Wood, has justified pretensions to be considered a playwright, it is fatal. Besides, the very idea of a concerted production number violates the implicit contract Miss Wood's other songs make with the audience. The show never recovers.

It does, though, provide many incidental pleasures. The theme of what men do to women, and more especially what women do to themselves with men in mind, is prettily dovetailed with the idea denoted in the title: pleasure, mostly imaginary, as a desperate panacea. The Northern background of fast food and minor bureaucracy - burgers and burghers - is impeccably observed; and nobody can beat Miss Wood at schoolhouse reminiscence.

The plotting is neat in conception, ineffectual in execution. There is, to scotch a growing rumour, no disparity between music and lyrics. When one is funny, the other is. Liz's last song is melodrama, words and music; a despairing solo for the unwilling mother is real drama. So does his parasitic ditto. Tina Marian's toughly melodious account of this role is one of London's two gains over Sheffield; the other is Kevin Moore's librarian. Paula Wilcox is pleasing as Liz, but the songs tax her and the production isn't much help. Nichola McAuliffe, by contrast, has more notes in her voice than the score can use, but she makes a neatly sardonic author's deputy.

*

The songs in The Streets of London (Her Majesty's) are discreet and enjoyable (music, Gary Carpenter; lyrics, Ian Barnett). The best of them, immaculately sung and strolled by Royce Mills, comes when we should be intent on the plot.

Boucicault, who wrote this roaring 1850s thriller, believed in throwing everything in. But new songs work, unavoidably, as a comment on the play rather than as a part of it, and there is rather too much comment already, especially in the audience, who on the first night were far more intent on enjoying themselves hissing the villain than on enjoying the play.

This was their loss, since the plot and stuffing of the piece are quite as entertaining as its conventions; three-sided reunions, double-suicide attempts, valiant sorties into blazing buildings, are affecting as well as rousing. William Squire is finely farouche as Gideon Bloodgood, unscrupulous banker, and Jane Wymark sublimely selfish as the daughter who is his only weakness; Michael Carter slinkily contorts voice and figure as a turning worm whose ambiguities show Boucicault at his inventive best. As usual the wrong'uns have the best of it; I would have turned the trio of odiously deserving poor into the snow without a moment's compunction. Diane Cilento's handsome and leisurely production knows which side its bread is buttered; the play may end with a prayer for the poor of London, but she caps it with a solid chorus in praise of gold.

*

Which brings us to Arthur Miller's The Price (Shaw), whose substance is a fight between two brothers, a cop and a doc, over the material and spiritual legacy of their father, who was ruined in the Depression and may or may not have needed their support thereafter.

Mr Miller, unusually among modern playwrights, is a humane and intelligent man of whom you might with some confidence inquire the time of day, and there are a good number of reliable insights, along with the carefully forged chain of memories. But you end up wondering why, if the dramatist knew so much about the characters' pasts, he couldn't have told us in the first place or better still why he couldn't have put that past on stage.

There is not much drama about the present; no sense that what these people learn will change their lives in any way. It is, by way of compensation, Mr Miller's funniest play; though this production is rather humourless. John Bennet depicts the gnawing pains of success successfully, and Frances Cuka manages not to play the policeman's wife as just an old grey nag: an achievement.

*

The Two Eugenes are not, as you might think, a pair of trick cyclists. They are O'Neill and Ionesco, two playwrights with historical status as innovators and "experimentalists" - maybe they were trick cyclists. Their plays revived as a double-bill at the Royal Exchange, Manchester, seem barren enough now. O'Neill's The Emperor Jones is chiefly notable for the blackface performance of a white actor, Peter Postlethwaite, as the Pullman attendant turned island dictator on the run in the jungle and encountering, in very sketchy hallucinatory form, his own fate and his people's. Monsieur Ionesco's The Chairs is the duologue for two nonagenarians with an incomprehensible message to bestow on an invisible audience. There is still some poignancy in their mutually supportive regrets, but they seem to go on for ever.
