Mar 23, 1980

Putting Paid To 'Othello'

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Putting paid to 'Othello'

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Scofield's Moor, a musical and Michael Frayn's latest play.

PETER HALL'S production of Othello, like his previous Shakespeares at the Olivier, is sternly devoid of anything you could call a concept.

This asceticism is apt to bear hard on those actors who have merely to stand around, especially if they are doing it on a stage this size. But Othello calls for fewer of these than any other tragedy; after all, the Venetian senate are likely to end up, however the play is staged, sitting in ranks and looking old. In this production quite a few of them are old, or at least mature. For once, too, the abundant talent and experience in the supporting cast is given the time and space to express itself.

Paul Scofield delivers the last three speeches of Othello himself -- especially the one that consists of the words "O fool, fool, fool" -- very simply and affectingly. Earlier he administers to Desdemona a crackling slap on the face. The rest of the time he is sonorously somnolent: no fires, no torment. Pride, of a quiet kind, there is; though what with his studied gestures -- which include the trying on for size of imaginary cuckold's horns -- it often seems more like self-approbation.

This suggests that Mr Scofield has adopted the Eliot-Leavis view of the Moor: an egotist who deserves even more than he gets. Why what amounts to a sustained jeer at the central character should be held to amount to a satisfying play I have never understood; but, such as it is, this interpretation needs to be followed through to the (literally) bitter end. Mr Scofield's end is sweet.

The first half of the play -- up to and including the brawl -- belongs to Iago, and Michael Bryant has no trouble with it, maintaining an even, sensible, humorous pace which enables him to switch effortlessly from manipulating his dupes to manipulating the audience. The chilled laughter he induces is just right. But he can make no contact with this Othello. The temptation scene is a slow fuse leading to a puff of smoke; it ends with no bond established between the two men, and that just about kills the play.

Felicity Kendal, who improves and improves, is a properly self-willed Desdemona, whose lies over the handkerchief are well marked. Yvonne Bryceland's Emilia has her usual unobtrusive firmness; Stephen Moore's kind and lazy Cassio, Michael Gambon's moon-faced, moon-minded Roderigo, and Penelope Wilton's Bianca, more housewife than hussy, all justify luxury casting. Mark Dignam even makes something of the sententious Duke.

*

As staged on Broadway, On the Twentieth Century (Her Majesty's) began with a theatre scene where a melodrama about Joan of Arc was interrupted by the absconding of the manager and the marooning of the cast who sang about it, feelingly and in operatic measures. "Screwed again," intoned a basso bishop, over heavy chords. This set up the style of the piece -- soaring tunes and ludicrous situations glorifying and undercutting one another -- as well as the vital relationship between the manager, Oscar Jaffe, and his two henchmen: business manager and press rep. It also delayed, and so made more effective, our first sight of the Twentieth Century itself, a glorious Art Deco beast, locomoting between Chicago and New York in the early Thirties.

This is a show about three monstre sacres: two people and a train. The train -- though it still gleams, shunts, jack-knifes and reverses with spirit -- is, perhaps through lack of space, less impressive than it was in New York: the people, though, are superior. As Lily Garland -- Oscar's one-time discovery, now lost to Hollywood and now his only chance of restoring his fortunes -- Julia McKenzie gives an authentic star-is-born performance, her top notes as triumphant as her snub-nosed comedy. (Often the two co-exist, which is the point of the show.)

Keith Michell is rather too low-keyed and likeable for the heartless flamboyance that book and score demand of Oscar; but there is enough chemistry between them -- and enough tact and timing in Peter Coe's direction -- for the romantic ending to seem crazily logical, where before it was inconsequential.

Betty Comden and Adolph Green wrote the book and lyrics from a play by Hecht and MacArthur; and the supply of one-liners is appropriate to the pedigree. Lily Garland, Miss McKenzie, shouts complainingly to the world, "has no life of her own." Equally absent-mindedly, she snarls of Oscar, "I wouldn't wipe my feet on this man if he was starving."

After the bludgeoning attempts at satire or nostalgia in recent musicals, this one's affable eye for the ridiculous may well come as a surprise. It is some time since a stuttering, and deliberately artificial, line -- like, but, but, but, but, but, but -- was given lyrical life. It's the verbal mainstay of a chase sequence, in which Robin Wagner's train really does its stuff; and which is followed by an even more stunning production number, a musical parody of a Somerset Maugham comedy, all silver-gilt and angular cigarette-holders.

Larry Fuller's witty, sometimes slapstick, choreography vitalises the longest numbers; and Cy Coleman's music, even at its lushest, retains his characteristic rhythmic pulse. This is actually his most theatrical score, appropriate to the characters and creating a style which the production (Harold Prince's originally and his touch still shows) takes up and polishes. I must mention David Healy and Fred Evans (one-time funny-faced dancer) as the sidekicks.

On the Twentieth Century is London's best musical since A Chorus Line.

*

You don't have to go back that far to find an exemplar for Michael Frayn's new play: Make and Break (Lyric, Hammersmith), but it ends an uncomfortably parched spell. It concerns the director of a British firm which makes walls, including doors, and is trying to sell them in German. He has erected his own psychological wall: an unshakeable absorption in his own work, maintained even while trying to seduce his secretary.

The seduction itself is undertaken because he thinks it's the thing to do: one way of taking an interest in his employees whom he questions unceasingly about their interests, as if seeking their technical specifications. Leonard Rossiter ferrets brilliantly; the role seems perfect for his particular form of withdrawn exhibitionism. A scene in which he thinks he may be dying of a heart-attack brings out unsuspected depths in both him and his author; it is not quite frightening, but it is certainly sobering.

Around him Mr Frayn builds a solid world, even though the construction is sometimes leisurely: the protagonist's entrance is too long deferred; and though Michael Blakemore's production handles a couple of dream-sequences (doors flying) with wit, he cannot quite make them fit. Pamela Salem as the secretary begins, so to speak, Sibylline, and steadily deepens, and there is a sensationally good (though quite unseasonal) performance from James Grout as second-in-command. The point boils down to "what's the point," but the question is seldom so carefully or sympathetically asked.

*

The new regime at the Theatre Royal, Stratford East, begins auspiciously with a revival of a Boucicault melodrama, The Streets of London; an exciting story and, in a burning-house scene, special effects to match.
