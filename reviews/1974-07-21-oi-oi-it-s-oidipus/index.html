Oi, Oi--It's Oidipus

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Oi, oi--it's Oidipus

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

MOST of us, when reading Greek tragedy, must at times have felt disinclined to take it seriously. We have found the heroes sententious and self-important, and the chorus tireless and tiresome staters of the obvious.

Most directors attempt to overcome this reaction; Hovhanness I. Pilikian tries to capitalise on it. Last week at Chichester he gave us the play which I generally think of as 'Oedipus the King' but which now confronts us, with fearless phonetic fidelity to the original, as Oidipus Tyrannus.

There is more than pedantry behind this. Mr Pilikian is much concerned with the literal meaning of the title. As he writes in his voluminous programme note: "'Oidipus' means 'swollen-foot.' Every time the name is mentioned in the play, it spotlights the cruel tale of his birth." We are assured that Sophocles's text is full of puns on the name, puns which the present translation (by Gail Rademacher) resourcefully echoes. Oidipus, say the chorus congratulating themselves on the joke, is the man who put our city back on its feet.

I cannot quite follow the director's apparent assumption that Oidipus's deformity--which, strangely, is hardly noticeable in Keith Michell's performance--in some way diminishes him. On the evidence of Philoctetes, whose hero suffered with even worse feet, Sophocles was sympathetic to such ailments. But Mr Pilikian is out to get Oidipus. Hence perhaps his use of that jarring pronunciation (summoning memories of Frankie Howerd's evocation of Greek tragedy: Odtpns... oi, de naghty puss).

Hence, too, the insistence on the hero as--in the modern sense--a tyrant. Did the word 'Tyrannus' have the same emotive significance for the Greeks? I lack the expertise to answer that question. I think Mr Pilikian has a telling point when he reminds us that reactionary regimes have had, not merely to emasculate, but to ban the Greek classics outright. I know that in Antigone Sophocles unsparingly condemned the lawless ruler Creon: the same Creon who is seen ascending to Oidipus's power at the end of the present play, and is also seen to behave outrageously in Oedipus at Colonus.

He condemns Creon, but he did not mock. Mr Pilikian would have it that in the case of Oidipus he did both; and he does his utmost to ensure that we should too. After all, goes the theory, Oidipus and his people were Thebans; the Thebans had sided against Athens in the Persian wars; and no public-spirited Athenian writer could possibly treat them sympathetically. The argument is horribly specious; Aeschylus, every bit as patriotic as Sophocles, managed in The Persians to write with compassion of the barbarians themselves. Could we not credit Sophocles with a similar immunity to xenophobia?

Mr Pilikian's polemical preoccupations give him one practical advantage; he cares about the way his actors speak the lines. His chorus are a collection of crotchety old dotards (and when their director decides to put them through the odd choreographic hoop they are plain embarrassing), but at least it is possible to hear and understand them. A sensible conversational vigour also informs much of the dialogue. The Priest (David King) in the opening scene hardly disguises his contempt as Oidipus, all too consciously the saviour of the city, addresses his suffering 'children.'

Mr Michell's sneering narcissism at this point is his best piece of acting in years. Thereafter his performance goes into neutral and remains there, a victim of the irreconcilable demands of author and director. For Mr Pilikian's juiciest news is that the play concerns 'a Watergate-style cover-up of the truth.' Last year, in this very column, Robert Brustein bemoaned President Nixon's failure to come as clean as did the Theban king when confronted with his misdeeds. The comparison struck me as unreasonable (a live politician can hardly be blamed for falling short of standards set by a mythical hero), but the implicit assumptions about Oidipus himself was surely correct. Halfway through the play he suspects that he is the incestuous parricide who has ruined Thebes and he says so, out loud, in front of Iocasta and the chorus. Being, by his own lights, a textual purist, Mr Pilikian has left this passage intact, and so sacrificed any chance of proving Oidipus a scoundrel. (No critics, to my knowledge, have tried to prove him a saint, though Mr Pilikian seems convinced that they have.)

Having--to his own satisfaction--knocked his protagonist down the director proceeds to kick him. He decides that the final appearance of Oidipus's two daughters takes place only in their sightless father's self-pitying imagination. Since the Greeks left no stage-directions there is no way either of proving or refuting this theory, but Mr Pilikian is fooling himself if he believes that Mr Michell emoting into space is a harsher or less pathetic image than Mr Michell embracing two real live girls. If you want (and I suppose you do) an opinion of Diana Dors's Iocasta you must go to some less charitable critic; but I liked Willoughby Goddard's Teutonically gutteral account of the shepherd-messenger (who chomes from Chorinth).

*

Mustapha Matura's Play Mas (Royal Court) is a sparkling achievement, as elegantly sewn as the suits whose virtues the Trinidadian tailor (Indian) extols in the first act to his apathetic apprentice (African). Comes the revolution, and the second act: the apprentice has become Chief of Police, and his former employer is tolerated (just) as a quivering informer. And still the talk is of suits, and of films, on the aesthetics of which the tailor had also been accustomed to hold forth. (Sometimes he combines the two; his theory is that Robert Mitchum is no actor--his seams do all the work.)

The symmetry is beautiful, both in the writing and in the acting of Stefan Kalipha and Rudolph Walker. The carnival image, denoted by the title, is less successful in binding the play together, though it occasions some delightful moments. As it proceeds, the play sails into late Shavian waters which it lacks the weight to navigate; not, however, to worry. Grieve instead for the Cambridge Footlights, who have thudded into the West End with a revue called Chox (Comedy). As my favourite Toytown character used to say, it ought not to be allowed.
