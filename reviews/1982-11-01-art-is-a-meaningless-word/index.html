Nov 1, 1982

Art Is a Meaningless Word

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

Art Is a Meaningless Word

Most theatre reviewers, I imagine, have been browsing through Diana Rigg’s book No Turn Unstoned if only to find some extracts from their own work. We have to browse because the index lists only the victims of the worst reviews ever, not the perpetrators. That really is the actor’s revenge.

The reaction of most of us, I think, will be that we never knew we were so nice. Do these amiable squibs really represent the worst havoc we have wrought on the egos of the distinguished players who replied to Miss Rigg’s questionnaire? They’re jokes; taken as such, frequently written as such. Quite often they are neutral, if amused, comments on the fix in which some poor performer has been placed by his author or director. They do not strike at the core of anybody’s talent. They can be laughed at, laughed with, laughed off. They will not provoke angry letters. You only, in my experience, get those when you have told a central, unwelcome truth. It doesn’t happen often.

The art of the showpiece bad review—as practised by Shaw, Tynan or Walter Kerr—seems to be dead. Bernard Levin tried to revive it in his period on the Sunday Times but how much more spontaneously vitriolic he seemed on the Mail or Express of the fifties. But the scene was different then. The commercial theatre was still thriving, so its worst products could be attacked with a clear conscience. They stood for something. Now, who cares? I used to enjoy myself writing elaborate demolitions, generally of misbegotten musicals, in this magazine. God, I was funny then. But it would seem perverse to give Fire Angel or Barnardo the lion’s share of a 1200 word piece in The Observer when there are worthy products of the subsidised theatre to be examined. Of course worthiness in itself is only a minor virtue, and may cloak major vices. That’s in theory. In practice, the National and the RSC have drawn some of our stings. Their offerings have a nasty habit of being at least technically sound. As demolitionists we are out of practise. Peter O’Toole’s Macbeth was, by general consent, the most notable of recent debacles, but how much truly sparkling prose did it elicit? Rapiers are rusting. Bludgeons are clumsy. Is there anything worse than the critic who, to show how uncompromising he is, goes on making the same negative point in every sentence of his review? There are critics who like dancing on graves in hobnail boots. But it’s themselves they condemn.

I believe that I now write best about things I like. For me the acid test comes on Sunday when I see the column in print. It reveals—perhaps to my eyes only—the differences between what I’ve really enjoyed and what I only thought I enjoyed. Too late then of course to do anything about it. I can only make good resolutions for the coming weeks, resolutions I almost invariably break.

A certain amount of self-deception goes on. Years ago, before I was doing this kind of thing for a living, I wrote in these pages that though no critic would turn black into white (‘integrity feels so much nicer’) every critic must often have juggled with the various shades of grey at his disposal, so as to come up with a shaped coherent account of a play which may have inspired no strong feelings in him either way. (Critics who claim to be passionate about everything are phonies.) But, where it matters, critics are usually honest, not because they are knights-errant but because they cannot function any other way. The typewriter keys freeze beneath the fingers of a liar (not always, unfortunately, beneath those of a fool). As I say, we may deceive ourselves, but we are truthful with you. We do not perjure ourselves for the sake of a good joke, because good jokes are born of conviction. They have always been made by the best critics. I suppose bad jokes are indefensible but I would suspect a critic who did not at least try to be amusing. I would wonder why he was wasting his time on two such frivolous areas as journalism and the theatre.

Our absence from Miss Rigg’s index is probably symbolic. Individual friendships may flourish between the professions but really they don’t think we count. They think we’re too kind. A critic who takes an actress to the theatre is in for a humbling experience. She will be more withering about the pathetic efforts of the people up there then he would ever dream of being, discussing them with the kind of scorn he reserves for talking about other critics. She went to a drama school that taught her the importance of Truth; those clowns on stage had obviously never been told about it and wouldn’t have had the taste to profit by it if they had. Meanwhile playwrights roam the streets in gangs, ready to mug any reporter who says a word in favour of some other mob. They seem to want us to adopt them (and most ungrateful children they’d prove). The constant cry of the professional is ‘you don’t mean you liked that’. The Brecht industry shows this in its most rarefied form: not its purest, since it allows academics to join in. It’s all fascinating; and I have to admit to feeling pleased when I find the professional opinion tallies with my own. But that’s a weakness in me. Professional opinion is immaterial. Actors aren’t audience. Theatre for actors (or, as it is known in a few remaining outposts, lunchtime theatre) is a sterile exercise. We might all just as well stay home and play charades.

Those who’ve actually been involved in whatever show is under inspection are likely to find the review irrelevant: not wrong, irrelevant. My own professional theatre experience has been minuscule—half-a-dozen shows directed, mostly ages ago, two appeared in—but it’s greater than any other critic’s so I may as well sound off about it. What it’s taught me is that there can be no reconciliation between those who live with a show for weeks and those who live with it for a couple of hours. Moments that have been lovingly prepared or elaborately despaired of are likely to pass by the assessors quite unnoticed. They rarely like what you think they should like, and they are unperturbed by what you expect them to hate. Reading the reviews, even when they’re favourable, is like getting a bucket of water in the face. Suddenly you are looking at the show from an angle you never knew existed. And then the angles multiply; an actor I worked with told me he could read all the reviews of a show, find them contradicting each other, and understand and sympathise with the reactions expressed in all of them. That’s unusually philosophical but I have something of the experience when I read my colleagues. A good reviewer is one who persuades you while you’re reading him that his is the only possible view of a play. The ones I respect are those who make me momentarily doubt my own judgement. (The opposite of that is the critic you dread agreeing with.)

Now you would think that the public, who also are only with the play for a few hours, would be closer to the critics than they are to the practitioners but this may not be so. For the spectator, as for the participant, the show is a special event; it bulks larger for him than it does for the reviewer who goes to the theatre four times a week. (That’s my own average, and incidentally the answer to the question I am most frequently asked.) The spectator has also paid money: a sobering fact that critics should bear in mind more often than they do. He may well be more attentive than the reviewer who only needs to get enough of the play to furnish him with copy, he will be more demanding, and also more tolerant. One common critical fault—I’ve often committed it—is to point out weaknesses and inconsistencies that will have no effect whatever on anyone’s enjoyment of the show. (We strain over implausibilities in new plays that we would swallow whole in a classic.) Our job is not primarily to pass judgement on a text or even on a performance but to take the temperature of a theatrical event and try to account for it. Many events are simple: we arrive, they perform, we applaud, they bow, we leave. But there can be complicating factors: audience expectations or suspicions of a particular actor, play or subject, the laughter of discovery, the confirmation of boredom, the draughts or heatwaves, the unacknowledged conventions. (People always enjoy songs in plays, because they put a stop to that endless dialogue. They are critical of songs in musicals, because they are meant to be the point of the evening. Folk-songs mean moral uplift and are greeted with suspicion except by devotees.) These are what I most like writing about, and I should do it more often.

Art is a meaningless word, employed to give false authority to personal preferences. There are just shows, enjoyable or insufferable. That is the one critically useful thing that working in the theatre can teach you. The customer is always right. Since the theatre is a collaborative form, it’s also theoretically useful to know who does what, but even then you can’t always tell from out front. A really scrupulous critic would probably never even mention a director. Directors, for this reason, often get an easy ride from critics; so, most of the time, do actors. The ones who really suffer are playwrights. There is no known way of reviewing a play without referring to its author. There was once a critic who was invited to rehearsals, and came away impressed by how hard everybody worked and how arbitrary was the convention of the first night; but let us assume that whatever their faults most reviewers are ordinarily sensitive people who can work this out for themselves.

I now want to contradict myself and assert that critics are quite close to audiences after all. Ralph Richardson, quoted in Miss Rigg’s book, gives me the cue. He says that all spectators worth their ticket-stubs criticise the show when they get home. This reassured me since I was once dashed by what seemed an unanswerable criticism of critics by William Gaskill. He said that we always got the theatre wrong because we were concerned with judging it while the right thing to do was just to experience it. (‘What else can they do?’ he asked, sympathetically but unforgivingly. ‘They are critics.’) But I now believe, fortified by Sir Ralph, that this audience which experiences without evaluating is a sentimental myth. As soon as you have seen more than two shows you begin comparing. I bet Mr Gaskill himself judges unmercifully.

Another myth is that of the lost, scorned masterpiece. I doubt if a play has ever been produced that somebody didn’t champion. Look at the list of approved martyr-works. Saint’s Day? JC Trewin, not usually an avant-guardian, included it in his ‘Plays of the Year’. Serjeant Musgrave’s Dance? Philip Hope-Wallace loved it. Afore Night Come? Tynan called it a work of genius. The Birthday Party? Harold Hobson will never let us forget. Now of course these were all minority voices. But that’s fair enough. These plays were all, when they appeared, minority tastes. The mass of the public—even of regular theatregoers—would have been bored by them, and there’s no point whatever in boring people. The critical majority prevented this happening; the minority insured that the plays would stay around to be revived when tastes had changed. Meanwhile the reviews acted as a barometer of public reaction: a considerable sociological service. The strength of our pluralistic situation is that every shade of response to a given show is likely to be represented somewhere in the reviews. This has nothing to do with the competence of reviewers. It’s merely a vindication of the law of averages.

I’m not consistent about this. I snarl when other critics praise a show I despise or when audiences flock to it. I sulk when something I have praised bites the dust. But really I have no right. I have had the chance to state my own opinion. I should take it, as vigorously as possible, and then shut up. The play has to make its own way—as it inevitably will, acquiring in the public mind a ‘critical reputation’ that may have little to do with what the reviewers actually said. Our effect on the commercial fortunes of plays is infinitely variable. We do have power, and it’s good for our souls that we should be aware of it, but we never know how much, and that’s probably good for us too. We can inflict pain; the knowledge should not inhibit us but it should not be spared us either. And perhaps the best thing to be said for the critic-as-practitioner is not that he can learn something but that he can add a little to the universe’s stock of cosmic fairness by getting shot at himself.

Readers, by the way, treat critics as cavalierly as critics treat theatricals. I have been accused of favouring the fringe, of favouring the establishment; of always raving or damning, of always sitting on the fence; of being solemn, of being flip, of being American. I have been asked, regularly, whether my first responsibility is to the theatre or to my paper and have still not figured out where the conflict is supposed to come in. I have comforted myself, when told that critics know nothing about acting, with the reflection that most actors know even less about journalism.

All a critic really needs—and it cannot be manufactured—is a temperament. Without it he may, in Lionel Trilling’s valuable terminology, be sincere but he will not be authentic. His only real subject is himself. But he’d better forget that while he’s actually writing. Critics who keep telling you who they are don’t trust themselves to be anybody. We should be able to convey our enthusiasms by describing the shows: bold admonitions like ‘a triumph—rush for seats’ are a cop-out like appending ‘recommended’ or the reverse to a review Time Out fashion.

Critics should, preferably, be able to write. And they should, undoubtedly, have standards. I want the theatre to make me laugh, cry, tap my feet or hold my breath. I think that covers everything.
