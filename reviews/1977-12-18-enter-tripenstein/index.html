Dec 18, 1977

Enter Tripenstein

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Enter Tripenstein

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on three comedies

IT PROMISED to be a funny week; it threatened to be a glum one. Three comedies were billed; all of them classics. Hence the promise and hence the threat.

It may seem rather early to brand Fosdyke II (Bush) a classic, but the original Bill Tidy cartoon probably qualifies. While the success of the previous stage version, The Fosdyke Saga, has infected everybody's attitudes towards its successor, both audience and performers arrive feeling that a thundering good time is about to be given and taken. The notices have been good, the houses crammed, everyone laughed a lot and so did I. All the same it took me some time to get going and even when I did I had misgivings.

Assurance has, I think, edged over into complacency; the Fosdykes have become a cult, and cultists are not good audiences for comedy: their enthusiasm takes from the evening its edge of danger. More important, though, is the script that Mr Tidy and Alan Plater have provided. Part One had at any rate the tips of its toes just touching the ground; the Fosdykes were a gorgeous parody of all the gritty suffering families who ever got involved in trouble at t' mill. So their rise to brutal capitalist eminence as usurpers of the Ditchley world tripe empire had a touch of satire about it as well as fantasy. However, Part Two, which begins with them at the top and (I don't think I'm giving much away) ends with them there, is all fantasy. The Fosdykes have - you guessed it - lost touch with their roots.

A few weeks ago I suggested the Goons as an influence on Censored Scenes from King Kong. Their inky imperial prints are all over Fosdyke II as well, with Fosdyke missions despatched on hazardous missions to the African interior or the top of Everest. (Beaten to the latter by the Accrington Stanley expedition, tripe-eaters to a man.) A line like "men can be driven mad by his sinister oriental background music" could only have one fountain-head. In Mike Bradwell's production it is completely done, but I wondered if they weren't working on the wrong side of the street. Instead of pure Goons there is a large flavouring of music-hall and a dash of pantomime. The audience is bewitched, bullied and belaboured. (This, by the way, is enjoyable; nobody else had better try it, that's all. Except Barry Humphries.)

Only one episode, though, is really inspirational: involving the creation by a mad scientist of an artistic but threatening but vulnerable monster (called Tripenstein) and his encounter with the eternally nubile Victoria Fosdyke (Hazel Clyne), who wonderfully, but for this scene only, has regressed to childhood. Otherwise the Fosdykes as characters lose out to Jim Broadbent as their implacable opponent, gleefully self-described as "smelly old Roger Ditchley," disinherited, vengeful, and if not smarmy then certainly, in his ill-fitting shiny mohair and his ill-fitting shiny moustache, trying to be. Maybe he will sustain Fosdyke III which must come, for how otherwise will I be able to dub the finished epic not a trilogy but a Tripetych?

*

The Roundhouse, especially when two-thirds empty, is not an ideal setting for The Importance of Being Earnest, and the first act, through no particular fault of the players, was death. The lines were well enough spoken and the lines in that act are practically all, but most of us knew them by rote already and the house was not warm enough to rekindle our laughter. But Act Two is another matter; it is far more dependent on character and situation, greed and selfishness coming increasingly to the fore.

The tea-table duel of Gwendolen and Cecily is practically infallible and by the time Act Three rolled round we were conquered. The Actors' Company has been much strengthened by the return, as players and directors, of founder-members Tenniel Evans and Edward Petherbridge. Mr Evans, responsible for this one, also weighs in as both butlers, done in differing shades of depreciation, and Mr Petherbridge is Chasuble, walking delicately as Agag and continually shamed by his unruly tongue: since he cannot help licking his lips with it when in the presence of Prism. Ann Firbank is an impeccable Lady Bracknell; and though none of the juniors merit an adjective, they all seize vigorously on salient points. Thought has obviously been given as to which epigrams shall be rattled off and which shall be worked out in transit.

*

The strength of Trevor Nunn's production of The Alchemist, over which I raved at the Other Place this year, was always its steady build from solid foundations. At the Aldwych I feared for a time that it might be too steady; the opening quarrel, the first deceptions and disguises, were all deliberately done, but I wondered if they would ever cut loose. In one sense they never do; the text - a rare thing in Jonson - remains within their grasp, joyous and comprehensible. In another they certainly do: the craft of the cheaters, the hopes and then the furies of their victims rise like Wonderloaf and Hovis (Fosdyke II, song from) and beyond.

The most improved performance is Roger Rees's puritan Ananias; at any given moment he will mount a chair, preach and denounce, so that in the end people hide their furniture at his approach. It is a fine gag, but it would not work if Mr Rees had not the mortified fire and the airy comicality to set it off. John Woodvine's Subtle and Ian McKellen's Face have grown to virtuoso proportions; Mr McKellen, who has had a year, is as funny as, in his dedicated cupidity and his apparent sacrifice of identity in the cause of multiple illusion, he is frightening. Indeed this play in this production has it all: the dirt and detail of humanity, the broad sweep and cutting edge of moral poetry, the breathless dazzle of farce.

Mr Nunn's production finally accomplishes for Jacobean comedy what William Gaskill did in The Recruiting Officer et seq for the Restoration variety. To really pile it on: it is a match for Peter Brook's new Paris production of Ubu, which I shall write about shortly, and it exemplifies his comments on the paradoxical nature of textual fidelity: that you cannot release a play's anarchic spirit unless you strictly respect its letter.
