Explosions In Naples

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Explosions in Naples

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on de Filippo's Filumena

The initial situation of Eduardo de Filippo's Filumena (Lyric) is comic operatic. The Neapolitan prostitute Filumena Marturano, for 25 years the mistress of the rich Don Domenico Soriano, finally entraps him into marriage. Like Volpone or Gianni Schicchi, she takes to her bed. She seems to be dying and Domenico consents to make her happy in her last moments. The ceremony performed, Filumena jumps spryly from her couch.

This event is the most ravishingly theatrical, and also the least obviously credible, in the play. It happens offstage; not merely offstage, but before curtain-rise. What we first see is the volcanically quarrelling couple, flanked by their respective servants. De Filippo, with the craft of either great cunning or great simplicity, has had it all ways. We are given the measure of Filumena's determination, and the play acquires the flavour of classic comedy while remaining a realistic drama.

To see Filumena's stratagem in action would be to become involved in the sheer joy of deception, and thus to get the wrong idea about both the play and its heroine. For the lady differs from her ancestors in Italian comedy; in no way is she in the game for fun, any more than in her earlier years she was on it for fun. Born in the slums she has been prosaically dependent first on prostitution, and now on household trickery, to keep her head above water.

It turns out that she has been fooling Domenico for years. She has three grown sons whom she has brought up on his money and without his knowledge; without theirs, come to that. Her revelation of this fact turns the play into a prolonged family reunion: a joyous event with doubtful, bitter undertones.

One of the boys is Domenico's; she refuses to say which on the grounds that, at this stage, she wants no favouritism. By the end of the play their marriage is confirmed, as we always knew it would be, and Domenico has agreed to stop asking questions. But it is impossible to believe that he will be able to keep this promise; we are given a happy ending with the certainty of breakers ahead.

You have been out of the theatre for some time before this hits you. We actually end with Filumena, for the first time in her life, crying: a conclusion which, the adaptors, Keith Waterhouse and Willis Hall, and the director, Franco Zeffirelli, probably make more sentimental than it need be. A British audience, though, can hardly help abetting them. For us, these people are refreshingly exotic, and de Filippo sunnier than he probably seems to his compatriots.

For the most part, Zeffirelli works a refined version of the magic he exercised in Saturday, Sunday, Monday. This show is less of a carnival: like his author, Zeffirelli acknowledges farce, but does not exploit it; he gives us this time (in a verismo set by Raimonda Gaetani) something more believable and more moving. I almost wrote 'more restrained,' but in fact the production lacks nothing in explosiveness; and in Joan Plowright and Colin Blakely it has two of the most resourcefully passionate players on the British stage.

Mr Blakely starts on a high note of badgered belligerence; his rage at moments lifts him off the floor. And he has the measure of the play's greatest comic scene: Domenico quizzing his three putative heirs to find out which of them--as a singer, perhaps, or a womaniser--takes after him, and pulling up a chair to get a good look at the likeliest suspect.

The throb in Joan Plowright's voice makes it difficult to believe that she has never wept, but her banked-up furies are tremendous. She turns self-pity into honest, cleansing anger, and single-mindedness into generosity. Patricia Hayes as her confidante gives her fierce and funny encouragement, and Larry Noble is a philosophically downtrodden manservant whose every gesture reduces to a shrug. The three sons are fetching in their contrasts, and even more in their unanimity. London could hardly have found a better antidote to power cuts.

*

James Robson's The Factory Birds (Warehouse) is more or less defined by its title. We are shown men and women, mostly young but ageing fast, having their humanity blunted (they make machine parts for cleaning out battery hen-coops, and if that sounds gratuitously symbolic, reflect that somebody must be doing it) and, for the most part, acquiescing in it. In the last consideration lies the author's originality--he differentiates. I don't know his history, but it is hard not to identify him with the goaded, sensitive boy who in the end makes the break his loud-mouthed mates have always talked about. The loudest of them all is a self-proclaimed Casanova; the escaper is a virgin. The second act centres on an off-stage rape; and sexism, as the emblem and product of a shuttered life, is most on the author's mind.

None of this is controversial; but then none of it is deniable. The play's obvious weakness is monotony, but it is necessary and stops short of tedium. The play survives, in Bill Alexander's production, on its authenticity and its acting. Enforced companionship always brings out the best in actors, and there is of course the added kick that these are from the RSC, and so presumably used to higher things; Roger Rees is the most spectacular breaker of new and greasier ground, but there are no flies on any of the cast.

*

Nor are there any, once you accept a convention in which everyone talks like Valentine Dyall, in The Fall of the House of Usher, which last week was visible at the Cottesloe. Steven Berkoff's style of rigid fantastication actually suits Poe better than it did Kafka.

Alas, the flesh cannot be made to creep indefinitely, as Mr Berkoff seems to recognise, since he makes abrupt descents to the conversational for the odd camp laugh. But the music and movement are as accomplished as ever. Mr Berkoff arrestingly gives Roderick Usher the sound of a mechanical winch in the last stages of disrepair, and Shelley Lee, in her anguished groping from beyond the grave, is a quite transfixing Usherette.
