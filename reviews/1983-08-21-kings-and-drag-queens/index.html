Aug 21, 1983

Kings And Drag Queens

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Kings and drag queens

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and New York.

WHEN critics write the regulation essay on what they did in the hols, they offer glowing accounts of companies encountered abroad of extraordinary vision, audacity, intellect and good manners. Sometimes they are taken up on this. So comes the Teatro Colletivo di Parma to the Riverside Studios in a season of very free Shakespeare. The Parma company offered a joky Macbeth set in a film-studio, the protagonists as narcissists; the highlight was the apparition of Banquo, like the head of John the Baptist on a platter. Some of the action was impenetrable, but most was imaginative and good-humoured. Their Henry IV (ends tonight) is more substantial. The audience sits on three sides, a privileged few at café tables, which the actors--eight of them--sometimes share. All the world's a tavern.

That is very much the angle. The court scenes are raced through. A man who comes on wearing a crown and a carpet must be the king. Presumably he is saying, `so shaken as we are, so wan with care.' He certainly looks careworn. Bad news is brought to him by maimed messengers crawling like biblical serpents.

Confusion descends for those without Italian (and, I gather, for those with it as well.) We become aware of motorbikes racing excitingly round the stage. That roaring fat man with his trousers down must be Falstaff. Certainly he has the presence for it. Watching him later, beaming in his braces, calling himself `Johnny,' we know him to be--what shall I say?--the real Marconi. That mounted tearaway has to be Prince Hal.

The roistering scenes are lovingly, riotously done, Falstaff's hearers almost choking on their spaghetti as they encourage him in his lies. Romantic pop songs back the revels; Falstaff gets rejected with Peachum's cynical song from `The Threepenny Opera.' All is as exciting and immediate as the travellers told. And it's a play; not just an essay on one.

But we don't have to feel inferior. Shakespeare in English and Shakespeare in foreign are different animals. Nobody cares if a translation is mucked about with. A British production seeking this degree of license would either have to play against the text to a degree fatiguing to both actors and audience, or paraphrase, which is an abomination. There are those rhythms, prose or verse, and we are stuck with them. They can take their revenge in strange ways.

*

Howard Davies's RSC production of Macbeth (Barbican) is as sober as the Italian is high-spirited, but it has a similarly disapproving view of its hero. Bob Peck's Macbeth is a soldier, and to be a soldier here is to be a thug. Those in the opening scene go on fighting long after the battle's lost . . . and won. (I learned that pause from this production's witches.) Mr Peck, dour and determined, implicitly denies Macbeth any scruples and the play any real development. The production puritanically denies him a visible ghost. A gibber at, but he hardly seems capable of imagining one for himself.

Despite the discrepancy between what he shows and what his lines say, Mr Peck speaks the verse superbly; he explores it, inhabits it, makes sense of it, never loses its shape. Vocally and physically, he has the power to occupy space. So do many of the others: David Waller, Pete Postlethwaite, Malcolm Storry make us feel Duncan's complacence, Banquo's suspicion, Macduff's grief, as fresh things.

There is an overpowering devil-porter (Geoffrey Freshwater), who dismisses comedy with a leer and a caper; and, in Sara Kestelman, a Lady Macbeth whose command is instant and whose crack-up is harrowing. There are also two percussionists who, playing Nigel Hess's score, seem to have usurped the witches' place as governors of the action. Chris Dyer's skeletal set is surly but shapely. If Mr Davies can bring himself to like his characters, he will be a fine Shakespeare director.

*

Terry Hands's production of another Elizabethan murder story, the anonymous Arden of Faversham (Pit), is admirable stuff, funny, gripping, persuasive. Everyone in it seems capable of mayhem, even Christopher Benjamin's Arden the victim, solid in a sombrero.

Jenny Agutter is a white-hot adulteress, though the love-scenes falter. John Bowe and David Bradley as the cut-throats (six tries all botched: a disgrace to an efficient Tudor profession) develop their brokers' men from `The Swan-Down Gloves.' A superb invention has their offstage voices circling the terrified servant Michael (Mark Rylance), who emerges as the most fully drawn character in the piece.

*

What did I do on my holidays? I failed to see La Cage aux Folles, which promises to be the most successful American musical in years. It opened at the Palace, New York's Palladium, tonight. I could not get into its try-out run in Boston, because there was not a seat to be had through press-agents or even authors. When booking opened in New York, there were queues round the block, and--a remarkable sign of confidence--the cast album was recorded two weeks before the first night. I went to the session, to get a sneak hearing of some of the songs, and to meet the director and composer.

When I arrived, a song called `A Little More Mascara' was in progress: a big celebratory number in which the singers, while descending a staircase, tells of how terrible everything feels until the make-up has been applied and the world can be faced again. A probable show-stopper, it bears the unmistakable composer's autograph of Jerry Herman, who also wrote those perennial matron-vehicles `Hello Dolly' and `Mame.' The only difference here is that the star is male; the character is a drag-queen. It represents a certain kind of musical number coming out.

Arthur Laurents, the director, says that he pulled `every trick in the book' on this number. A slight, outspoken man with his own successes as playwright and musical librettist, he speaks of the show, with its two middle-aged homosexual heroes, as `a real love story, which most musicals only pretend to be' and says that he liked the film, but did not admire it. He thought it condescending and sometimes camp: defining camp as `getting easy laughs from screaming queens,' and claiming to have eliminated it from the show.

I doubt, even so, if the show will prove radical enough for militant gays. Even if it only reflects an already changing climate (Laurents admits: `we are not doing anything devastatingly new') that is a kind of health.

I wonder how it will cope with the French provincial atmosphere of its original, and how music will affect its tight and traditional farce structure. (It was a play before it was a movie.) But I like the source material, and the people concerned, and I wish them well.

I did see a revival of Mame, with Angela Lansbury in her original role; it is machine-made, but she gives it strength and wit. At the opposite extreme of musical resurrection there was The Cradle Will Rock, a social-protest expressionist musical of the Thirties. (Stand-out song: `Joe Worker Gets Gypped.') It is a museum-piece that gets up out of its case and lives: New Half Moon, this is for you.

My holiday discovery I almost blush to reveal. Years after everyone else, I learned to love Sam Shepard. He has traditionally been praised for his presentation of American archetypes. Fool for Love--a mysterious comedy set in the seediest of last-chance motels and featuring violence, memory, jealousy, incest and more violence--shows that he actually works outwards from passion. The play is as self-contained and as unashamed as a vintage Pinter.

A couple of words for the lyricist Ira Gershwin, who died last week: they are `sly' and `charming.'
