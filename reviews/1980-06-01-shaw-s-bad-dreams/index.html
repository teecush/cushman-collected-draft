Jun 1, 1980

Shaw's Bad Dreams

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Shaw's bad dreams

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on `John Bull's Other Island.'

NOBODY would ever describe Shaw as self-effacing, but it is only in recent years that we have begun to look in his plays for evidence of personal pain or personal guilt.

`The Philanderer' gained in interest a couple of years ago when we saw it as the confession of a womaniser for ever keeping fascinated tabs on himself. In John Bull's Other Island (Greenwich) the authorial figure is Larry Doyle, an Irishman who fled his native country after adolescence and made first for America and then for London, where he achieved success as a civil engineer in partnership with a hearty, sentimental and fearsomely energetic Englishman named Tom Broadbent. The play narrates Doyle's return to Ireland after an eighteen-year absence, as escort to Broadbent who wants effectively to buy up Larry's home village of Rosscullen. He succeeds; of course; and while he is at it, he also acquires Larry's former sweetheart.

If you want to interest an Irishman in Ireland, Larry complains, you've got to call the unfortunate island Kathleen ni Houlihan and pretend she's a little old woman. Shaw's method of interesting the English public in Ireland was to make her an attractive woman in her thirties and call her Nora Reilly. What Nora comes to feel for Broadbent is not so much love as incredulous fascination. Her compatriots have their own variation on her response. They adopt Broadbent as their parliamentary candidate. Larry, having declined the honour, though they find both his personality and his political views puzzling. The year is 1904, he is a Gladstonian Liberal, and the most vociferous Home Ruler among them.

What rivets the attention is Larry's anguished, sardonic impotence as his English friend (to whom he is devoted) comes into what should have been his own inheritance: one from which he is debarred from fighting by his own exiled nature. Larry is a man who sees through everything and so can do nothing; and he must have embodied some of Shaw's own worst dreams of himself. This was, after all, the only play in which he dared to confront himself as an Irishman.

The play has a curious, straggling construction: dropping either its romantic or political theme for an act at a time, but tying them together when we realise that Broadbent's pursuit of Nora and his attempt to bring progress--defined as a hotel with golf-links--to Rosscullen have in fact been identical. The last scene confronts the two engineers with Peter Keegan, the ex-priest who believes the world, since it is 'very clearly a place of torment and penance,' to be hell. His pessimism is a bracing tonic among the well-meaning fools who, here as elsewhere, people Shaw's universe; he is possibly the most persuasive of Shavian sages. (Captain Shotover of `Heartbreak House' seems a pretender beside him). His indictment of the 'efficient' methods that will ruin everyone except the projectors themselves sounds unanswerable. And what counters it is not argument, but Broadbent's approval. (`Too true, Mr Keegan, only too true. And most eloquently put') which reduces the whole matter to one of words, words, words.

That, if you like, was Shaw's vice; and in some plays the demonstration that one point of view is as convincing as another is only an infuriating, gentlemanly game. Here it is the base of a dramatic system, maybe even of a philosophic one. Keegan's final vision of heaven, `the dream of a madman,' is the kind of Shavian writing one is officially meant to find embarrassing; in fact it is haunting, and the more impressive in Alan Strachan's production for being delivered from an almost bare stage.

My main memory of Mr Strachan's previous production of the play at the Mermaid is of P. G. Stephens's quietly convinced exposition of those closing moments. He repeats the performance at Greenwich and the quality remains, though it was often obscured on the first night by uncertainties of phrasing and even of volume. Mr Strachan's current direction is uneven, at its best in the opening scene in London: a warmly exasperated duel between Christopher Benjamin's eternally boyish Broadbent and Tony Doyle's splendidly incisive Larry.

When the scene moves across the Channel, character acting, of varying quality, takes place, conspiring (with the author's connivance) to turn satire of stock comedy into the thing itself: honourable exceptions are Sorcha Cusack's Nora and Chris Gannon's Matt Haffigan, a small man in every sense, concerned that reform has gone quite far enough and had better stop lest other people have things easier than he did. Some of the electioneering scenes are impenetrably dated, but most confirm the impression that anything written about Ireland always sounds topical.

*

David Pownall's Livingstone and Sechele (Lyric Studio, Hammersmith) is latter-day Shavianising lacking the transforming features: witty without craziness, intelligent without communicated involvement. Livingstone, you know about; Sechele turns out to be his first convert among African chiefs; a conquest achieved only after years of endeavour, and then on dubious grounds. What Sechele really wants is (a) protection from the encroaching Boers and (b) rain. For this he is willing to embrace Christianity and give up most of his wives, one of whom is a constant--and, in Anni Domingo's performance, quite understandable--source of temptation.

Mr Pownall writes with arctic playfulness; his Sechele is a smooth-tongued sophisticate who addresses Livingstone with jewelled endearments--a vein of fun admirably mined in Joe Marcel's performance. Equally good are Peter Kelly's tetchy David and Jennifer Piercey as his understanding spouse. At one point, most decorously staged, both couples make love on opposite sides of the stage, all climaxing simultaneously and tempting reviewers into the forlorn hope of coming up with some new joke about the missionary position.

*

Theatermanufaktur Berlin completed their season at the Old Vic with Johannes Faustus, written by Brecht's musical collaborator Hanns Eisler, and finally sending its hero to perdition for devoting his magic powers to entertaining the nobility instead of assisting the peasants: following in fact in the footsteps of the proto-traitor Luther. To invoke eternal damnation as a penalty for being on the wrong side in the class war seems to me to conflate two irreconcilable myths; but Theatermanufaktur manage to draw on both traditions with gusto and imagination.

The preview of eternal torments to which Faustus is treated before the devils come for him is especially effective; a tattered banner is drawn aside, then replaced, while behind it the actors group themselves into some new tableau of torment. The unstressed joy they take in their own ability to frighten is typical of the production, which purports to be done by travelling players. So, come to think of it, it is.
