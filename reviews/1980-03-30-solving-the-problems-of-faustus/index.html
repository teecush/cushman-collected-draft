Mar 30, 1980

Solving The Problems Of Faustus

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Solving the problems of Faustus

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London, Leicester and Sheffield.

IT is plain from the opening of Dr Faustus (transferred from the Lyric Studio, Hammersmith, to the Fortune) that the director, Christopher Fettes, has found a solution to the play.

A couple of soberly-clad young men sit studiously at a long table. A third enters, and delivers the prologue, coolly and a little insolently, with none of the thunderous rhetoric usually deemed appropriate for getting a Marlowe tragedy off the ground. Indeed, in generally accepted terms, this production never gets off the ground at all; it remains tethered to that table and to its attendant furniture: stools and a couple of forms.

When the two seated figures open their mouths they turn out to be, not Faustus's fellow scholars, but his Good and Bad Angel. Faced with the problem of bringing medieval iconography on stage the production merely sidesteps it, much as Jonathan Miller did years ago when presented with the supernatural extravagances of The Tempest. The effect--playing off what we get against what we expect--is very powerful. When spectacle is unavoidable--as in the show of the Seven Deadly Sins--it is dimly glimpsed behind a gauze.

Mr Fettes plays a pretty full text, including a generous selection of the prose scenes in which servants and ostlers try their own luck with Faustus's magic books. James Aubrey's Faustus is, in essence, the same kind of bemused simpleton, with the difference that he also happens to be an academic whizzkid. Mr Aubrey makes very believable the character's premature disillusion with conventional success, as well as the desperate shallowness that lets him convince himself--even when confronted by a spirit as palpably damned as Patrick Magee's sepulchral Mephistophilis--that there can be no hell.

Mr Magee is inspired casting; Mr Aubrey goes well beyond his previous range (mainly slim, disintegrating young men) to discover some authentic terror for the last scene. 'I'll leap up to my God' is an actual jump, horizontal across the table with an upward-stretching arm. But his vocal colouring is limited, and draws uncomfortable attention to the monotony of the play's middle. Whatever Faustus did with his 24 years of power would, in the end, seem inconsequential; but this fact is of little comfort to the audience while actually watching it.

Point by point, Mr Fettes has solved every one of the play's problems, but, though he expands for the scenes of Papal charade, all his answers have the same flavour; and though they may well have seemed inevitable in the original studio staging, on a proscenium stage--even one as small as this--they become mannered.

At the beginning one is continually startled by imaginative invention: by, for example, the presentation of Faustus's tutor Cornelius as a blind man in a wheelchair, whose 'the spirits tell me' is magnificently eerie. When Faustus calls for a wife he gets a husband-beating harridan, initiating a series of homosexual jokes on the ingenuous hero's ideas of pleasure.

There are no women in the cast of eight; and Helen, who finally damns Faustus, is a boy--a tribute less to Elizabethan stage convention than to what the production, probably correctly, takes to be the author's mocking, self-tormenting spirit. (We may want to believe that he dissociated himself from the orthodox judgment of the last lines, but there is no evidence for it.) Mr Fettes's production is the equal of John Barton's a few years ago, which means I do not expect to see it bettered; but it still disappointed me.

*

John Byrne's Threads (Hampstead) reintroduces us to the characters of The Slab Boys (nearly all played by the same actors, an impressive blow for continuity in these fractured times) and lets us accompany them to the works dance. The dialogue is as uncompromisingly, not to say impenetrably, Glaswegian as ever--especially in the first act, set in the ladies and gents cloakrooms, where, naturally, sexual segregation reigns. The linguistic barriers go down quite noticeably in the second, when the sexes mingle.

Either my ears were growing keener or Mr Byrne is making a point about the adoption of disguise (i.e. standard English) in social situations. Anyway the wit that I, encouraged by the spirit of the acting, had taken for granted in the first half, turned out really to exist in the second, as did a certain formality. Mr Byrne's characters soliloquise, none more poignantly than the girl-starved Hector, frustrated by the unaccountability of women: 'One minute I'm the bees' knees--next, I'm lower than a knat's ankle-socks.' He is actually the victim--as are the audience, to a certain extent--of the fact that each of Mr Byrne's people has a separate, and ruthless, strategy for survival.

The result is 10 separate lines of action. Pat Doyle's Hector vies for first claim on our sympathy with Robbie Coltrane's fat jokes, who has learned the ropes but still ties himself in knots, and with Claire Nielson's spinster, recalling a wartime meeting with Clark Gable in terms that reaffirm Mr Byrne's status as a punster: 'Rhett Butler in khaki, and me scarlet with embarrassment.'

*

Graham Greene's For Whom the Bell Chimes (Haymarket Studio, Leicester) is meant to unfold with the enchanting craziness of a Magritte tableau--as denoted both by the clinical set and by the painting of a snatch of sky that hangs crooked on one of its walls. There are even two characters who exchange a bowler hat; one a pearl-grey exquisite, the other a down-and-out ostensibly collecting for charity.

Some of the dialogue concerning the children whom he claims to represent (what an easy life they have, no responsibilities) is engagingly heartless. But the play winds down; the action becomes whimsically improvisational without (unlike Tom Stoppard's After Magritte or the best of Orton) any logical base to which to return.

The acting, too, is uneven, though Roy Macready's collector is fine, as is Derek Smith's fire-breathing colonel (resembling his brother-officer in Peter Shaffer's Black Comedy). Mr Smith is also seen to effect in Mr Greene's fairly engaging curtain-raiser, Yes and No, in which he impersonates a director instructing an actor in the difference between a Pinter pause and a Pinteresque pause. Privett being the author whom they are trying jointly to interpret.

*

Stephen Poliakoff's The Summer Party was done, by contrast, on the main stage of the Sheffield Crucible, a gamble rewarded by attendances if not by the play itself. It might actually have moved faster in a studio. As it is, a reasonably suggestive ending--hinging on the difference between the fake magic of an infant Uri Geller and the more respectable variety invoked by a chief constable to calm a rock-concert mob--took a wearying time to arrive.

Mr Poliakoff has assembled standard characters from the pop and law-and-order worlds, without giving them anything much to do. Only Dexter Fletcher, as the child, acted with a terrifying assurance that seemed in itself to justify the author's worst nightmares.
