Jan 12, 1975

Billy Gets The Treatment

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Billy gets the treatment

EVERY collector of American show records knows that the best original cast LPs are made by CBS, and are generally produced by Goddard Lieberson. He has been imported to supervise the recording of Billy (CBS 70133), and the resulting album is as sleek as the Drury Lane original.

There is, however, nothing aurally remarkable about Michael Crawford's performance in the title role and John Barry's tunes are repetitive without being compulsive. The most pleasing is 'Any Minute Now,' a duet for two of Billy's birds which can turn wistful or brassy depending on which lady is singing. Don Black's lyrics survive respectably, though invention often gives way to trickiness. A couplet like 'I'll hang my hat in every part of the atlas / Most of the time I will be hopelessly hatless' is not worth the trouble it takes to sing.

Billy's West End competitor The Good Companions (EMI EMC 3042) is ramshackle by comparison. All the same it wins. John Mills and Judi Dench are authentic stars; talking or singing, Miss Dench captivates the moment she opens her mouth. Andre Previn's music is sometimes undistinguished, but never formless; the ballad Dance of Life distils an original, complex melancholy, and the production number A Little Travelling Music racily underlines some of Johnny Mercer's best syllabic juggling.

Mr Mercer is past his prime as a lyricist (though not as a singer, as two LPs recorded in London this year bear eloquent witness); he has a shaky grasp of English, as opposed to American, locutions, and he has never really been a theatrical writer. He is the genius of the one-shot song. In this show he is liable to move from inspiration to cliche within a single song. But he retains the lyricist's supreme gifts: a delight in the flavour of words and the ability to make them ride the music. I doubt if there is a rhythmically misplaced word in the score. The rhyming tag at the end of a song ('Agitate your tempo, professor / I taught the score to your predecessor') is unmistakable Mercer.

A consideration of his work leads us backwards, to where most of the best show records come from. The Mermaid's Cole has been recorded on two discs (RCA LRL2 5054), which preserve most of the songs and, less appealingly, some of the linking material in which the compilers attempt to father on Cole Porter their own nudgingly patronising view of Broadway and Hollywood. In the theatre the show is delightful, but when taken home most of the performers are audibly trying too hard; the exception is Kenneth Nelson, who expresses all the defiance of I Happen to Like New York and even made me listen afresh to Night and Day. Otherwise I shall treasure the album for the ensembles, for Ken Moule's tingling arrangements, and for nine or ten songs elsewhere unobtainable.

Porter has done better than any other composer out of the renewed interest in classic show songs. Though successive explorations of his neglected work have shown his musical range to be far wider than has been supposed -- and I rate him far higher now than I would have done five years ago -- his lyrics, even when impassioned, all speak with the same dryly ingenious voice. (When he ventures into Shakespearia, in Brush Up Your Shakespeare, the effect is ventriloquial, we cannot see the Manhattan sophisticate's lips moving.) His songs may belong to the supper-club to which no composer has bequeathed a larger catalogue of viable material rather than to the theatre.

More of it can be sampled on another double album, Cole Porter in London (World Records SHB 26), which collects original cast recordings of Porter shows (and of individual Porter songs included in revues) performed in London between 1919 and 1951. The gift inside the package is Gertrude Lawrence with her classic The Physician and I, neglected but rousingly cynical exercise called It's Bad for Me.

Miss Lawrence turns up on a companion record, George Gershwin in London, as does Claude Hulbert, rendering Clap Yo' Hands much as Bertie Wooster might have sung it in his bath; which -- since P. G. Wodehouse wrote the book for this as for dozens of other musicals in the twenties -- is bizarrely appropriate.

An astute A & R man can make a show LP an enjoyable entity, but films generally sound scrappy. This is particularly the case with the MGM double album of That's Entertainment (MGM 2659 033), a film which was scrappy to begin with, because of the producer's reluctance to stay with any number long enough for us to see it build. At full stretch On the Atchison, Topeka and the Santa Fe is irresistible; cut down it means little, and on this record virtually nothing. Far more satisfying are the soundtrack recordings of four Astaire-Rogers pictures: Swing Time and The Gay Divorce (EMI EMTC 101), Top Hat and Shall We Dance? (EMTC 102). These find space even for the RKO pipes (endearing like a distress signal) at the start of each movie, as well as for significant dialogue (mostly Ginger telling Fred that he'll never learn to dance). If you just want to hear the songs (Porter, Gershwin, Kern, Berlin), there are better versions available, including studio performances by Astaire himself. But as nostalgia objects these discs are irresistible and though you may weary of the sound of tapping feet in current versions of the continental so overwhelming.
