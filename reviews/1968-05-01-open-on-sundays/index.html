May 1, 1968

Open on Sundays

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

Open on Sundays

Ever alert for a chance to offer really constructive criticism, I would like to point out to would-be patrons the salient fact about Open on Sundays, namely that it isn't. I can now get round to explaining why, if you are a would-be patron, Sunday is the day to choose.

An actor I know who was appearing in an evening of folksy fun and not enjoying it very much was a little dashed after one performance by an enthusiastic spectator who informed him that it was just what she'd always imagined Jewish humour to be. The actor replied ruefully that he'd always assumed Jewish humour was funnier.

This crystallises my reaction to Open on Sundays, which is set in a timeless community of no fixed abode, where a few kopeks will buy you a tin of Heinz tomato soup. Waste no time on wondering whether you are in pre-1914 Odessa or the pre-1939 East End; the authors see no essential difference and have worked in guest appearances for Moses, Spinoza, Dai Francis and others. Groucho Marx even gets to heckle his uncle Karl in stray lines from his movies whose delivery here should kill the legend that he owed everything to his writers. The Hitler-Chaplin bit also turns up, done in the old flicker-flashback style.

Like much of the show, this sequence is briskly staged and excellently lit, and, to the extent that the entire cast looks unabashedly and unforcedly involved throughout, the group-improvisation method has obviously worked. They also look directed; Ron Pember always manages to keep attention focused on the right spot. But the goings-on in that spot hardly justify him; let a bunch of actors improvise on the general theme of how colourful and lovable they are, and astringent entertainment will not result. A one-act play by Michael Landy lies buried somewhere; what it can have been like is anybody's guess, and mine are all doleful.

Things happen in moderate quantity, but their running-order could easily be reversed, which is a working definition of bad dramaturgy. Between whiles, the actors sing, better than most of them act. The songs are standard traditional and have an unnerving habit of resembling, if you can imagine such a thing, pastiche Lionel Bart. The jokes are trad, too, and as such well below par.

The show's most crippling limitations are inherent in its premise. The Anglo-American theatre has benefited enormously from Jewish talent, but Yiddishness is not of itself enough. An excruciatingly protracted and sentimental anecdote about whether to put grandfather in the old folks' home seems to labour under the impression that this is a situation whose poignancy is peculiarly Jewish; it could happen anywhere and is of no dramatic moment unless the participants are individualised. Nor can I take seriously a self-styled “full-length experience of Jewish life and humour” which takes no account of assimilation, the state of Israel, or any Gentiles other than Hitler and an unspecified Tsar, and which equates the ghetto with the Garden of Eden. Jews are supposed to be the worst anti-Semites and I cannot forgive this production for the feelings it aroused in me.
