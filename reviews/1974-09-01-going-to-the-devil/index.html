Sep 1, 1974

Going To The Devil

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Going to the devil

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN reports from the Edinburgh Festival

MARLOWE'S Doctor Faustus is a rather shifty play, but if you can manage to look it straight in the eye you may be able to obtain from it a straight and extremely orthodox answer to the old question about the profits accruing to those who gain the world and lose their souls. The answer is, of course, that it profiteth them not at all.

This at least is the moral punched out at the end of the play: "Faustus is dead, regard his hellish fall" and do not be so impious as to seek forbidden knowledge. A dramatist committed to this treatment of the legend faces a tricky problem, not unlike that confronting Faustus himself: how to while away the time between initial commitment to the devil and ultimate damnation? It is a problem which Marlowe notoriously failed to solve.

The middle sections of the play are traditionally, and quite legitimately, dismissed as buffoonery. They show Faustus exercising his magic powers to ingratiate himself with neighbouring potentates or to play practical jokes, or both. It seems, on the face of it, rather a poor price to get for one's soul; but there is a self-congratulatory note about the writing of these scenes, thin as they are, which prevents one from assuming that this is necessarily what Marlowe meant.

Besides, we are told--though inevitably not shown--that Faustus also engaged in headier pastimes, driving through the heavens in dragon-drawn chariots. The higher they ride, the further they fall; and when the end is damnation (whether envisaged as an eternity of roasting, or more subtly as permanent exile from grace--again the play is undecided) the most exalted fancies are liable to look like meaningless pranks. Whatever Faustus does with the middle of his life, or Marlowe with the middle of his play, the upshot would be the same, but this thought is not much consolation to us while we are watching, even if we can believe that the play has whole-heartedly accepted it.

John Barton, directing his own adaptation at the Lyceum (and this week at the Aldwych), has added his own complications. He employs the play's diabolic trinity--Lucifer, Beelzebub and Mephistophilis--to speak the Chorus of the original text; in their mouths its traditional exhortations and moralisings sound decidedly wry. The intention may have been only to provide an ironic frisson; but if taken seriously it suggests that the whole moral system which Faustus transgresses is diabolically controlled.

But on this matter, as on much else, the play is inconsistent; and the most satisfying passages are certainly those which assume that the hero's fall is essentially his own work.

Mr Barton--himself no marvel of consistency on this occasion--reinforces this view with his presentation of the Good and Bad Angels who give explicit utterance to the protagonist's internal struggle. They are reduced to a couple of silent glove-puppets; Faustus himself appropriates their lines. The resulting dialectic puts an added edge on Ian McKellen's performance, which is pretty well barbed to begin with; profiting from the prologue's reference to his parents as "base of stock," Mr McKellen presents a thrusting arriviste scholar whom ambition has propelled to the top of every faculty in Wittenberg, and who now finds himself satiated and a little disgusted. He has perforce gone short on sensual pleasures, and it is for these as much as anything that he turns to the devil. In fact they do nothing to alleviate his distemper; his increasing sourness becomes the binding thread of the production. Only at the end is he seriously disappointing, employing vocal tricks which include the rendition of "O, I'll leap up to my God" as a practicable bright idea.

Emrys James adds a chilling imperturbable Mephistophilis to his fine line of theatrical fiends: there is a remarkable sureness about his colloquies with Mr McKellen. For this, perhaps, Mr Barton may be held responsible; a surpassing feeling for the space around, rather than between, Elizabethan words has generally distinguished his Shakespeare productions, and it is now obvious that he could help us all to deal with the other dramatists of the period.

The supporting playing--though Denis Holmes and Terence Wilton speak sturdily for the lower orders--is generally dull. There is a far more even acting level in the Actors' Company production of Euripides's The Bacchae (Assembly Hall), though its peaks are a good deal less exalted. The company deserve our thanks for letting us hear William Arrowsmith's tersely exciting translation (one of the brilliant Chicago versions which have languished on the shelves while our directors have sported with inferior models) whose economy is echoed in Edward Petherbridge's direction.

The bones of the play are there but they remain bare; the evening is gripping but never disturbing. On two occasions the production bit into my nerve-ends: when Charles Kay recounted the butchery of Pentheus (they played with scraps of his body) and when Sheila Burrell as Agave cradling her son's head was wrenched out of frenzy into a recognition of ultimate horror: "I feel as though my mind was somehow changing." The production works hard at establishing the bi-sexual tension between Dionysus and Pentheus, but the effort is more notable than the result.

Robin Ellis's Pentheus is gallant, but he only realises the full possibilities of drag in the afterpiece, a mimed version of Jack and the Beanstalk in which he plays Dame, walking with a pained and careful delicacy as if suffering from mild indigestion. This entertainment, sometimes coy, often delightful, features juggling, home musicianship, and a wholly entrancing interlude of shadow-puppetry, culminating in the cutting off of the giant's head. (Decapitation is the evening's unifying theme.)

This has been a festival full of notable acting: Mr Kay's Tartuffe, Mr McKellen's Faustus, and matching if not overtopping them Sven Wollter's electrically temperamental portrayal of Strindberg's Gustav III, presented by the Gothenburg City Theatre. On the fringe there has been Fulton Mackay's delicate double performance in McGonagall and the Murder at the Pool; John Spurling's study of two men differently obsessed by the image of Queen Victoria was a little infirm in establishing its premise and occasionally dilatory in developing it, but haunting and ingenious none the less. Mr Spurling has the expected fun with McGonagall's verse-forms; though he depends far less on pastiche than does Richard Crane in The Quest, presented by the Bradford University Group at Lauriston Hall.

I cannot see that The Quest reverberates much beyond the Arthurian myths of which it treats; but it approaches them with great buoyancy, and builds them into a remarkably stable structure. It is given a superb production by Chris Parr, the audience seated in two opposing ranks, as if watching a tournament: the best battle I have seen on any stage.
