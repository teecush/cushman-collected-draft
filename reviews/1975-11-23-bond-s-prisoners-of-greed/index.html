Nov 23, 1975

Bond's Prisoners Of Greed

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Bond's prisoners of greed

EDWARD BOND'S The Fool (Royal Court) begins with a mummers' play. A group of East Anglian villagers perform outside the local lord's door: very imposing as designed by William Dudley and apparently free-standing. The gentry applaud politely. It seems a reasonably happy, peaceful scene--the only such scene, it turns out, in the entire play.

The Napoleonic Wars have just ended, and we are in at the death of agricultural England. The parson, in attendance on the squire, preaches an impromptu sermon on the new industrial age. "An iron age," he calls it, and the ambiguous phrase gives promise of a complex evening. The parson talks of working for the common good, we sit back and wait for exploitation.

Two scenes on, the mummers have turned rioters. Once again they come up against the parson, this time to strip him, partly for the loot that adorns his body and partly so that they can cry "Under that he's same as us." When he is raked, though, they decide that this is not quite true. His skin, white and uncalloused, seems another mask it hides. "Where you stole that flesh, boy? Your flesh is stolen goods"; the obvious next step is to flay him. This is prevented, but the violent (and in context irresistible) logic of Mr Bond's writing makes it a real momentary possibility. Meanwhile the parson whimpers that he is a powerless old man; and that's true, too.

His attackers are arrested and condemned to death. Then their sentences are commuted to life imprisonment. This grisly mercy sets them laughing in thankful joy: a powerful device that survives its own awareness of how powerful it is.

While in prison they are visited by a friend. We have glimpsed him before, in the mummers' show. It now transpires--nearly half-way through the play--that he is a poet and also ("scribblin' come to summat") that he has found a publisher and is off to London. In fact he is the fool of the title: John Clare, whom the last scene will find in the madhouse.

Part of his folly seems at first to be lovesickness; he has become smitten with a maid at the squire's house. He misses the stripping of the parson having rushed off in search of her, but she returns in time not merely to witness it, but to make off with much of the booty: the others are too busy with the ritual of humiliation to notice. But "Mary" remains a presence in his poems; his real wife he calls her when questioned by his fans--not an idea that commends itself to his legal wife, who is tormented besides by the painful realisation that poetry brings in even less money than labouring. She nags him at length, but with some virtuosity: "Sleep on your own--you have the pleasure of getting kids, you have the worry of feeding them." She has throughout this knack of yoking images: granting Clare his right to a drink, she defines the question as one of "letting your glass empty afore your purse."

Bridget Turner makes the most of such lines as she does of the character's whole progress--from brisk girl to stony shrew to stranger, visiting her husband just once in the asylum because someone has paid her fare. Some other actors are equally adept with good material: John Normington with the quaverings of the clergyman, Bill Fraser with the complacencies of a poetaster admiral, asserting that some of Clare's amorous verses are too warm ever for the bedroom, "and I've been round the world." The best of Mr Bond's writing has matchless stage presence; his carved prose must be the envy and the goal of half the playwrights in Britain.

For all that, the play halts; nothing binds it. A gamekeeper early declares the need for "keepers of people," anticipating Clare's incarceration by the best part of two acts. The lord, mellowed, or perhaps simply tired by the last scene, notes that his son has fulfilled the parson's prophecy: he is "in love with his factories." But these are literary felicities; they do not amount to a theatrical structure.

The play is meant, I think, as a panoramic indictment of greed; apart from Clare himself, all the characters are, with varying degrees of volition, its prisoner. Mary searching for silver while Clare searches for her, the villagers stripping the parson, the gentry stripping the villagers. On the fringes are prizefighters breaking themselves down for money; off-stage are Clare's publishers unloading on him his unsold books.

The protagonist of Mr Bond's last play Bingo was an earlier poet, Shakespeare, depicted as the victim of his own acquiescence in the rise of capitalism. Clare is a victim, period, and easy to sentimentalise. Lunatic, lover and poet, peasant and social critic: he has something for everyone. But he is too slim a character (and too tardily developed) to unite the action. So the play--which is considerably more varied than Bingo--appears even more static. Nor is it much activated by the grey performance of Tom Courtenay, cast fatally to sensitive type; or by Peter Gill's production in which the muttering subfusc style falsely fathered on Brecht--and now deemed suitable for all plays set in the past or in changing locales or both--begins to approach self-parody.

*

I thought we had done for the Theatre at New End, but no, here we trudge again to the playhouse opposite the hospital (and converted from a mortuary). Trudging is the mode on stage as well; hopeful new management or not, David Edgar's Death Story is rather less interesting than the Anouilh Electra play with which the old gang kicked off. This one too retells an old story, Romeo and Juliet, divided within a modern city in which the Montagues are craftsmen and the Capulets financiers. An alien military force tries cynically to keep them off the streets; Mr Edgar must mean us to think of Ulster, but he hardly explores the parallel (and a modern town disrupted just by two families is not very credible).

His best moments are sour jokes on Shakespeare: Juliet writhing in an exhibitionist nightmare while waiting for Romeo, Romeo impotent on the night; it is probably healthy to be reminded that the two had imperfect bodies as well as perfect voices. Mr Edgar manages a good Mercutio of suicidal impulse (well played by Terry Palmer) and winds up with a thrice-repeated version of the lovers' suicide to show us how easy it has always been for us to lap up personal tragedy instead of facing the real truth about sectarian warfare. I trust that makes you feel guilty.

*

Ronnie Scott's Jazz Haven in Soho has been temporarily captured for the theatre in the early evening, through the agency of the Sadista Sisters, four female persons who sing very well--and act less adroitly--pungent little rock-songs and sketches about feminine stereotypes. Particularly arresting is a stripper who turns out to be heavily anaphrodisiacally pregnant. The attitude towards men (equally trapped by stereotypes) is altogether less helpful than seems to have been intended.

The Greenwich revival of Coward's Fallen Angels is frothy, neatly set and over before you know it. This particularly applies to the two heroines' famous scene of alcoholic reminiscence; if you wait for the scandal, you wait all night. Eileen Atkins and Judy Parfitt do a conscientious job (Miss Atkins in particular is more fetching than she has been in ages, and more feathery than I would ever have imagined), but conscience, alas, cannot make Cowards of us all. Joyce Grant, at least, has no truck with it, turning what was written as modest scene-stealing into grand larceny. I missed this production's first night by going to watch Charles Pierce working over the queens of Hollywood; somehow I feel the two experiences might have been profitably and entertainingly combined.
