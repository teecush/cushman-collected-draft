Apr 26, 1988

Demon Drive

Robert Cushman · The Guardian

Demon drive

The star of Ziegfeld has the right training for his challenging part. Robert Cushman weighs him up

FOR ME, Len Cariou will always be a homicidal maniac. That is not a personal aspersion. It means I am haunted by his New York performance as Sweeney Todd in the Stephen Sondheim musical. Sondheim's demon barber is a man much wronged for whom revenge is the force that drives him to slit his customers' throats. Cariou's feeling on approaching the role was "Thank God I've done Lear."

He is about the only actor whose career alternates between leads in Broadway musicals and major classical roles, usually at repertory theatres in America or in his native Canada. Florenz Ziegfeld, the role that brings him to London for the first time, is not exactly in the King Lear or even the Sweeney Todd class. All the same, it is quite a weight to carry.

To begin with, the musical Ziegfeld has cost about £4 million to put on at the Palladium, and to be the centrepiece of something so expensive is a responsibility.

Cariou, in the role of Broadway's legendary impresario, is on stage almost throughout. The show purports to be Ziegfeld's autobiography, his story as he would have told it had it been one of his own shows, festooned with showgirls and sequins.

Of course nobody's life really resembles a spectacular revue, and in the end reality catches up with him. In Cariou's words, "He's told, 'OK, you've done all right, you've more or less stuck to the truth, but that's enough.' He's interwoven so intricately with the show, it's like reading your own obituary."

Ziegfeld was obsessive, getting through money and mistresses as relentlessly as Sweeney got through throats. It's the girls rather than the gelt that the show focuses on. We see Flo with two wives, two floozies and a secretary: a representative sample.

Along the way Cariou gets to sing a lot of standard songs: from A Pretty Girl Is Like a Melody, which historically has very strong Ziegfeld connections, to I'm Always Chasing Rainbows, which has no Ziegfeld connections whatsoever.

Singing is where it all began for Cariou: "I was born a singer. My mother was a singer. The Irish side of my family were all musically involved."

He started in the musical theatre but quickly went legitimate, somewhat aided by geography. He grew up in Winnipeg, where, in the late 1950s, a brilliant Hungarian-born director, John Hirsch, was starting Canada's first major regional playhouse -- the Manitoba Theatre Centre. Hirsch let him play his first Shakespeares, Petruchio and Caliban, but also advised him, "Don't ever give up musicals."

In the early Sixties Cariou did three seasons at Stratford, Ontario, but found himself "pretty low on the totem-pole", unlikely to get the leads he coveted. Stratford's then director, Michael Langham, whom Cariou now rates "far and away the best Shakespearean director alive", told him: "There's no way you can play Prince Hal."

But Stratford also had an operatic arm and Cariou was able to spend a pleasant season ("the first time in my career I had the leisure to play golf") appearing in Mahagonny.

From there followed an invitation to cross the border to act at the Guthrie Theatre in Minneapolis; he played Orestes in The Oresteia for Tyrone Guthrie himself. Since 1966 his career has been US-based.

He was at Stratford, Connecticut playing Henry V when the call came to audition for the musical Applause, a vehicle for Lauren Bacall. He had a conventional leading-man role and displayed not only personality but one of the most pleasant voices on Broadway. He left after 13 months to return to rep. "Betty Bacall couldn't understand it. She said, 'Why are you leaving this job?' "

Hal Prince, after seeing Applause, told Cariou: "You're the best leading man I've ever seen." That was in 1969. In 1973 Prince got round to honouring that opinion and cast him in the male lead in Sondheim's A Little Night Music. From that followed Sweeney Todd. "Hal told me it was written with me in mind. I don't know if Stephen would agree."

Whatever the case, Cariou sang and acted it with a fierce lucidity that allowed the passion and fury and sour wit of the score to shine as if through clear glass. I thought it was a great tragic performance.

He has since returned to Stratford, Ontario as a star, playing a Coriolanus stronger on blunt anger than patrician pride. He also directed his old home theatre in Winnipeg for a season, but found his energies diverted from administration into a couple of whacking leading roles.

He wants to go on directing occasionally, but running theatres for the time being is out. "I'm an acting animal."

Ziegfeld opens at the London Palladium tonight.
