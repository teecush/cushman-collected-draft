Oct 16, 1983

Faust And Furious

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Faust and furious

Theatre

by Robert Cushman

It took me a long time to appreciate Ralph Richardson. In my schooldays he struck me as blind and remote. From a knighted classicist I wanted passion and/or protean transformation: qualities I got, in various degrees, from Olivier, Wolfit, Guinness and Redgrave.

Then I heard him, on a record, reading Blake. This was one genius illuminating another, and it was the poet who lit up the actor, showed him to be great. When Richardson inquired, "little lamb, who made thee," he was at once childlike and inexorable. It was the voice, though it took me years to realise it, of the King Lear he never played, certainly the Lear of the madness and the reconciliation. Can you imagine what that performance would have been like? We would have cried on our way into the theatre. He, though, would not have cried.

I have one other fugitive memory, of his performance in Dr Zhivago, as Zhivago's father-in-law. Told of the murder of the Tsar, he exclaimed (I think), "Oh, that's a savage deed." Any other actor would have made that line sound stilted. He also made it sound inevitable. You got the impression -- a recurring one with him -- that he had written it himself.

On stage I remember a wholly unsentimental Shylock, in a duff West End production, and his emergence over the past 10 years -- at benefits, award-ceremonies and chat-shows -- as a consummate stand-up comedian. He had, we thought, to be putting us on; but the mask never dropped. Maybe it had stuck to the face.

The same dignified outrageousness characterised all his recent performances, without ever compromising them. He seemed to have a hotline to the numinous. As Old Ekdal in The Wild Duck, he lifted the play bodily onto another plane. When he appeared last year in a piece called The Understanding, I tried to get to grips with him. This was the best I could do:

"Sir Ralph is one of the wonders of the world, and also one of the great wonderers. While taking the impossible in his stride, he contemplates the everyday like one new-born . . . you want to hug him, but doubt that he would ever let you close enough."

I can only add that he radiated compassion without ever seeming to ask it for himself; that, playing the disturbed dreaming conscience of Inner Voices, he went out on a very high note; and that he must have died the best-loved of all actors.

*

He was also the best-loved of all motor-cyclists. A candidate for least-loved Orin, who figures in Little Shop of Horrors (Comedy). Orin is a leathered leader-of-the-pack, he's-a-rebel-and-he'll-never-ever-be-any-good figure from the rocking 1950s, who has channelled (not sublimated) his vicious instincts by becoming a dentist.

His long-suffering (I mean that) girlfriend, Audrey, is loved by a much gentler person called Seymour, but what can she do? As she sings, "I know Seymour's the greatest/But I'm dating a semi-sadist." I think we may forgive that dubious rhyme for the sake of others that chime perfectly, while pushing good jokes along. "When I start extracting those molars," sings Orin, Elvis-like, to the shing-a-linging trio of Crystal, Ronette and Chiffon, "you girls'll be screaming like holy rollers."

Orin meets a gruesome end, between the jaws of a highly carnivorous plant. Other characters fare no better, but it is worse for them because they are fully conscious when they go, while Orin has been happily asphyxiated in his own gas. (Better dead than fed.)

The plant has been innocently raised by Seymour in the Skid Row flower-shop where he lives and is exploited, and he names it, in his love-sickness, Audrey II. It grows -- there is more of it in each successive scene -- and develops a raucous soulful singing-voice (halfway between Ray Charles and Clarence Frogman Henry), makes promises and demands. Seymour, Faust-like, succumbs to its blandishments until he realises, too late, that it is too late. The plant wants the world.

Little Shop of Horrors is a witty, loving, detailed raid on an identifiable era. It so disdains anachronisms that there has to be a glossary in the programme. They could have got an easy laugh by substituting Macdonalds for Howard Johnson's, but, no, they preferred to have integrity.

Serious rock-musicals (no names, no imminent closures) are intolerable; the form is so unyielding, the style so overheated, that the emotions look ludicrous. Singing a rock-song on stage is inherently funny; and the only successful shows are the ones that trade on the fact -- that are celebratory like The Wiz or mockingly camp like this one, which makes The Rocky Horror Show look amateurish. (Actually The Rocky Horror Show made The Rocky Horror Show look amateurish, but that was the point.)

Taking off from a B-movie that was more like a Z-movie, Little Shop of Horrors piles one rubbishy idiom on another, and emerges with something valuable. The show is really frightening. Now in London, as last year in New York, Audrey II -- its voice, its puppetry, its failings and its yawning maw, not to mention its tendrils that finally engulf the stalls -- scared me right out of the theatre.

From New York with it comes Ellen Greene as Audrey (I), a walking blonde lisp who can slip into invisible quotation marks to deliver the prim rebuke, "Seymour, you're aythtewical," and right out of them again to give him a dainty little slap. A definite London bonus is James Wood's Seymour, complete nebbish; and Howard Ashman's direction is as adroit as his book and lyrics, which is to say very.

There is probably no point in saying this to a world that applauded the verbal and musical tone-deafness of Blood Brothers (sorry, it slipped out), but the wedding of Mr Ashman's words to Alan Menken's gleamingly exact song-parodies exemplifies what the musical theatre is for.

*

William Humble's Fly Away Home (Lyric Studio, Hammersmith) is a painstaking, and painsgiving, dissection of a breaking marriage. Hywel Bennett, very engaging, plays the man with a fatal yearning to stay one of the boys. Diana Quick is his wife: a subtle performance, since, though not allowed to show any amiable qualities, she leaves us in no doubt that she has them.

Occupying adjacent territory is Sarah Daniels's ferociously-named Masterpieces (Theatre Upstairs), which blames male brutalisation of women on pornography, though, since the play defines pornography as male brutalisation of women, the argument, as a famous philosopher once said, is circular and not worth much.

What the play actually shows is pornography leading a woman to violence against a man: she pushes him under a train. The actresses -- Kathryn Pogson, Patti Love and Shirley Dixon -- are far better before their consciousnesses have been raised than after, when they have to deliver all their lines through clenched teeth. Miss Love has an uproarious speech about contraception, and I spent one whole scene happily admiring Miss Pogson's legs. I mention this only to suggest what a very tricky arena the theatre is for the discussion of sexism. One of the occupational hazards of my job is falling in love four times a week.

John Guare is an American playwright who can be wildly funny about contemporary Manhattan. In Gardenia (Gate, Notting Hill) he is uncharacteristically sober, though still witty, about the Puritans of nineteenth-century New England. The themes are bedrock American -- idealism and violence -- but the trouble, as in all his plays, is that you stop believing in his characters as soon as they shut their angelic mouths. Still, Mr Guare writes like nobody else. Ce n'est pas magnifique mais c'est le Guare.
