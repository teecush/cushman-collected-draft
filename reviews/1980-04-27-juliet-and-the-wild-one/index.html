Apr 27, 1980

Juliet And The Wild One

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Juliet and the Wild One

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Stratford, Manchester and London.

THE Verona which Ralph Koltai has imagined for the latest Romeo and Juliet (Stratford-on-Avon) is an intimidatingly stylised urban jungle, composed of revolving slabs of wall, decorated by some civic-minded action painter. They have to be trundled around by half-lit supers, which seems to me to diminish their utility considerably, but when in position they make a fitting environment for roving teenage thugs.

'Punk,' one mutters when the lads first appear, but the analogy is imprecise. The young Montagues and Capulets are hardly underprivileged. The period of Ron Daniels's production is hard to locate, but Romeo's zippered leather takes us back 25 years to Marlon Brando and The Wild One. So does Stephen Oliver's music, the jazziest I have heard in a Shakespeare production; the Prince seems to like his fanfares blown on a cool blue trumpet. That sets one thinking -- and it's odd how little fashions change in juvenile delinquency -- of West Side Story. One way in which that show illuminated its source was in its emphasis on the escalation of gang warfare. Most productions lead us to believe that the two families have been slaughtering each other for decades; in fact the text makes it pretty clear that, up until the death of Mercutio, none of the participants could justifiably have been run in for anything worse than grievous bodily harm.

Mr Daniels is unusually scrupulous about this; his Mercutio and Tybalt begin by fighting a formalised duel with swordsticks, their friends egging them on with ritual grunts. When the blades come out, a new and scarifying stage has been reached; Mercutio's end marks a point of no return. But it's more or less accidental; it takes Romeo -- a self-confessed 'madman' -- to plunge his knife into Tybalt with conscious exultation.

Romeo is to be regarded as a sizeable but unrewarding cross to be chivalrously borne by some actor blessed or cursed with conventional good looks and a pleasing manner. Of late he has emerged as a rewarding character in his own right and the fulcrum of the play. Anton Lesser, physically small, makes him a clever, self-conscious, rather shallow youth with a violent sense of fun. He favours Benvolio with a friendly kick in the shin, and gives as good as he gets in verbal combat with Mercutio.

Juliet's love brings out the best and the worst in him. Mr Lesser, in line with current practice, gives full value to his screaming self-pity after banishment. This is a risky interpretation, only to be vindicated if the actor can at the end make Romeo grow up. Mr Lesser brings it off; his 'I defy you, stars,' rings round the theatre as Shakespearean syllables should and rarely do.

Jonathan Hyde offers a Mercutio high on blood and self-esteem. It fits, and Mr Hyde is an authoritative actor. But I have this old-fashioned idea that Shakespeare rather liked Mercutio, and meant us to. Actors and directors, though, are nowadays too highminded to let us. In fact, as Romeo has increased in prestige, Mercutio has declined; so, more surprisingly, has Juliet.

Judy Buxton's performance is at the moment an accurate but impersonal sketch. Her best moment is her first, gazing gravely at her own reflection before the plot descends on her. There is a determined Tybalt from Chris Hunter and a fine, unfussy Nurse from Brenda Bruce, who makes the advice to marry Paris sound the most logical thing in the world. Jimmy Gardner's Peter supports her from a world of his own, one adorned by some economical visual gags. The production grips both as a comment on the play and as a piece of story-telling.

*

A story perhaps more for our own time is Love on the Dole (Royal Exchange, Manchester), the Thirties success adapted by Ronald Gow from the Walter Greenwood novel. It has some of the marks of its origin; the action proceeds in chunks and set pieces, juicy scenes that the writers could not bring themselves to sacrifice. But they are juicy, and -- this is the rare thing -- remain so on stage.

A first-act seance, apart from drawing a ripe performance from Rosalind Knight as an amateur medium, works as an image of the desperate attempts at celebration necessary to keep afloat in Hanky Park, the slum that sucks down the unemployed. The heroine, Sally Hardcastle, has her moment with a young labour organiser. He is fired from his job, then (a too easy irony) killed while attempting to keep the peace at a demo. Sally takes the one escape route open to her, and becomes the mistress of a rich bookie.

Johanna Kirby is better at expressing Sally's bitterness than her moments of joy, but it is the bleakness that matters and she uses it electrically, attacking her family as they cling to a respectability that economics have rendered meaningless; Carmel McSharry as her mother, greying in spirit as she tries to balance conflicting loyalties; John Bardon as Dad veering between alcoholic joviality and righteous wrath as reaction to an intolerable situation; Alan Parnaby as young brother Harry, for whom love means a deeper descent into the poverty trap.

The play, compared to the dramatic preachings of our own time, is amazingly unsentimental. When Harry's pregnant girl laments in conventional terms that they cannot afford to get married, Sally coolly inquires the price of a licence and, with wet kindness, hands it over. Eric Thompson's production is one of the most firmly rooted I have seen in this theatre.

*

Applauding Ralph Richardson is one of the greatest pleasures of contemporary playgoing and there is ample opportunity for indulging it at the curtain call of Early Days (Cottesloe). We are afforded one of the longest looks so far at the Richardson eccentric, far gone in forgetfulness, but capable -- to paraphrase James Thurber -- of disconcerting spurts of lucidity. The author, David Storey, has supplied him with a political past and a fractious family in the present, but neither means much, either to him or us. He keeps harking back to a single miracle childhood memory -- it is rather like watching a print of Citizen Kane in which the Rosebud references have multiplied to the virtual exclusion of everything else.

There is some melody in the writing, and some wit -- though less than there was in Sir Ralph's speech at the Evening Standard drama awards. Supporting acting (except possibly from Barbara Flynn and Peter Machin as the youngest contributors) is stiff, possibly owing to Lindsay Anderson's directorial penchant for stoical groupings. Jocelyn Herbert's set and Alan Price's music are similarly economical, especially the latter. This, I fear, is what is known as Style. It would be interesting to see Mr Anderson at work on a good play, though my memories of his last two attempts in this line (The Seagull and What the Butler Saw) are not happy. But, if his celebrated intransigence is responsible for the Cottesloe seats being rearranged so that the stage is actually visible, he deserves our thanks.

*

It may be that Creditors (New End) is Strindberg's best play; at least its three characters -- a wilting artist, his vampire wife and her avenging ex -- are unusually well balanced. But it needs powerfully graduated acting to make it seem more than another exercise in bilious self-pity; this polite production, brought in from Leicester, seldom gets nearer passion than routine bluster.
