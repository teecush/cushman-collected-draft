Dec 14, 1975

The Whole Of Hamlet

Robert Cushman · The Observer

The whole of Hamlet

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the National Theatre's new production.

ALBERT FINNEY is almost out of his thirties. His first Hamlet (National Theatre) is from one point of view an audition: we want to know if, after all this delay, he will redeem his promise. The answer, in thunder and spades, is Yes.

His success is one of presence and stagecraft, rather than of acute definition. And here the performance merges into the production, which is by Peter Hall, and which, though it is far from being entirely successful, is, in a double sense, revolutionary: it makes a change and it completes a cycle.

The cycle was begun at this same Old Vic in the twenties, when Shakespeare was presented in full texts and on bare stages, and when the production of 'Hamlet' in its entirety was almost an annual event. Almost as soon as that norm was established it began to shift; the plays were cut and they were scenically ornamented. Much of what we know as interpretation is reducible to just those two factors: what you dump on to the stage and what you gouge out of the text. And this is particularly true of 'Hamlet,' as Mr Hall's present uncut version reveals.

His line on the events onstage is no more readily identifiable than Mr Finney's; or, as it turns out, than Shakespeare's. But no critic that I know has yet offered any credible account of what 'Hamlet' is supposed to be about. Plainly it is not about its plot; Eliot was right, though ungrateful, when he complained that there is more play here than is needed to tell the story. As inherited from its source, it is a revenge drama; and what fascinates is the movement of the writer's mind around the idea of revenge, and indeed around the idea of writing a play about it. Hamlet's delays -- that gigantic alibi of 'The Murder of Gonzago' -- are so much fuel for author and character; they both create events and diversions (and it is typical of Shakespeare that he refuses to let things just happen to Hamlet) in order to think and talk about them.

Mr Finney's performance, though short on explicit characterisation, is compounded of raw irony and intelligence; and for once the soliloquies are as enjoyable as the banter. (His timing is marvellous; he can detonate a famous phrase before you have time to duck away from it.) And he meets the most basic condition of all for a Hamlet: he is an actor in whose company one is happy to spend four hours.

Agreed, he could range wider and dig deeper than he does. His Hamlet is pivoted on one moment; his reason jumps when the Ghost leaves him. His subsequent determination to put on an antic disposition is self-delusion (and treated as such by his hearers) since he is already wearing one. It serves him until the killing of Polonius when it is replaced by what Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz or the other one called 'a high, not to say, homicidal excitement' which communicates itself -- the excitement, not the homicide -- to the audience. Throughout all this middle section of the play Mr Finney is dishevelled, with his stockings round his ankles, just as Ophelia describes him; I hope he gets away with this but reviewers are notoriously prone to pounce on actors for obeying Shakespeare's explicit instructions.

In the graveyard he effectively mocks the audience with Yorick's intimations of mortality, but the performance tails off in the last scene which is hurriedly staged, yielding no sense of a destiny accomplished or an action resolved. John Bury's set is dispiriting; if Denmark's a prison, this is the yard. There are other questions to put to Mr Hall. Why is Ophelia's madness witnessed by a semi-circle of yokels, whose expressions of solicitude look even falser than their beards? Why, having erected a stage within a stage for the players -- an apt image of the Chinese-boxiness of 'Hamlet' -- is he so tentative about letting Mr Finney mount it? Why do Polonius and Reynaldo enter from opposite sides when they are plainly in the middle of a conversation? Why are so many relationships rudimentary, which is not the same as fluid?

Mr Hall is not yet in control of his new method and it is a little ironic that he should be trying it at all, since it was he who perfected -- for what were at the time excellent reasons -- the highly detailed style, thick with props and intellectual concepts, against which he now seems to be reacting. It took time to get the first right and while the possibilities of the second are equally exciting its demands are even more rigorous.

Some of them are met. One of the play's major harmonies is thrillingly sounded in the varied treatment of the young avengers: Hamlet spinning webs, the callow Laertes daring damnation, the cool, victorious Fortinbras collecting a kingdom, and the bloody-handed Pyrrhus, inset in the Hecuba speech (delivered with predictable perfection by Robert Eddison, who also snares a good play-scene with Struan Rodger). The full text constantly assaults the ear with unexpected material.

The time goes by fast, and the counter-manoeuvrings of Hamlet and Claudius are gripping; Denis Quilley, in resplendent voice and excellent at plotting, reverses the new orthodoxy about Claudius being the nicer man of the two. Indeed many old verities are restored in this production: Polonius (Roland Culver) is back to being an old duffer after a few years living beyond his means as a subtle politician, while Laertes, who has always seemed like type-casting for Simon Ward, is revealed to be just that. Ophelia is Susan Fleetwood, for whose grace and power I am beginning to run out of superlatives. And the clowns are J. G. Devlin and Stephen Rea, who virtually reprise their father-and-son act from 'The Playboy of the Western World': a sequel suggests itself in which the Second Gravedigger, goaded beyond endurance by the riddles of the first, brains him with his own mattock and runs off to take refuge in a hostelry in Finland. (Title: 'Inn at the Finnish.')

Only with two performances does Mr Hall flout tradition. Angela Lansbury, gazing into space as if human contact would wrench her from her dream of queenship, valiantly attempts to do justice to Gertrude's stupidity: how could she sit through the play and not suspect? This is an area which most actresses, faced with an underwritten role, tactfully ignore; and Miss Lansbury, acting against her temperament and in an alien style, might have been happier if directed to follow their example. But Philip Locke, from his first wry words ('What is Horatio there?' -- 'A piece of him') makes brilliant sense of Hamlet's friend. Truly his fellow-student, he is also his censor; seeing far enough, he knows when, for safety's sake, to draw back. Complementing Mr Finney's performance he also lends it meaning; his is the watchful thought of the survivor while Hamlet's is the unbridled mind of the self-destructive hero.

*

I should have more space for two most likeable openings. Ben Travers, unquestionably our and everyone else's finest octogenarian dramatist, has a new comedy called, not too meaningfully, The Bed Before Yesterday (Lyric). Joan Plowright plays a woman who, after half a lifetime of revulsion from the flesh, rather suddenly takes to it; never an actress to do things by halves, she offers in effect two performances, one of vinegar and the other of honey. John Moffatt partners her with great skill. Mr Travers's plotting is uncertain, but his words coruscate and his feelings do him credit. Writing about sex from (forgive me) a missionary position is apt to impair a writer's sense of humour, but his appears to be inviolable. Of the 7:84 Company's Yobbo Nowt (Shaw) more, I hope, next week.
