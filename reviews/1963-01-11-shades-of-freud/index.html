Jan 11, 1963

Shades of Freud

Robert Cushman · New Cambridge

Shades of Freud

Robert Ashman interviews Philip Mansell

[The printed surname “Ashman” is a byline error for Robert Cushman.]

Philip Mansell, who produces The Winter's Tale for the ADC this term, is an habitual joker with a voice ranging between Peter Ustinov and David Frost, and a habit of fingering a guitar when at a loss for words.

His serious moments derive a contrasting intensity from the facetiousness that surrounds them. He can be very disarming, as when he blithely admitted that he wanted to produce the play because, having read the critics and seen the lamentable television production, “I thought it couldn't be as bad as that.”

Why Shakespeare for his first major production? He feels that he is more liable to understand a play that he has studied academically, and wishes to communicate his understanding to an audience. He dismisses previous productions as “lopsided and sentimental” and refuses to regard the play as a fairy-tale or Leontes' jealousy as incredible. His theory is at any rate ingenious.

“Leontes' marriage has been on the rocks for a long time before the play opens.” He sees Hermione as a frigid sophisticate whom Leontes is now unable to satisfy. Leontes projects on to their marriage a guilt-complex deriving from unconscious homosexual feelings for Polixenes, his inseparable companion in adolescence; Hermione's curiosity about their boyhood suggests that she suspects the memory is undermining her marriage.

Welcome change

The emphasis on Hermione's unsympathetic qualities is at least a welcome change from the traditional plaster-saint conception. Mansell brushes aside the suggestion that all this psychology would have surprised Shakespeare by referring to Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy.

“Elizabethan psychiatry,” he claims, “lacked our vocabulary, but not our insight.”

It is usual to regard the play as an allegory of the healing-power of youth; but for Mansell, Leontes is cured purely by instantaneous shock-therapy: the news of his wife's “death.” His essentially weak nature is easily led to religion as a consolation; he is more conscious of having sinned against his God than against his queen.

Sympathetic

This interpretation, regarding Leontes as basically sympathetic and the fulcrum of the play, places a great responsibility on the normally placid Tony Gooch: not the most obvious casting, but Mansell regards Gooch's range as extending far beyond the parts he has played hitherto.

Tony Palmer plays Autolycus, whom Mansell unexpectedly describes as “bitter—all thieves must be cynics”—a piquant piece of casting, perhaps. But certainly the comedy scenes can stand some freshening: it was heartening to hear Mansell inveigh against Gloucestershire accents for the shepherds.

He also rebels against the customary dewy-eyed treatment of Florizel and Perdita (played by Helen Kaswell). For him they represent “A type of Lawrentian uninhibited sex-relationship” which throws into relief the soured sexuality of the Sicilian court. The cast is predominantly young—more than three-quarters freshmen, culled from an influx which Mansell regards as exceptionally promising.

Perry Mason

I mentioned a rumour that he planned to equip the Sicilian court of justice with a stenographer. He remained non-committal, merely remarking that he wanted to achieve “A businesslike Perry Mason atmosphere.”

But no modern dress—“that's cheating.”

“The whole process of Shakespearean production,” he informed me with an oracular air, “should be simplification.”

This profession of faith is perhaps a rationalisation of his lack of confidence in his ability to handle mass movement. For him a crowd represents “an extra duty for the producer”—a duty which, unlike some other directors (he mentioned Mark Lushington), he prefers to forego.

Welcome approach

His own acting experience is fairly modest, but this does not worry him. As a director he wants to explain rather than demonstrate—an approach which most actors will welcome: nothing is more demoralising than a director who attempts to act a part by proxy. He refused to admit the possibility of an actor saying “I can't do this” but is prepared to tolerate “I can't see the way to do this.”

Twenty-seven actors have been warned.
