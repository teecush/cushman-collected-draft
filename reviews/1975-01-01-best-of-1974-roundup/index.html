Jan 1, 1975

Best Of 1974 Roundup

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

ROBERT CUSHMAN

OBSERVER

Best New Play: KENNEDY'S CHILDREN

Best New Musical: COLE

Best Performance by an Actor: ROBERT STEPHENS in Ghosts

Best Performance by an Actress: CLAIRE BLOOM in A Streetcar Named Desire

Best Performance by an Actor in a Supporting Role: JOSS ACKLAND in A Streetcar Named Desire

Best Performance by an Actress in a Supporting Role: BERYL REID in Spring Awakening and Romeo and Juliet

Most Promising New Actor: MICHAEL FEAST in The Tempest

Most Promising New Actress: CELIA QUICK in Stallerhof

Best Production (Director): EDWIN SHERIN for A Streetcar Named Desire

Best Production (Designer): CARL TOMS for Sherlock Holmes

I HAVE one or two doubts about Kennedy's Children; I am enough of a traditionalist not to feel total confidence in a dramatist until he has made his characters talk to each other as well as to me. Still, you can only approach these annual contests determined to vote for whatever turns you on (posterity can sort out the ultimate values) and Mr Patrick's monologues, more than any conventionally structured play this year, sent me out of the theatre aching to tell my friends. It contains, by the way, the most stunning musical moment of any show this year; when the silent barman (as vital to the whole as the auditor of Beckett's Not I) crosses to the juke-box and selects Don McLean's American Pie. [Song lyric excerpt omitted.] Mr Patrick's achievement has been to assemble the generation, and find them the place.

Second best for me was Peter Nichols's Chez Nous; nice that he should have given us this in the same year as the tearing disappointment of The Freeway. If, as we are always being told, a dramatist's job is to get up the noses of his audience Chez Nous should have delighted everyone, since I met hardly a soul with a good word to say for the play. Either they didn't understand what the characters were about, or they understood all too well and winced. I liked Play Mas, The Island, Stallerhof, Travesties (if Tom Stoppard had really got it together it would have been the play of the year); and laughed at The Gingerbread Lady and sizeable sections of The Norman Conquests.

My pleasure in seeing Robert Stephens re-emerge as a major, unmannered actor puts him ahead of considerable competition, much of it emanating from the RSC, where the concentration of male talent at the top has been extraordinary. Nicol Williamson, Ian McKellen, John Wood, Richard Pasco, Ian Richardson, Emrys James and Alan Howard have all given major performances. Pasco's Richard II, which so brilliantly realised the king's ultimate triumph "in being nothing" might have had my vote if it were not a hold-over from the previous year. The same consideration debars Frank Finlay in Saturday Sunday Monday. I also admired Charles Kay in Tartuffe and (not really a contender but delightful nonetheless) Derek Godfrey in The Marriage of Figaro.

It is probably no accident that the best female performances were in American plays: Miss Bloom was hotly pursued by Elaine Stritch and Deborah Norton, though there might be a sui generis award for Maggie Smith in Snap. Joss Ackland only just outpaces Timothy West (in A Month in the Country), Arthur Lowe (in The Tempest and Bingo) and Stefan Kalipha in Play Mas. Mickey Feast may not be altogether new, but then who is? And Cole, being an anthology, may not be an ideal musical but then, this year, what was?

1974 began well (Sherlock Holmes was as nice a New Year present as you could hope for) but it got pretty horrible, particularly in the West End. The RSC did far better at the Aldwych than at Stratford; the new National got off to a splendid start with The Tempest, lost it, and are now apparently en route for Square One. Frustrating.
