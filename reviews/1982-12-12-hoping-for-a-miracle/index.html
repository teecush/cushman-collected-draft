Dec 12, 1982

Hoping For A Miracle

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Hoping for a miracle

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Messiah, Steafel Variations and Alcestis.

IN Bent, Martin Sherman wrote a celebration of homosexuality set in a Nazi concentration camp. In his new play, Messiah (Hampstead), he goes to similar extremes, using the pogroms of seventeenth-century Poland as the backdrop for a celebration of Jewishness.

Not of Judaism: the play's attitude towards religion is, at its most enthusiastic, a weary shrug. It is epitomised in the character of Rachel, an ugly young woman with a lively mind, who treats God as a confidant, someone to complain to. Reluctantly, she lets herself be married off to a rich middle-aged fruiterer. Then news comes, relayed by her new husband's scholarly nephew, of the advent of the long-awaited Messiah, alive and working miracles in the Middle East, under the name of Sabbatai Sevi, and prepared to go to Constantinople and take the Sultan's crown from him.

The news produces galvanic effects; the Messiah proves a focus for the community's hopes and fears. The fruiterer, hitherto a worldly man, gives away his possessions and persuades himself he can fly to Jerusalem. He throws himself off the roof, with disastrous results. (Clive Swift plays the role, and any saviour who would kill off Mr Swift halfway through a play has got to be suspect.) Rachel is sceptical, but nevertheless, as an almost merry widow, she accompanies the nephew and her old mother, traumatised into dumbness when the Cossacks killed her husband, on the boat-trip to Turkey. She does not enjoy the ride.

Asher, the nephew, says that under the rule of Sabbatai, all the scriptural prohibitions are abolished. He persuades Rachel to eat non-kosher meat. She persuades him into bed with her, and is delighted with the results. When disillusion strikes, Asher violently rejects her. Worse follows. It seems that the Old Testament God is revenging himself. Rachel tries to reject him, but finds she cannot; she goes on addressing him, even telling him he doesn't exist. She is saddled with the idea of God; which is why I call the play a celebration of Jewishness.

"God damn you, God," says Sarah at the moment of despair. That ought to be a neat and poignant paradox, but as dramatic English it is badly strained. Mr Sherman strongly resembles Peter Shaffer in his choice of themes and in his execution; his audacity and his stagecraft run considerably ahead of his actual writing. It is hard for any playwright to portray an orthodox Jewish character having chucklesome conversations with the deity without summoning up Fiddler on the Roof, and Mr Sherman does not escape the cosy shadow.

What he is saying is far from cosy: a rejection of salvation in human form (Rachel has some wry asides on what Jesus meant for the Jews), an acceptance of a capricious and implacable presence that exacts worship, but does not reward it. Rachel's mother, breaking silence, sees God as the demon who led the Cossacks to her husband. Sabbatai's wife (we meet her, but not him) has her own views, though they are not easy to follow.

Ronald Eyre's production, whose merits include a brilliant instantaneous evocation of a Turkish bazaar, cradles a conquering performance from Maureen Lipman, funny ("Of, do I have impure thoughts"), erotic and adventurous. (The play has a strong feminist strand.) Jack Klaff's Asher is a strong, cracked idealist and Shirin Taylor a sultry Sarah.

*

Sheila Steafel, flying solo in Steafel Variations (Apollo), has acquired a new opener, with which she closes, and a new closer, with which she opens. These are not original ideas, but she performs them so intently that they acquire shine. Also new is a fanatically rhymed song, written by Dick Vosburgh and Frank Lazarus and recalling Lorenz Hart on an over-ingenious day: clever in itself and funny as parody.

I was happy to meet again the chanteuse who smokily celebrates a certain Mère Brun (who must go sous la table), the lieder singer performing extracts from the Freierreise cycle and showing a marked impatience with her subject ("The Maiden and the Halibut"), a pubescent variety star called Popsy, whose breathing is hopelessly out of sync with her lyrics, and a pinched-voiced impressionist, whose act consists of a hopeful re-creation ("and look who's standing over here") of a Hollywood party. Small and quizzical, Miss Steafel preserves an ironic detachment not only from the people she does, but from her doing them. It has been suggested that she needs a smaller theatre; actually she just needs a larger audience.

*

Theatro Technis, a Cypriot company in Crowndale Road near Mornington Crescent, has revived Euripides's Alcestis. Alcestis was a queen who sacrificed her life for that of her husband Admetus, a deal sanctioned by the god Apollo, who owed Admetus a favour for hospitality received. Ted Craig has staged this perplexing tale in Edwardian country-house clothes (Heracles comes for the weekend bearing racquets) against red upholstery on red carpets, which is striking; and has pointed up the family background, which is apt. The chorus become, more or less, the waiter and the porter and the upstairs maid; Andreas Markos's old retainer is especially convincing. Admetus does not seem to have decided just how selfish he is meant to be (Euripides seems to think very), but Anthony Woodruffe is quite uncompromising as his horrible dad (doubled with Death, done as an undertaker) and Anna Savva is quiet and gentle and very beautiful as Alcestis, who is, of course, at the point of death. If she can play alive as well, she may be an important actress.

A few days before she died I received a commendatory note from Caryl Brahms concerning a review of a show with which she had no immediate connection. This was generous and possibly eccentric; Miss Brahms was well known to be both. She co-authored some extremely funny novels and was an enthusiastic and thoroughly individual critic. She was one of our few genuine characters, and since I cannot thank her in person, I do so here.
