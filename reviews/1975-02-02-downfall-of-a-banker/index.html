Feb 2, 1975

Downfall Of A Banker

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Downfall of a banker

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the National Theatre's new production of John Gabriel Borkman.

THE theatre's contribution to the Ibsen revival is Ibsen's John Gabriel Borkman at the Old Vic.

Ibsen's penultimate play is concerned, characteristically, with the hallucinatory effect on normal life of a man -- a "builder" who preceded him and the sculptor who was to follow. In When We Dead Awaken, J. G. Borkman, banker, expresses his author's fears of having sacrificed his life -- not to pay his wife -- to the demands of his art (or more brutally of his career) and of not having produced work of a quality to justify the sacrifice. Master builder Solness grew tired of building houses, and turned instead to spectacular towers; Ibsen increasingly forsook intricate domestic realism for prose poetry.

But since he was still telling stories about families he could not abandon his old skills altogether; and, in John Gabriel Borkman, he parades them before dismissing them. Borkman has dreamed and dared too much; more prosaically he has embezzled a vast amount of money, and been detected; eight years on, his career and his marriage lie in ruins. He is a recluse, never stirring from his room; one floor below, his unforgiving wife listens to the ceaseless pad of his feet; and dreams of getting their son to redeem the family name.

The situation is explained and developed in three rather tight acts with a decidedly exceptional event for Ibsen, and with a new limpid simplicity. The machinery is far less cumbrous than usual; Mrs Borkman's hopes are foredoomed to frustration, and there is something almost contemptuous about the ease with which her son is allowed to escape her, slipping off to a warmer climate with the local siren. We sense that the reason for the speedy dispatch of this business is that Ibsen is not really interested in it. The play exists for its final scene, in which Borkman begins an ascent to the mountaintops in order to survey the country which he has always believed to be his kingdom. He dies of exposure, according to his wife, but by the account of her sister whose love he had sacrificed to his ambition at the start of his career -- of guilt, clutching his heart like ice.

The mountain air may kill Borkman, but it makes the piece the most bracing, least stifling of Ibsen's after Peer Gynt. It is a play of extremes, beginning with a strange attractive combination of psychological tension and narrative relaxation, and bursting into poetic freedom; corroded love for her husband.

The seductress, Mrs Wilton, the only unillusioned character present, is played by Anna Carteret, most enlivening at her first appearance, rather too blatant at her second; and the smallest parts are done exceedingly well by Cheryl Campbell and Barbara Keogh. The production yields a surprising number of laughs, none forced, all justified, but I doubt if Mr Hall's view of the play is fundamentally comic. Grandeur seems to be the aim; and all three leading performances, though incomplete, sporadically attain it.

It is in the nature of Ralph Richardson's Borkman to play tricks with perspective; his is a typically quirky performance, allowing the character no mercy, half Ancient Mariner, half White Knight, he presents a florid, majestic fraud. His pacing of his room denotes nervousness rather than caged impatience; his gait, once freed from his wife, is as awkward as the iron I once dreamed of quarrying out of the mountains, is invested with all the mysterious conviction of which this actor is capable. Borkman dreams of a comeback, but Richardson never lets us believe in its possibility. The text may appear to support him; Ibsen is at his most self-lacerating, and Borkman's actual lines may suggest no more than wistful megalomania. But the architecture of the play seems to demand something more. For Borkman finally does confront his wife, does break free; both the confrontation and the release are under-written, but the actor must somehow find them within his own personality. They are nowhere prefigured in Richardson's performance, though he is magnificently rotund in the final speech; actor and character reach the heights together.

In his ascent he is beautifully partnered, selflessly served, by Peggy Ashcroft as his forsaken sister-in-law; beauty and selflessness, indeed, are everywhere apparent in her performance, sometimes too oppressively. She emphasises nobility as insistently as Sir Ralph denies it; it has grown on her as the play has proceeded, for at the beginning she has played a fine duet with Wendy Hiller's Mrs Borkman, suggesting that they remain sisters in selfishness; they have merely sublimated their bitterness in different ways. Miss Hiller is rocky, unforgiving and, like the production, rewardingly unafraid of melodrama; but she fails to convey her corroded love for her husband.

Statements is about South Africa's racial laws; and yet it is these that inevitably make the greater impact on an audience. I found the carefully fashioned dialogue more rewarding on a second hearing, but it means less than the cruel play of police torches on two naked, imperfect, vulnerable, human bodies.

Nudity here is more than a device, almost a dramatic subject; Miss Bryceland's shyness and pride under interrogation, Mr Kingsley's shame and anguish, are what will remain. Coupled with Statements in a daunting double-bill (probably the most dimly lit show in London) is Not I, Samuel Beckett's monologue for a frantic, disembodied mouth, white-lipped in the encircling blackness so that my thoughts strayed towards Al Jolson. This was probably reprehensible of them and certainly fatal; since, by the time I had refocused them, the play (11 minutes long) was over. Mr Fugard had exhausted my concentration; but two years ago I found Not I (voiced, as now, by Billie Whitelaw) riveting.

Howard Barker's Claw is the liveliest thing I remember at the Open Space: the engrossing story of a neglected schoolboy's rise to prosperity as a ponce and summary extinction at the hands of the ruling class. Act One is mordantly funny scene-setting; Act Three is a savage verdict. Act Two should assemble the evidence, but Mr Barker devotes it instead to a cartoon of sexual laxity at Ministerial level, racier but no more relevant than John Osborne's in The End of Me Old Cigar. Billy Hamon burns through the leading role in a tight, lucid production by Chris Parr in which everybody deserves praise and Roger Sloman, Rod Beacham and Isabel Dean hereby receive it.
