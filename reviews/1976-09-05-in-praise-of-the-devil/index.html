Sep 5, 1976

In Praise Of The Devil

Robert Cushman · The Observer

In praise of the Devil

THEATRICALLY this year's Edinburgh Festival is the best I have known in four years' service. The Assembly Hall, tightened by Birmingham Rep's Measure for Measure, is positively glorified by its setting. The Devil is an Ass, Peter Barnes's streamlining of a particularly rare Ben Jonson, is less fortunate in its surroundings but nearly as good.

Mr Barnes has helped the text on its way by simplifying its intrigues, cherishing its most durable jokes, and adding a few of his own. He has, for example, made repeated and increasingly hilarious references to a valued but absent gentleman-usher, making a running gag out of what in Jonson was at best (the character in question being called Ambler) a strolling one. He has rendered the same service, on a larger scale, to a more important character, the devil himself. Jonson's hero is a hopeful fiend called Pug, who begs Satan for a day on Earth to gull humanity. Set down in Jonson's London, a town composed entirely of knaves and fools, he finds himself, unexpectedly but repeatedly, of the latter party. Satan had warned him how it would be on Earth, how far behind hypocritical mankind had left the Devil ("they have their Vices there, most like to Virtues"); he is even forced to rescue his protege from jail.

Together they make a final return, survey humanity which they find crouching in attitudes of corruption as if posing for a Breughel; they determine to find new worlds where devils are still devils and men still eat apples rather than each other; they will quit Earth, "this plague-spot of the cosmos."

That last phrase does not sound Jonsonian (it tries too hard). Nor is it; the entire episode is Mr Barnes's own and the archness of the writing is a price worth paying for the new shapeliness of the action. Jonson let his devils dwindle and vanish, but his adapter helps Bernard Lloyd to play a magniloquent Satan, and rescues Chris Ryan's pugnacious Pug from the law of diminishing returns. As he progresses through the Satanic mill, clumping and straddling in his human shape, trying to get laid or just get lucky, his frustrations build.

The terrestrial society that torments him is composed, as usual in Jonson, of gullible inventors, confident tricksters and painted ladies. Mr Barnes and his director, Stuart Burge, manage to persuade us that this constitutes a sustained version of corruption, rather than five acts of inveterate (and invertebrate) grumbling. Charlatan-in-chief is Meercraft, described by Jonson as "a Projector"; Russell Hunter does indeed project, very forcefully, what it would be nice to know Meercraft might be like when off duty.

The principal victim is an ambitious bumpkin named FitzDottrel; Jonson, almost alone among comic dramatists, has no affection for his dupes, and the part is accordingly well-roared by Peter Vaughan, who is an unusual combination - fanged and greasy. The supporting cast usefully includes David Burke as a quiet and stony factotum brimming with uncracked smiles and Alan Rickman, who speaks breathtaking verse while in drag, which may have been nothing in the seventeenth century, but calls for attention now. Robin Archer's costumes do London up proud and Hell up something sumptuous.

Japan's Bunraku Puppet Theatre (Royal Lyceum) is hypnotic, with the rider that hypnotism inhibits detailed response. Each puppet is controlled by three hooded but otherwise very visible figures about twice its own size. The dolls constantly seem to be about to break free, and at the curtain calls, which they take with shameless abandon, they appear to have succeeded. (One of them looks strangely like Rab Roy, perhaps in homage to Scotland.) At the side of the stage a narrator chants the stories; one feels sorry for the Japanese audiences who, understanding him, must miss the full exoticism of the entertainment.

Language uncomprehended is more deliberately employed by the La Mama Company of New York performing Greek tragedy in the long, bare gymnasium of Moray House College. The director, Andrei Serban, begins from the position that every translation of these plays, ornate or colloquial, sounds forced. Mr Serban goes back to the original Greek, not merely undeterred but encouraged by the fact that his actors do not understand it; it is enough that they understand the emotion behind each speech.

This would seem to be a recipe for bad generalised acting, but in fact Mr Serban's belief in a feral power residing in sound rather than sense turns out to be justified; as Andromache, whose young son is taken from her and murdered in The Trojan Women, Priscilla Smith emits a strange keening screech as if she were expelling a demon that had taken refuge in the roof of her mouth. For this play Mr Serban has utilised Aztec and American Indian dialects as well as Greek; he owes much also to the universality of the play. (Next to The Bacchae, it is for good reasons the most performed of Greek tragedies.)

It is easy for us to identify with the captive Trojans, especially in this environmental staging in which we mill around while soldiers, bearing prisoners in wooden cages, divide our ranks. Euripides's subtlest and most acrid passage - the acquittal of Helen - has been transformed into something simpler, though exciting. Dirt and abuse are hurled at her, but she does not wriggle free; we see her executed.

Vocally and visually, this is a brilliant production; Mr Serban's broad effects are more than acceptable because he can convince us that this is a play about ourselves. But he cannot do that with Sophocles' Electra, a play about a family, in which we need to apprehend details. The meeting of Orestes and Electra, accompanied by gentle but overwhelming bell-ringing from the chorus, is moving because there is power in the mere fact of reconciliation; not, it seems, in the mere fact of murder. We watch this play sitting down, and it pays to look to the sides, where scaffolding climbs the walls and actors climb the scaffolding. Few productions make such thrilling use of height; few spaces, of course, offer the scope.

On the fringe there is joyous story-telling by Shared Experience, the best group to emerge since Hull Truck, who specialise in dramatisations of the Arabian Nights. The piece I saw, Dalilah the Wily, features trickery on a scale (and in a good-humoured style) that would bemuse even Jonson. It is performed at great speed, with a minimum of costumes and props, and a maximum of character-transformation by the six actors, who are liable to switch roles in consecutive speeches. Ensuring our complicity, without a hint of blackmail, they raise the comic temperature to heights rarely experienced.

Of two shows at The Traverse, Kibbo Kift, billed as a rock song-cycle, suffers from the limitations of its style and of its subject: a breakaway Boy Scout group of the thirties that went political, to be reviled from Left and Right; only when it gets to broader issues - what makes men join movements - does it become really interesting, and it gets to them late. Still, it is something to see a middle-aged actor belting rock with the power and wit displayed here by Godfrey Jackman.

In Richard Crane's Nero and the Golden House, Mr Jackman plays St Peter and gets crucified. Mr Crane, with a rage and authority beyond anything I have found in his earlier plays, here counterpoints the birth of Christianity with the death of Nero, a self-made god who failed to pass the test of resurrection. Welded to this is the conflict of two modes of kingship: one sober and authoritarian, the other capricious, light and playful. That last is the operative word; the Golden House is Nero's own theatre (actor and author, his name should have been Orson Welles) and there are two plays within the play. The intricate structure, supported by language that rings, chimes and sparkles, and unbeatably directed by Chris Parr, must be reassembled in London.
