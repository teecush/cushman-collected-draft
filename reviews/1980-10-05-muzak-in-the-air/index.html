Oct 5, 1980

Muzak In The Air

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Muzak in the air

NEIL SIMON'S musical They're Playing Our Song (Shaftesbury) concerns an affair between a composer and lyricist, which at least adds a new twist to the perennial question of which comes first, the words or the music.

I call it Mr Simon's musical, since the book is more important than the score. This may seem odd, given the avocations of the characters -- who are reputedly based on Marvin Hamlisch and Carole Bayer Sager, the composer and lyricist of the show -- but disappointingly little is made of the working relationship. Vernon Gersch and Sonia Walske might be any urban couple in a Neil Simon comedy; there is nothing here of the fantasy or broad satire with which Mr Simon has extended his range in his previous musical scripts: Little Me, Sweet Charity, Promises Promises.

But the form still takes its toll; the songs are Mr Simon's alibi for digging less deep than he otherwise might. The first act, which is glad preparation for the affair, is enjoyable; the second, which is relationship and break-up, gets frustrating. Mr Simon won't tell us anything. All we learn is that Sonia has a dependent ex-boyfriend called Leon who won't let go; and phones up at unearthly hours. She keeps rushing to his side and is late for appointments; so she's a kook. Of Vernon we're told nothing; or rather we're told that he's an obsessive perfectionist but we never see it. Leon (Leon Petsky, I can just see him) is the plot.

He's also one of two running gags. The other concerns Sonia's wardrobe. She has an actress friend who gives her old costumes, so she can talk about her "Cherry Orchard" dress, or the one from "Pippin." This turns out to be an arresting affair with a halter-top and nothing but fringe below. Gemma Craven looks stunning in it; Tom Conti has fun primly arranging the bits. But both these jokes are shamelessly over-exploited.

I'm a fan of the Simon wisecrack; it's not only funny but truthful, a natural defence for people who lead with their nerves and guard with their intellects. There are a couple of deathless ones here about Vernon's broken-down car and the American Automobile Association.

But this doesn't make a play; or, really, a musical. Just one scene breaks out; Vernon and Sonia, on their first date, hear their biggest individual hits, back to back, and go into the title number -- much the strongest in the score -- and thence off to bed. Success is an aphrodisiac; there is a point musically and theatrically made. But otherwise the songs are just wan decoration; Mr Hamlisch's music is spineless (theatre music may be predestined for muzak but the ghastly end doesn't have to be met so eagerly halfway) and Miss Sager's words anonymous. I played the American cast album recently and found that, despite having seen the show in New York, I couldn't place any of the songs; they had no dramatic weight.

Mr Conti as Vernon has some excellent physical comedy, notably a sequence in which his hands close round a bottle, impelled by a sudden urge to brain his beloved; but the burdens of singing (which he does perfectly adequately) and an American accent seem to constrict his acting. Miss Craven, cool and level-headed, is hardly a natural for Sonia but she is maybe our best actress-singer. They make a good dancing pair: he funnily self-absorbed and angular, she flowing and dynamic. Each protagonist is equipped with a trio of alter egos, mainly there to provide an excuse for a disco sound; the cues for their appearances steadily get thinner and more irritating. There are no other visible characters. The sleek original production has grown somewhat tattered in transit but Douglas W. Schmidt's sets, mainly black and-white projections, retain their sheen.

*

I wonder if Sonia and Vernon would work as vehicles for Victoria Wood and the Great Soprendo, whose Funny Turns (King's Head) is the best entertainment in London though I must admit that it tells us little about the state of their marriage. The Soprendo is an actor pretending to be an oily Mediterranean pretending to be a magician, both pretences succeeding magnificently well and each serving the other in fighting off the stupor that may result from the contemplation of total competence. I generally find conjurers boring but the Soprendo knows when to muff a couple of tricks and to subdue his audience with barbed-wire patter. ("Thank you," he murmurs with murderous obsequiousness, "for that applau.")

His is the first half, Miss Wood's the second. Her repertoire of songs is not growing as fast as might be wished, and I can't quite concur with the colleague who declared that she purposely avoids writing memorable tunes; I reckon she wouldn't mind one or two. But at least her melodies support her lyrics, the only ones I know to combine traditional rhyming and metrical skill with mordantly funny observation of today's Britain seen as part supermarket, part surgical ward. She manages, as writer and singer, to be warmly dismissive; as stand-up comic she spoils an excellent run of gags about her native Morecambe by fast nervous delivery; once she slips into a persona (as a patronising W.I. chairman) she is much happier.

*

The first half of Jeeves Takes Charge (Fortune) I was inclined to write off as an impossibility; I suppose Bertie Wooster, in the flesh, would be the guffawing chump that Edward Duke makes of him but what has flesh to do with Wodehouse anyway? Bertie is not a man but an angel, truly ineffable; Mr Duke makes him undeniably effable. The Wooster style is swift and light, beyond the power of any actor to capture at a pitch suitable for the human ear.

In Act Two, however, Mr Duke turns, far more happily, to Aunt Dahlia being ungainsayable and Gussie Fink-Nottle handing out school prizes with a gap between his teeth as wide -- to judge by his lisp -- as the hyphen between Fink and Nottle. Here, too, Gillian Lynne's direction desists from underlining every word with a gesture and turns to the discovery of a tap-dancing style suitable for the young master. There is a charming Art Deco set: the kind in which every cupboard looks like a fridge.

*

You will have noticed that everything so far discussed is, at biggest, a duologue. Here's another. In Vaclav Havel's Protest (Orange Tree) a Czech writer smoothly argues himself out of signing a petition while a released dissident (played by Frank Moorey like the spirit of conscience) hears him with infuriating compassion. You think Havel may be trapping himself into a tract; he shows his stature with a tartly theatrical ending. The companion piece by his compatriot Pavel Kohout, The Licence, is a group piece on the same theme, more plainly laid out and more obviously a parable. Havel you take as literal truth.

*

Just now at the Half Moon in Pal Joey they were asking (or would have been if they had kept to the original lyric which I understand has been restored in the transfer to the Albery) "will Saroyan ever write a great play?" A negative answer is returned at the same theatre by Playthings, a set of yet more duologues between actors representing inanimate objects. The conflicts -- of ceiling and floor, lampshade and hat-rack -- and so on -- reflect human jockeying for position, and at the end we are treated to the brief sermon on man's destructive nature you might expect from an author of advanced years and whimsical disposition.

If I've made it sound dreadful so has he, but it somehow manages to be pleasing as well. The cast are admirable, notably Thick Wilson as, among other things, a clove of garlic whose very image he manages to look in a bunched green tunic credited -- if that is what "illustrations" means -- to Andy Warhol. Let us now praise famous men and women: the adroit director is Mai Zetterling.
