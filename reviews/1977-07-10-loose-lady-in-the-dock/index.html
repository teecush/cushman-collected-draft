Jul 10, 1977

Loose Lady In The Dock

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Loose lady in the dock

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN ranges from Rattigan to Shakespeare.

Terence Rattigan's Cause Célèbre (Her Majesty's) is by way of being a triumph for the traditional West End play. I say "by way of being," since, though the Rattigan vessel sails securely enough into port at the end of the evening, its early passage is pretty rough. There are even moments when it looks like capsizing.

The weaknesses which, with a becoming sorrow, I noted down en route divide in retrospect into two categories: those which turn out to be strengths in crafty disguise and those which just lie there helpless.

To take the latter first: Rattigan's heroine is Alma Rattenbury, a suspected murderess of the 1930s, whose feckless bewildered charm melts even those most prejudiced against her; I wish Rattigan had found some other means of making this point than providing her with a stony-faced wardress who draws sentimental sighs from the audience when she consents to address the prisoner by her first name, and is her devoted friend thereafter.

Then there is the matter of Edith and her circle. Edith, I should explain, is forewoman of the jury at Alma's trial and her story is told in tandem with Alma's. Alma lives in Bournemouth, Rattigan's cockpit of gentility (cf. Separate Tables); Edith wants to move there now that she has rid herself of her husband. Ridding herself of her husband - by bashing him on the head or getting her teenage lover to do so - is what Alma is being had up for; Edith has merely divorced hers for infidelity. Alma, the loosest of loose women, is everything that Edith, upright and uptight, most abominates. Edith's thin-blooded upper-middle circumstances are the kind with which young playwrights today are likeliest to come unstuck; it is a shock to find Rattigan too resorting, probably unawares, to caricature. "Jury service ... my dear, how too frightful," says Edith's confidante as the plot gets under way. "He was only about 40 when, you started ... having headaches," she goes on apropos Edith's old man. Edith picks up the Significant Pause habit, while laying her soul conveniently bare: "I never did care much for that ... side of things."

But we are already on the verge of the play's deceptive faults and actual successes. The juxtaposition of the two protagonists comes in the end to something very impressive; both are brought unsparingly to a desperation which seems inevitable rather than just a playwright's whim. Though Rattigan seems at times to be putting his leading actresses through some fatiguing hoops, he makes it worth their while, and they respond in kind. Edith's son, aroused by the newspaper accounts of Alma, goes out to get laid and contracts VD; this gives Helen Lindsay the opportunity to play a minor Mrs Alving and she rises steadily from there. Alma, passionate and bedraggled, is ideally served by the butterfly vulnerability of Glynis Johns. On a more relaxing level there is a showcase role for a KC (descended from the one in The Winslow Boy), with holes in his socks and invincible courtroom command: Kenneth Griffith, heading a covey of television notables, plays him with the right Rattigan blend of sensitivity and shamelessness.

The trial scene, by the way, makes great use of flashback; this would seem to be a disastrous idea, fatal to the infallible tensions of the courtroom. In fact it makes the play, providing scenes that are not only gripping in themselves but propel the characters forward to the point where each must jettison her most cherished image of herself. Rattigan's celebrated technical mastery usually leaves me cold; here it knocked me out. This is certainly the best of his recent plays; it may be the best of them all.

*

And so we leave the commercial theatre, remarking only that Lonsdale's On Approval is back yet again - at the Vaudeville - and that Kenneth More and Patricia Routledge are too mild a pair of holy terrors to dissuade us from heeding the play's title and sending it back to the management with a curt note.

Meanwhile, at the Open Space, E. A. Whitehead's long-awaited Mecca proves a disappointment, an "is that all?" kind of play. Like Cause Célèbre, it is a schematic piece with an unobjectionable moral about tolerance and hypocrisy, particularly sexual; the difference is that Mr Whitehead's characters never transcend their frame. They are brought to a pretty pass, rather than a transfixingly ugly one.

Six British holiday-makers (two couples, two singles) are at a holiday-camp in Morocco, protected from the natives by barbed wire and broken glass; one girl strays outside and is raped. Her compatriots turn on a native boy they think responsible and beat him to death. Their very smooth Moroccan courier eventually agrees to fly them safely out, at a stiff price; soon, he says icily, Morocco will reach their dubious standard of civilisation, but they should stay away till then. As if to bear him out, the muezzin sounds, reminding us that some people still have standards.

Earlier we have seen our tourists drink cheap red wine and eat oranges, wince at Moroccan poverty, quarrel over their own various degrees of permissiveness, make variously successful passes at one another. Most of the author's bile is reserved for a childless career-woman. Played by Judy Cornwell, who was one of Mr Whitehead's Old Flames, she takes respectable feminist arguments, brandishes and degrades them, tearing apart an unsatisfactory lover and reacting to the report of rape with the self-righteousness of a lynch-mob. The dialogue, well-tempered in Jonathan Hales's production, flows with malignant ease, but none of the people are more than attitudes and some - notably Miss Cornwell's homosexual husband - are less.

*

I suspect that while rehearsing The Alchemist Trevor Nunn actually found the philosopher's stone. If King Lear at the Aldwych was an improvement on its Stratford self, Romeo and Juliet is a transmutation. I never thought to be so moved by this play: by the reconciliation at the end (with the stilling of John Woodvine's raging Capulet) as well as by the lovers. Francesca Annis plays the potion scene with terrifying immediacy, as she plays her love-passages with enchanting immediacy. The balcony scene, as she and Ian McKellen act it, is funny as well as magical; hyperbole made flesh.

This is a great production, partly because it is so abundantly witty; the play is as conceited as its contemporary Love's Labour's Lost. So Michael Pennington's Queen Mab speech is mockery and celebration at once, and this whole performance brilliantly blends Mercutio's loneliness and camaraderie. The stage is crammed with meaning, in relationships and in individual performances (Miss Annis fairly oozes with it). It extends to the clown, Peter Richard Griffiths, with his Bottom, has come to full comic mastery. He is a walking slow-burn.

Bow Down (Cottesloe) must leave in the main to Peter Heyworth, merely congratulating Tony Harrison on bringing a beautiful macabre poem to our attention and skilfully exploiting its variants, and Harrison Birtwistle on his most supportively dramatic score: to date, Walter Donohue's production is both witty and scary, though inclining to artiness when all else fails. Actors and musicians share responsibilities and look very intense about it; we should inform them that the same thing is being done very light-heartedly on Broadway in I Love My Wife.
