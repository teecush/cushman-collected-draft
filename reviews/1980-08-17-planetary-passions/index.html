Aug 17, 1980

Planetary Passions

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Planetary passions

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on `The Life of Galileo' and `Othello.'

IN an interview a couple of years ago Howard Brenton described himself as `an anti-Brechtian, a Left anti-Brechtian.' Now here he is as the translator of Brecht's Life of Galileo (Olivier): a play whose central event, fittingly enough, is a recantation.

There should be joy on the South Bank, if not actually in heaven, since Mr Brenton's version is mostly excellent. Its clarity is in line with the best aspects of the play and of John Dexter's production. This play, with a Renaissance European setting, has an Oriental delight in contemplation beyond anything to be found in Brecht's Eastern fables. Galileo, his disciples and his enemies are united by their passion for thought: by its shape as much as its substance.

I remember Mr Brenton, in his former mood, complaining that `The Caucasian Chalk Circle' didn't connect with the class struggle. I don't know; it features kindly exploited peasants and unpleasant masked landowners, and I'm not sure that `Galileo' gets much further. The play posits that the Inquisition's real objection to the physicist was not that his cosmology contradicted that of the Bible, but that in removing the earth from the centre of the universe it greatly diminished the prestige, celestial and terrestrial, of the princes of the church, and gave the masses ideas above their station.

Sometimes this is believable; a young monk, who almost against his will becomes Galileo's pupil, has a good speech about his parents, happy in their poverty, because they consider it part of a divine order. But once the scene is over you begin to wonder (in line with Brechtian theory but not with the thrust of the play) whether their faith would be much affected either way by the position of the planets. Tom Stoppard is not a playwright one would ordinarily compare with Brecht, but this play resembles `Jumpers' in overrating the influence of astronomy on personal conduct.

For the most part Brecht keeps the people offstage; when they do arrive they celebrate Galileo's ideas in a carnival scene which has rather more impact as spectacle (though it's oddly underdone in this production) than as argument. Mr Brenton suggests that Galileo's smuggled truths are a metaphor for Communism in our day. You can just as well equate them, if you start to think that way, with any other minority creed: with Christianity in Russia, for example. Actually, in the sharp early scene where Galileo debates with the orthodox thinkers, the traditionalists in their eloquent appeal to the beautiful symmetry of their received ideas sound more like Marxists than Galileo ever does.

Our hero is in fact transcendently selfish; that is what unifies his character and the play. In the last scene Brecht, disturbed by the atom bomb, gives him a social conscience: `I believe the only aim of science is to relieve the toil of human existence.' That's a noble aim, but it is as unlikely to inspire a great scientist as a great artist. It hardly squares with the Galileo whose opinion of other people is governed solely by whether they help or impede his work. Knowledge (as Brecht's own pupil Friedrich Durrenmatt was to point out in `The Physicists') is a neutral, therefore dangerous, force; Brecht wants to tame and moralise it. As usual he evades tragedy.

The play lives as a clean piece of story-telling, and as a celebration of genius. Mr Dexter's production, in which even Galileo's legs-akimbo stance at his wash-basin looks like a geometrical figure, does full justice to the first but little to the second. Michael Gambon, who plays the lead, is an actor of great skill and subtlety who excels in depicting the character's ageing process, and in elucidating his thoughts. But he has to try hard (in plain terms, to shout) in order to convey energy and the returns rapidly diminish. (I will muzzle the unworthy suspicion that Brecht protested so much about Galileo's sensuality because he couldn't actually dramatise it.)

Selina Cadell meticulously traces the development of Galileo's daughter from ingenue to jailer, but much of the acting is disappointing. On the principle of chastising the best-loved, let me pick on Simon Callow and Stephen Moore. Mr Callow plays the peasant monk as an urban mooncalf. Mr Moore negates his imaginative casting as an Inquisitor of Shavian gentleness by utilising his familiar spaced-out timing to get Ayckbournian laughs.

*

In Othello (Aldwych) Cassio's rhetorical wish that Jove might watch over the Moor is incongruously turned into a formal prayer. That is the only gaffe I could discover in Ronald Eyre's superbly lucid, detailed, eloquent and moving production. Relationships are tangible; the outbreaks of violence have the shock of inevitability; movements are always revealing. Othello, entering the Senate, makes straight for the military briefing table.

Some of the deepest obscenities of his degradation escape Donald Sinden, but he brilliantly captures the boyish innocence and uncorks a fine passion. Burnishing his voice, he reveals it as an instrument of unsuspected virtuosity; his clubman's warmth has given way to a suave authority that can turn `the goodness of the night upon you' into a genuine benison, disturbing in its confidence.

While his assurance cracks, that of Bob Peck's Iago hardens; he ends as a squatting statue of satisfied malevolence. The solidity of the character merges with that of the actor who discovers new conversational riches in every line without ever veering from his course. What tortures Othello most is Iago's conscientious matter-of-factness about Desdemona's infidelity; Mr Peck worries him into madness. These two performances, in opposed but complementary modes, are not only a magnificently interdependent Othello and Iago but golden examples of the strengths of contemporary verse-acting.

*

Writer's Cramp--which to these southern ears is John Byrne's most congenial play--has resurfaced at Hampstead, enriched all round. A prologue comments on the growing fame of F. S. McDade, Scots bookman and no-hoper, since this tribute was first unveiled three Edinburgh Festivals ago. Mr Byrne, with an eye as sharp as his ear for risible detail, has himself designed a McDade Memorial Corner, fit for the most painful of illustrated literary lecturettes. The cast of three has reassembled, with John Bett's narrator even more of a Muggeridge, discoursing in a silky wheeze that makes every word of praise come out as a disparagement.

Mr Bett doubles as McDade's flighty mother, sublimely. Alex Norton reads the poems, in tones of surprised disgust, and also plays a uniquely unnerving prison warder impinging on one of our hero's frequent darkest hours. F.S. himself, going from school to death-bed without so much as a change of blazer or moustache, is Bill Paterson, as a virtuoso blend of swaggerer and mendicant.

*

A full-stage revival of Elmer Rice's The Adding Machine, which in 1923 domesticated expressionism to the American marital front, would probably expose the play unkindly. The pocket production at the Gate, Notting Hill, allows us to take it as an elegant little joke, with enjoyable soliloquies for the nagging Mrs Zero and for her biddable spouse who turns murderer when automation pushes him off his counting-house stool. He goes to heaven, where he is revealed (and reviled) as the essence of wage-slave. He also meets another murderer, endearingly done by Mike Burnside, a plump and dignified comedian, like a louder Richard Griffiths.
