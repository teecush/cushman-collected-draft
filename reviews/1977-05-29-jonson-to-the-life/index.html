May 29, 1977

Jonson To The Life

Robert Cushman · The Observer

TREVOR NUNN'S production of The Alchemist (Other Place, Stratford) is the funniest and most accomplished I have seen of this or any other Jonson. The Alchemist, being so well-erected on a basis of intrigue and trickery, presents the problem of playing its author in its purest form. The action seems to demand that the play be treated as farce, but if taken with that degree of velocity, the language -- that knotted, exuberant compound of music, pedantry and slang -- becomes an incomprehensible morass.

Most productions pause reverently for Sir Epicure Mammon's famous rhapsodies of covetousness, but otherwise devote themselves to knockabout, with tedious results. Others, operating with virtuous and scholarly restraint, have achieved a different kind of boredom. Nobody until now has bothered much with character. Mr Nunn's company reveal it to be the foundation of the play.

Each of the minor characters is given a sharp visual identity. Most striking of all is Nickolas Grace's Drugger, on whom the habit of nervous ingratiation has bestowed a permanent timid stoop, and whose complexion suggests either Chinese ancestry (unlikely, really) or a nasty case of jaundice. He takes some pride in his ill-health; his eyes almost pop out when he proclaims "I had another sickness worse than this", quite in the vein of Aguecheek recalling that he was adored once, too.

Alan Cody's Dapper, with the nose if not the brain of a sharper, Susan Drury's rampageous and catarrhal Doll (a very tart tart), Hilton McRae's rural quarreller and Bobbie Brown's outstandingly buxom widow, all fit neatly and assertively into place: a fine parade from the RSC's apparently endless reserves of new acting talent. Paul Brooke, though he might take his own injunction "be rich!" a little more to heart, wheezes and waddles quite acceptably along the paths of Mammon. Only the Puritans falter, surprisingly since they are usually infallible; the one line from the play that everyone remembers ("thou look'st like Antichrist in that lewd hat") goes for nothing, as -- more importantly -- does the greeting "peace to the household" at what should be the height of chaos. Mr Nunn still has some way to go in the orchestration of riot.

A subtler, more crucial orchestration really underpins his production. The play begins with a quarrel between the two cheaters-in-chief, Subtle and Face, issuing in forced reconciliation and a determination to see "who shall shark best" the rest of the day. Their relationship becomes part contest, part alliance; and its tensions are charted here by John Woodvine and Ian McKellen with wonderful concentration, each keeping obsessive score of the other's takings.

The tension is aggravated, and our evening made, by the succession of quick changes enjoined on them by their own and their author's plotting, transformations which Mr Nunn, with pardonable cruelty, has made as frequent and difficult as possible. The abiding image of the production is of the actors switching furniture and removing evidence with one hand, unbuttoning buttons and jerking-off jerkins with the other, while outside the door of Chris Dyer's ambuscaded set the customers pile up.

Each, besides, has a relish for disguise that goes way beneath the skin. Mr Woodvine when he plays the alchemical adept can be a self-flagellating fanatic or your gentle GP. Mr McKellen's Face lives up to his name; as himself he is nothing, but as patch-eyed highwayman, as fire-blower swathed and hooded almost to mummification, as primly subservient butler or -- in moments of great stress -- as bits of all three, he is everything. At the close his eyes, surveying his pile, have the gleam of the possessed: a striking finish if too insistently moral for my taste. The company has room to expand -- spiritually if not, in the Other Place, physically -- but it has the play under its belt and I left feeling that, at last, I have it under mine.

In comparison, the RSC's Man and Superman is a little tame, though certainly pleasant: exceedingly so if the circumstances -- reopening of the Malvern Festival and the weather and the Cotswolds -- are added in. Richard Pasco's Jack Tanner has exhilarating attack; his theories could hardly be better voiced. But the play hardly registers any more as one of ideas, and it is doubtful if it ever did.

Tanner the fine speaker is dramatically the mug; as he says himself, "the marked down victim, the destined prey" of Ann Whitefield. He realises it late, but we should be in on the joke almost from the start, enjoying her wiles and his helplessness. Susan Hampshire's Ann, sweet and sly, plays all that is in the text, but the role really needs a Maggie Smith to go beyond that and establish a conspiratorial rapport with the audience.

James Cossins and Nicky Henson are their expert selves in a production set, I imagine for economic reasons, in a void. At least I could discern no artistic motive for a drawing-room in which a solid flight of library steps leads only to invisible bookshelves. The play, robbed of stuffiness and prosperity, also forfeits its atmosphere.

At the Royal Exchange, Manchester, David Terence's dramatisation of The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn is mainly notable for the emergence in the title-role of Enn Reitel, all horse-sense and confusion; and for the proper establishment of the river, flowing serenely and invisibly through the middle of the auditorium, as the story's source of energy and of repose.

An earlier epic is given elaborate treatment by the Prospect company at the Old Vic; those bits of the Iliad dealing with the deaths of Patroclus and of Hector have been rechristened War Music, a title which seems, if taken literally, to signify percussion (courtesy of Gary Kettel, a virtuoso). While the beat goes on, the lights (Nick Chelton) turn blood-red, the set (Pamela Howard) looms wide, the ochred actors gyrate to William Louther's choreography. "Half-naked men hacked slowly at each other," says Christopher Logue's text, observantly.

Achilles's Myrmidons wear fearsome headgear, like black motor-bike helmets with teeth. It all looks like a Stratford production of Troilus and Cressida and it gets much the same kind of agitated acting; both sight and sound are more in place here, however. It is saved from being a celebration of war by Mr Logue's contribution, notable less for crude undercutting (which occurs -- e.g., "the Greeks are in a fix") than for an alternating command of momentary and sustained effects. The focus ("the daylight stiffens to translucent horn") is always sharp.

Mr Logue reclaims the blank-verse iambic as Tony Harrison the rhyming couplet. Most of its delivery is safely with Timothy West, a master both of rhetoric and the throw-away. Unlike Homer, however, the creators of War Music cannot make you care; the impressiveness of Toby Robertson's production is abstract.

The night after the fall of Troy, the Russian Revolution broke out. Robert Bolt's State of Revolution seems to me a pedestrian though not a boring chronicle, well produced and excellently acted. More of it next week.
