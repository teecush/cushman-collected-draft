Nov 14, 1976

Garnett In His Glory

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Garnett in his glory

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

In The Thoughts of Chairman Alf (Criterion) Warren Mitchell as Alf Garnett comes in glory before, and very nearly among us. Though he stays up on the stage Mr Mitchell straddles, besieges and buttonholes his audience. He talks, sings, squeals and bellows directly to us, and most effectively; he was squandered on those other people, that family, on television, involving him in situations and even occasionally plots: a right waste of time.

Every so often Mr Mitchell, whose audience control is 95 per cent consummate, sings music-hall songs which--on the lips of a real character from a recognisable life--sound for once as good as has been claimed for them. They are, in truth, a bit genial and observant for near-sighted Alf, but only a quibbler will quibble. In Johnny Speight's script--a two-and-a-half-hour monologue--there are inevitable sags, but at its best it is extraordinarily resonant; it made me want to hear Mr Mitchell as one of the great Shakespearean or Shavian demagogues.

It even has a shape; after Alf has thoroughly aired his prejudices (alternately massaging and outraging ours) he compares his life unfavourably with that of an animal (and yearns to be a royal corgi or Princess Anne's horse) and finally subsides into silent despair, bald head quivering, piggy eyes sunk, moustache and glasses mute reproaches to an unheeding society. In so fully exhibiting and understanding a man whose views they detest, Messrs Mitchell and Speight have healed the bruised carcase of political theatre.

*

This was still aching from wounds inflicted three days earlier by A Seventh Man (Hampstead) adapted by Adrian Mitchell from John Berger's book on migrant workers. Confronted with characters (West German industrial doctors for example) of whom he disapproves, Mr Mitchell makes no attempt at understanding but he cannot bring himself to be honestly external either; so he throws in bits of discreditably "revealing" dialogue. The people he likes hardly do better: the script flounders between individual and group portraiture, and though the play declares that peasants should not be sentimentalised it seems unsure what else to do with them. The affecting moments are soberly factual; but most of the time the Foco Novo company provide sloppy dramatics. I was not convinced that any of the actors had ever met a migrant worker or that they had worked out any compensatory style. There are the obligatory songs; Mr Mitchell's lyrics carry the occasional charge defused by the uniformly dreary music. Some groups (7.84, Hull Truck) make effective use of music, others (Joint Stock) virtuously do without. Both ways are preferable.

*

Forty years ago Rodgers and Hart wrote a really meaningful song about a lady on a trapeze. In the main refrain she told us, in an appropriate metre, how she swung high and low, initially subject to falls but progressing to a state of perfect security. Extrapolating a general principle from her particular experience, she enjoined us to "stick to your trick and your trick will be art," adding that "artists are hard-working men." The subject of the song was Practice, long recognised as the only route to Carnegie Hall; its title was Over and Over Again.

There is no trapezienne among the characters of The Force of Habit (Lyttelton) but there is a tightrope-walker, sharing the arena with a clown, a lion-tamer, a juggler, and a ringmaster. They are not artists, they are artistes, and that extra undroppable "e" causes them great anguish. They are trying to better themselves, to double as musicians and play Schubert's Trout Quintet, and on the day they bring it off won't the paying customers get a surprise. That day, though, seems distant; they have yet to get through a rehearsal, let alone a rendition. Playing Schubert Over And Over Again is for them not Practice (noble) but merely Repetition (dull). The force of habit wearies even as it compels and the playwright, Thomas Bernhard (of Austria), pushes the point ruthlessly home.

He has constructed his play on the pattern of that very quintet, filling it with reiterated phrases. Today, like every day, will be the day that the circus left town; each player tells us, on many occasions, what he will do "in Augsburg tomorrow." This reiteration, most resourcefully engineered, is neither particularly haunting nor unduly significant, but it makes a bright running gag. At least it could. This being a National Theatre production we might expect acting to compare with a really sparkling professional recital of the Trout Quintet itself; here we get the equivalent of the show the circus five might put on if their great day ever arrived. Which is carrying Pirandellianism a bit far.

Philip Locke, a brilliant supporting actor, is monotonously querulous in the dominant role of the ringmaster, a musical man with a wooden leg and with a load of other grievances. Gawn Grainger, a character actor deprived of a character, settles for sneering camp. Warren Clarke works up a good head of drunken resentment and Oliver Cotton has a pleasant motley grin, but Brenda Blethyn, scornfully and almost silently pert, was the only one I credited.

Stripped of its surface entertainment value (you would never guess from Elijah Moshinsky's ponderous direction that the text is rhythmically composed) the play is forced to rely on its philosophical merits, which are small. In a key passage (a trade term for the bit reprinted in the programme) the ringmaster declares himself fed up with playing Schubert but condemned to go on trying; life, it is hinted, is one long process of failing to get through the Trout Quintet, a proposition that may be refuted by a visit to almost any reputable concert-hall. The participants don't seem to like their circus lives much either, but this theme is largely neglected in favour of the musical one and finally contributes little more than shades of Anthony Newley.

*

Simon Gray's Dog Days (an Oxford Playhouse production now on tour) was written before his Otherwise Engaged, and if it is not a trial run for that play the arm of coincidence must have stretched to extravagant lengths. Both plays concern publishers with teacher-wives, tired of their apparent indifference, and teacher-brothers, sucking up to their headmasters. The later version, in which the hero's icy smartness denoted bastardy all through, is preferable to this one in which it merely disguises pre- and extra-marital impotence. Not that this is a false conclusion; merely an uninteresting one.

The wit of the two plays is similar; which is to say that it consists of smug put-downs which I found very funny. Charles Kay dispenses them with address, though I was not convinced he had the right address. He may be one of those sharp mellifluous actors adrift outside the classics. Everyone else in Gordon McDougall's production (and especially Gayle Hunnicutt) looks not adrift but marooned.
