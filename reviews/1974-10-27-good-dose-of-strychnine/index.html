Oct 27, 1974

Good Dose Of Strychnine

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Good dose of Strychnine

EVER since I took on this job I have been waiting to see a West End comedy at which I could laugh consistently. It seems a modest ambition, but only this week with the arrival of Neil Simon's The Gingerbread Lady (Phoenix) was it gratified.

Mr Simon writes phenomenally good wisecracks; not since Tom Stoppard's Jumpers has any play in this city boasted so worthy an accumulation of laugh-lines. Mr Simon, to adapt Jean Hagen's defiant splutter in Singin' in the Rain, is funnier than Alan Ayckbourn put together. In this production he has Elaine Stritch to deliver his goods; and she is an incomparable mouthpiece.

Evy (Miss Stritch) may represent Mr Simon's means of exercising his own fearsome facility. She is a compulsive high-quality gagster; and her jokes are shown to be both the mask and the consequence of her own inadequacy. Psychologically this may be sound, but theatrically it has its drawbacks. After an immensely enjoyable two acts we are implicitly commanded to stop laughing in the third. Just what we are meant to do instead remains uncertain.

Evy is a woman with several pasts: in fact, a past-mistress (of a guitar player). Her other former occupations include nightclub singing, marriage, motherhood and alcoholism. When we meet her she has just returned from being dried out. Her friends hover around, hoping that the cure will last, but in fact helping to precipitate her fall from the wagon. For they are losers themselves, and their troubles are quite enough to drive Evy to drink; or at least to furnish her with the pretext which is all she requires. Meanwhile, however, another piece of her past has materialised. Her 17-year-old daughter has decided to adopt her, maybe even to save her from herself. At the final curtain it looks, precariously, as if she may stand a chance; Polly, says her mother, enunciating a standard piece of Broadway lore on the proper relationship of mother and daughter (cf, the close of Gypsy), "when I grow up I want to be just like you."

This aspect of the play is not so much false as ill-realised. The young are not a species with whom Mr Simon feels at home, and daughter Polly has been synthesised rather than created. ("I'm sticking with marriage" is her reaction to an experiment with booze; the time-lag between the play's American and British productions has not been kind to her.) She suffers, too, in the direction (we are not always allowed a clear view of her reactions to her mother's progress) and even in the playing. Jenny Quayle is clearly a strong and intelligent actress; but her personality, not just her voice, is ringingly English.

Evy's pals are given good if not outstanding performances. Sarah Marshall, with a set of vowels all her own, is an experienced man-eater who, having with fanatical conscientiousness kept young and beautiful, finds that on her fortieth birthday she is not loved, and her husband wants a divorce. Kevin Lindsay is a middle-aged actor, still dreaming of stardom while being fired from a bit part by a boy producer just out of college, but sensible enough to resist marriage with Evy on the grounds that he's an alcoholic nymphomaniac and he's homosexual and they'd have trouble getting their kids into a good school. From which it may be seen that in Mr Simon's world wit belongs not to Miss Stritch alone, but to all the desperate.

She none the less appropriates the lioness's share, to such effect that it is hard for us to wish her other than she is, no matter what it would do for her health. She comes on in a non-alcoholic daze and remarks on the change that sobriety has wrought on her view of her apartment: "I thought I had twice as much furniture." From then on I never wanted her to stop. Miss Stritch does as well by her lines as Maggie Smith but where Miss Smith teased, soothed and bandaged her words (some of them needed it), Miss Stritch, with richer, less vulnerable material, is a bouncer, occasionally a murderess. A furious self-deprecation ("I very nonchalantly got down on my knees") is her speciality; Max Factor should bottle her verbal acid and market it. They could call it Stritchnine. That it really is poisonous--suicidally poisonous--can be seen in her face: in the wrinkles, in the winces. There is one other performance as complete as hers and it comes complete as hers and it comes not in the smallest role in the play: from Lulie Callan as a Puerto Rican delivery boy.

*

Whatever else may be ailing in the West End, the star system, on first night's showing, still prospers. Miss Stritch speaks her first line from off stage and so on Wednesday she was applauded before entering. The previous night, in Alun Owen's The Male Of The Species (Piccadilly), Edward Woodward entered, was applauded, crossed the stage, wandered off, came back, and was applauded again. This I take to be the theatrical equivalent of double-entry bookkeeping; a practice initially profitable but ultimately hazardous. This is Mr Woodward's show; he plays three parts (as in The Wolf last year) and plays them most efficiently; if he wishes to prove that his range extends beyond Callan he has made his point. But versatility is not everything; Miss Stritch in The Gingerbread Lady is not essentially different from Miss Stritch in Company, but her full-length portrayal is far more satisfying than Mr Woodward's clever snippets.

Mr Owen's play is in fact three playlets, Mr Woodward portraying three men in the life of Michele Dotrice. The first two treat her rough; so she takes her revenge on the third. Now for the evening to work in any sustained way we would have to take an interest in Miss Dotrice, she being the only constant. But no actress should be asked to play the betrayed innocent twice in one evening; even once is pushing it. By the time the third play comes round we are attuned to watching Mr Woodward; and Miss Dotrice, as tough a cookie as she now is, may go hang. As it happens the third play is the only tolerable part of the bill. Its predecessors are soggy and soapy, but in the end Mr Owen gets down to frank and competent gag-writing (though it sounds a little hollow without canned laughter) with the acting and production stylised to match.
