May 9, 1982

Shaw For Our Times

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Shaw For Our Times

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Chichester and London.

IT rained heavily on the first night of the Chichester season. I have never known this happen before in all my experience as a critic. Truly the country is going to the dogs.

Or it's going On the Rocks. That is the title of a play by Shaw, written in 1933, which seemed topical when I read it 10 years ago, prescient when the Mermaid staged it a couple of years after that, and positively uncanny at Chichester now. It takes place in Downing Street, where Sir Arthur Chavender (Lib.) presides over a Coalition Government which is having trouble with unemployment. That is true in two senses: the governed haven't got jobs and the governors aren't doing too much work either.

Chavender is an amiable fellow, overwhelmed by it all and longing to get away for a spell of golf. Meanwhile he spends time dictating a flatulent speech on the sacredness of the family to be delivered to high-ranking churchmen. His own family does not live up to his prescriptions, his wife being at her Welsh sanatorium. During his stay there he gets through the complete works of Marx and comes back determined to push through a full Socialist policy. This turns out to have beneficent side-effects for the police, the Admiralty (hearty laugh when the Ruler of the Queen's Navee talks of sending warships to the Gulf), capitalists large and small, and the landed nobility, who see the abolition of death-duties as a lifeline.

The representative of each group pledges his support, delighting in his own perks and sure that everybody else's will prove chimerical. The only opposition comes from the resident Tory, whose threats include the raising of a private army in Union Jack shirts, and from the forces of Labour, threatened with the loss of the right to strike. This odd but quite plausible alliance scuttles Chavender; but in fact he has been ruined already by the factional selfishness of his own supporters.

At the end of the play, with the unemployed rioting in Downing Street and the mounted police charging them, and his secretary rushing out to aid the resisters, Chavender muses on what has to be done, adding, 'I shall hate the man who will carry it through for his cruelty and the desolation he will bring.' This is a jolt--almost the only point at which the play recognises the realities of dictatorship--and it carries a lot of weight.

By the end both children will have been taken in marriage--and I mean taken--by aggressive members of a deputation from somewhere called the Isle of Cats, who claim to speak for the working classes. One of them is a peer, the other a self-educated Shavian superwoman. This being late Shaw, he has christened her Aloysia Brollikins.

Sir Arthur is also visited by a lady in grey whom he takes to be a spirit, but who turns out to be a doctor offering him an extremely expensive rest-cure with awful persuasiveness of the failures of parliamentary democracy, and he has all the Shavian Cockney charm of Alfred Doolittle. The best book on Shaw I know ('The Shavian Playground' by Margery Morgan) describes Hipney as the Tempter, someone, presumably, to be resisted. But I defy any production to get that over. Certainly the present one (by Jack Emery and Patrick Garland) does not try.

It is a handsome affair, dominated by a long table that cries out for a full-scale conference scene. This does not arrive until the second half, but, when it does, it fulfils expectations. Both Shaw's characters and his jokes are thinner than in earlier plays, but he can still set up half-a-dozen opposed viewpoints and send them ricocheting round the room. This scene, naturally, brings out the company heavies: not before time in a production whose younger characters are poorly--in some cases wretchedly--played.

Keith Michell keeps the show going through the first act with appealing eloquence, though his conversion seems to make no change in him; he keeps vocalising, and mostly to the same tune. Paul Hardwick brings some good bluster to the diehard, Lockwood West a delightful ruddy benevolence to the Sea Lord, and Nigel Stock is ungainsayable as the Duke of Domesday. Arthur English deservedly stops the show as Hipney, but the most striking of all is Aubrey Woods's suave police chief, chilling the house with his memories of service in Ireland: 'Auxiliary forces . . . it means extermination; and when it comes to the point you can't go through with it.'

This is a particularly striking moment if you have read Shaw's preface, which uses the word 'extermination' with deliberate blitheness as a synonym for the painless weeding-out of social undesirables. This, remember, was in 1933. In that year Shaw could write, 'The extirpation of the Jew as such figured for a few mad moments in the programme of the Nazi party in Germany.' He was sharp enough to have noticed that, and stupid enough to dismiss it. He could write of the OGPU, not to qualify his enthusiasm for Stalin's Russia, but to reinforce it.

Somehow his dramatic instincts survived his polemical ones, and though On the Rocks is conclusive (the family metaphor is never properly pursued) it fizzes almost throughout. This production puts it effectively into modern dress (the substitution of 50p for 3s. 6d. is a comment in itself, if a wildly optimistic one), though it barks its shins a few times. Hipney claims that his father knew Karl Marx: in person? The show ends with an invasion by riot police: not what Shaw wrote, but a logical addition to it.

*

Proof that Shaw put the best of himself into his plays can be found in his letters: at least in those assembled by Jerome Kilty in Dear Liar (Mermaid), an epistolary account of his long and preposterously literary flirtation with Mrs Patrick Campbell. Robert Hardy and Sian Phillips, she especially, do their stuff with style as the correspondents (never, never co-respondents), but he comes across as a finger-wagging bully and she as a spoiled vamp. She was, by all accounts, a first-rate bitch. He, by all accounts, was very nice. Perhaps that is how he could afford to be so inhuman.
