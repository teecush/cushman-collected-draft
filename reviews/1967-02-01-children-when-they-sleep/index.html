c. February 1967

Children When They Sleep

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

Month and year estimated from a handwritten archival annotation; exact publication date unverified.

The Christmas show I most enjoyed was the Players’ Victorian panto Whittington Junior and his Sensation Cat: an Original Civic Burlesque by Robert Reece (1870). The Players’, of course, is working in ideal circumstances. Audience and performers have a real rapport; they each know what is expected of them, and rush to provide it. A play may be able to spit in its audience’s eye (though even this is a form of flattery) but no show stands a chance without some alignment out front.

The feeling is contagious. I’m no aficionado but I felt like one for the duration. There were regrettably fewer outrageous puns than I’d been led to expect; maybe the artificiality of rhyming couplets creates the illusion of punning where none exists, and the one fault of Don Gemmell’s production was an occasional loss of the rhythm of these couplets. Loss of form inevitably meant loss of content (fewer laughs) but there were quite enough compensations: Sheila Bernette’s brilliantly witty miming of the Cat (a face that’s deadpan, bewhiskered and fur-thatched is irresistible) and John Heawood’s black slave, a camp refugee from George Mitchell-land with a great line in song-and-dance. In fact, all the musical numbers in an eclectic (Wagner to Stephen Foster) score were projected with terrific flair and precision; particular credit to Anna Dawson, the ultimate principal boy, and Ann Beach, whose line in roguish bitchiness suited the Belle of Bow (ready for a ring) to the last tinkle. Reginald Woolley contributed some splendidly artificial sets; effects were neither shamefaced nor ostentatious, the twin banes of our theatre’s attempts at spectacle. But then this isn’t mainstream Christmas entertainment; there’s no pretence that it’s aimed at the children, which must account for my own conviction that they’d lap it up.

For consider the alternatives. The Palladium’s Cinderella is hopelessly tethered to the archaic ‘family audience’ ideal, and since about the only thing they can be sure the family have in common is a television set, we get a blown-up jaded TV variety show. The programme correctly states that ten years of Sunday nights have made the Palladium the most famous theatre in the world—the most vicariously visited anyway. That’s still no reason for making Monday to Saturday just an elongated replica of the real business of the week. It may make things easy for the gag-writers if the show’s humour consists entirely of a stream of telly advertising slogans, but I noticed the response steadily diminishing as the evening wore on. Either the commercials are not as deeply ingrained in pop mythology as someone thinks, or the audience recognised them only too well and felt vaguely cheated at being offered the same again at Palladium prices. Might just as well stay at home; next year, perhaps.

There are things to enjoy in Cinderella. A lot of money has been spent, and is handsomely seen to have been spent. The transformation scene is all anyone could desire and the costumes are gorgeous; after the penny-pinching of most Christmas shows in London, this is bounty. The Ugly Sisters are rewardingly individualised by Hugh Lloyd and Terry Scott. Cliff Richard, who passes for Buttons, has developed puncture-proof stage assurance, but no personality to go with it; his singing is as cutely colourless as ever. The Shadows remain a highly efficient backing-group, but apart from Hank Marvin, impregnable behind spectacles and teeth, they are non-existent stage performers. The best of the show is in its pop-package aspect, though I’d reckon on more kicks from the programme announced by Brian Epstein for the Saville. The under-twelves, though, should dig Cinderella, particularly if the accompanying mums are those who buy the Bachelors and request Family Favourites.

But just who do the Aldwych think they’re catering for with The Thwarting of Baron Bolligrew, which looks by no means lovelier the second time around? Leo McKern’s Bolligrew has proved as irreplaceable as his Toad of twelve years ago, and though Sebastian Shaw and Clifford Rose do well by the goodies, other talented actors like Nicholas Selby and Charles Kay are reduced to hopeful or desperate bashing. The piece is self-defeating; few children old enough to follow its convolutions would tolerate its whimsy and condescension. It seems aimed at the offspring of the liberal couple anatomised by Glenda Jackson in US; preaching, in fact, to the children of the converted, who would probably be happier with a good middle-range Shakespeare like this company’s Henry V. As it is, the best things are the in-jokes: the echoes of The Wars of the Roses at the council-table and in the field, and the transformation of the Dragon into one of Paul Scofield’s greatest roles. The play is so full of leisurely incident that it weighs itself down, and the rather fussily inventive production does nothing to speed things up.

If ever the National Theatre feels like doing its own bit of Bolligrew-thwarting, it might have a go at Peter Pan. Certainly somebody should, and my delirious fantasies of an Olivier-Hook and Plowright-Pan were perhaps defences erected against the tired and tatty proceedings at the Scala. Julia Lockwood is all right, nothing special, as Peter, and Ron Moody’s immense gifts find no outlet in Hook. Straight or comic, Hook is a baroque villain as fruity as his cake. Moody is too much a miniaturist for this, while, maybe through misplaced integrity, he underplayed Mr Darling into oblivion. Diana Scougall made him a charming spouse but the pirates were just a shabby testimony to the doctrine that the kids will swallow anything. Well, they didn’t; they talked.

No such fate befalls the buccaneers of Treasure Island. The Mermaid production has ripened with the years. Some of the juice of Stevenson still seeps away, and the opening sequences are so rushed as to be meaningless to anyone ignorant of the original. The story works if trusted, and an irrelevant storm could well be jettisoned in favour of a closer look at the characters. Ben Gunn gets the best chance here; Clive Elliott is splendid, despite a surplus of Milliganesque antics. Bernard Miles is a first-class Silver when he sticks to Stevenson, but still undercuts himself with superfluous gags. Richard Beale and Edward Jewesbury are a fine square pair as captain and doctor; and though I think George Innes places too much trust in Israel Hands’ Christian (sic) name he makes a strong impression. The other pirates are rushed out of existence; their final dissensions could be gripping but the production wastes its chances. The fights are great, though, and enough basic masterpiece comes through to be worth anyone’s while, whatever their age. It sent me back to the text.
