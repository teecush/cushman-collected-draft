Feb 1, 1969

The Man with a Load of Mischief

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

The Man with a Load of Mischief

Certain shows should be reviewed the morning after or not at all. Writing for a monthly the temptation to control yourself, contain yourself, and try to let tranquillity be your goal can be overwhelming. Having succumbed to it in this instance I find that tranquillity offers very little in the way of recollection. But then Man with a Load of Mischief offered very little in the way of emotion (that much I do remember), belonging as it does, or did, to the tinkly style of musical comedy, staking its all on fragile charm. That which aspires to the condition of gossamer has no kick coming if it is found to float, traceless, out of the mind. I am making the experience sound more agreeable than it was, but in the interim there has been Mr and Mrs, which has exhausted most of my spleen for this month. That was fish-and-chips theatre, and this one has perceptible inclinations towards caviare. They are not fulfilled but the show does occasionally reach the tinned salmon level.

It calls itself “a romantic New Musical” and the appellation has brought the name of Novello down on its head. But nothing so lush has been attempted. The real inspiration would appear to be The Fantasticks, the off-Broadway equivalent of Salad Days, though in longevity it now more resembles The Mousetrap. In its wake there have appeared such slender confections as the present one, small-scale costume intrigues which make no great claim to being funny or anything vulgar like that, but will be happy if you go away with your heart a little warmer and a little more wistfully wise in the ways of love and life.

The settings are mostly European (actually English in this case), thus imparting an air of inverted cultural snobbery, and confronting their original American performers with virtually insoluble problems. For they are being asked not just to adapt themselves to an alien style but to travesty it; to assume outward flourishes which are only meaningful as a means of conveying a corresponding inner substance in works from which the substance has been carefully removed. (It has to go, as I have observed elsewhere in this issue, to make way for the songs which in this case are no compensation. They are immovably well-behaved, though far from well-bred and, though I never thought to find myself saying this, there are far too many of them.)

Here the cast is English, but not much the better for it. Three of the five have good, if gluey, singing-voices; the acting of the entire quintet runs an interesting gamut of “stylishness”. Skittishness is the mode most affected, particularly in the playing of loose women (in this kind of show all women are loose but none are actually sexy). When events take a broader turn, the higher orders prove to be good at strutting and bellowing, and the lower at winking, forelock-tugging knowingness. A mysterious valet, trapped between decks, settles for immobility, as well he might, living as he does with the knowledge that each night he must give vent to a ditty bearing the curious refrain “Hullabaloo belay”.

Small blame attaches to any of them (well, hardly any) but period comedy playing has taken such a heartening turn in the last few years that charades like this provoke more irritation than they deserve. Maybe the original play (by Ashley Dukes) is a mangled masterpiece, but such evidence as peeks through between the songs suggests only an irresponsible attempt to pass off a pointless story by decking it in fancy dress. There is the odd enjoyable moment (a company number called “Romance” gave me fleeting pleasure early on, though I thought they were pushing their luck by reprising it). At least—and this is the saving grace—a real human audience is being aimed at. I can't share their fun but I don't begrudge it.
