Jan 4, 1976

Return Of Annie Oakley

Robert Cushman · The Observer

IT IS always good to see an audience enjoying itself, and this is the main delight afforded by the Nottingham Playhouse production of Annie Get Your Gun.

It is cheering too to find that a major rep can fill its house with a modern classic. Actually revivals of the best post-war musicals have been a profitable commonplace for some time now; the regions have bristled with Kiss Me Kate and Guys and Dolls. But Annie is a comparative rarity, and I approached it warily.

The story goes that when somebody, during the show's original New York run, described it as old-fashioned, its composer, the imperturbable Irving Berlin, retorted: "That's right, a good old-fashioned smash." For him it certainly was; the score is justly considered his masterpiece. From "Doing What Comes Naturally" to "No Business Like Show Business" and on again to "They Say It's Wonderful," it seems to consist of nothing but hits.

But Annie is a comparative rarity, and I approached it warily. The Nottingham programme lists just one unfamiliar song-title, "I'll Share It All With You," whose obscurity must result from its being a holy terror to sing. Its complications, which happen very fast, are not what one expects of Mr Berlin: a reminder that this composer, so often taken for granted, in fact confounded expectation throughout his career.

Despite its technical originality, however, "I'll Share It All With You" is generally a very traditional kind of musical comedy number. It is a comedy love-song for the show's two second leads; there are numbers in Show Boat which fulfil exactly the same function. Annie came 20 years after Show Boat, but architecturally it has not advanced a step further. Modern enough to boast a story, it is still not sure what to do with it.

Annie Oakley, sharpest of shooters from the backwoods, becomes the heroine of a conventional backstage romance. She might, what is more, be back of just about any stage. (Not for nothing has "Show Business" become an all-purpose anthem.) Playhouse patrons, looking at the publicity and the programme covers, might be forgiven for thinking that they are off to see the Wild West show; but the book makes little of the ambience (beyond a few random jokes about Indians) and the production much less.

There is altogether a distressing baldness about the show at Nottingham; it consists of little more than the songs successively happening; and though they are enough to make a pleasant evening they also make an unsatisfying one. The script does remarkably little to set them up; the hero, Frank Butler, has hardly got his sights on Annie before he starts in about the girl that he marries having to be as pink and as white as a nurseree. I can imagine a production and performance so effervescent as to fill out the gaps, as the authors doubtless trusted they would; you can probably imagine it too; at the Playhouse you have to.

The most distinguished element of the evening is David Collis's open set, but this, though stark and stylish, is also a downer. It looks brilliantly economical; but brilliant economy is not really what one wants of a good old-fashioned smash. The one really joyous number, "I Got the Sun in the Morning," significantly deploys enough actors--virtually the entire cast--to obliterate the background. This, too, is the only point at which the band wakes up, spurred on perhaps by an arrangement which, like the tune itself, has not much to do with the Old West, but a great deal to do with swing. Annie is a forties musical and this song rejoices in the fact. It makes a lovely noise.

But this kind of volume and vivacity is noticeably absent from the other songs, most of them solos or duets for the leading couple. The show is essentially a duel for egomaniacs; shortcomings in staging, decor and choreography could all be redeemed by justified assurance from the performers. But John Price shambles apologetically through Frank Butler, his defences down from the start; and though Ursula Smith acts Annie rather well, this virtue, affecting only the dialogue, is of strictly limited use. Her voice is a throaty purr (at least it was last Monday), charming for a ballad like "I Got Lost in his Arms" (the best song in the score, incidentally, winding its melody up tight before releasing it), but fatally devoid of brass. So when the two of them square up to what should be a classic encounter ("anything you can do I can do better--no you can't--yes I can" and all that), nothing much happens.

The plot is interesting at this point though; Annie, having lost Frank by her irritating habit of outgunning him, purposely shoots below her best and gets him back. And this in a sex discrimination week: throw in the comic book Indians (a diminutive Sitting Bull must, to judge from his jokes, have been as much Jew as Sioux) and you have a stimulatingly reactionary entertainment.

*

The Oxford Playhouse, also in seasonal flight from tradition, has come up with an elaborate re-telling by Frank Marcus of Beauty and the Beast. Its comic diversions are mostly elephantine; a quasi-dame only just rescued by Hugh Paddick, a monstrously coy French fairy not rescued at all, a black postman who sings his messages. Others are potentially interesting: the postman is turned into a mountebank's symbol of the noble savage; this is obviously meant to illuminate the main plot, but it is difficult to see how. It comes over as filler; and the production has the heaviness which seems the hallmark of the new regime at Oxford.

But though Mr Marcus takes a long time to get to his real story, there is some magic in its eventual telling: in Terence Evans's bewilderment as Beauty's father lost in the Beast's castle and in the immaculate priggishness, not at all repellent, of Debbie Bowen's Beauty. There are her attempts to teach the Beast (Christopher Gable, piquant casting) to dance, and, most satisfyingly logical, her dismay when he turns into a prince. She misses his furriness.

*

In The Everest Hotel (Bush), Snoo Wilson transports three girl singers, rock-and-rolling in the service of the Lord and more particularly the Pope, from the Himalayas to Portugal by a route I found it difficult to follow. As usual Mr Wilson's writing ("what a life--you turn round and it's winter") is abrupt and kaleidoscopic; it is possible to see why those who like him like him very much. His own production is visually attractive in an off-hand way; but the casualness encouraged by the tiny theatre does little for clarity. The play seems to have two points: one is the performance of Janet Chappell, the other is faith, its desirability and its evanescence. Do not, however, quote me on that.
