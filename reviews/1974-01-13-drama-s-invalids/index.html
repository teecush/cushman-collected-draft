Jan 13, 1974

Drama's Invalids

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Drama's invalids

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

IN the theatre, even more perhaps than in life, we flinch from illness and from physical pain. This is not due merely to queasiness. Dramatically it seems an unprofitable subject.

It leaves the playwright and his characters nowhere to go; a cry of rage, and then what follows? There is rarely anyone to blame, and hence no conflict. The earliest, and probably the greatest, play on the subject - Sophocles's Philoctetes - rather neatly sidestepped the problem. The hero's poisoned foot causes him atrocious suffering, expressed in some harrowing lyrics, but he is also provided with human enemies. (His comrades, unable to bear the stink of his wound, have marooned him on an uninhabited island, so a large part of his fury can be directed at them.) Finally he qualifies, through heavenly intervention, as one of the few curable invalids in world drama.

His successors have generally been less fortunate; the luckiest were hypochondriacs. They have not been able to call on divinely infallible physicians, and the human substitutes available, though frequently efficient, have not always been sympathetic. The rise of institutional medicine has triggered off as much resentment as gratitude; the cleaner the surroundings, the more degrading illness can seem. This helpless rage at physical decay throbs, I think, beneath a play like Peter Nichols's The National Health, only occasionally breaking surface.

It bursts right through in David Rudkin's Ashes (Open Space), which concerns a married couple who, though not precisely sick, are still the victims of their own malfunctioning bodies. They are mysteriously childless; medical opinion puts the blame on the husband's insufficient sperm-count. Cold water, taken externally, is the prescription ("Hands up," says our beleaguered hero to the audience, "who's tried bathing his balls"); the treatment grows more complicated as the doctors consulted become more specialised. At last, after some reorientation of their sex-life (they never seem quite at ease with canine-type coupling), the miracle happens: triumphal music and a golden light on the victorious urine-sample. But it proves an ironic respite. The balance of humiliation now shifts to the wife: she miscarries, losing both babies (yes, she was carrying twins) and her womb as well.

That is some hard-luck story. Mr Rudkin makes it convincing by getting the domestic tone accurate. We believe in this couple - in their love, in their tolerance (or I should say in hers, since he is the one with the interesting neuroses) and in their rows: when she is ill, and he brings her breakfast in bed and spills the milk, her edgy martyred exasperation ("I'll eat the cornflakes dry") is exactly right. Hence we believe in their pain as well, and do not begrudge them their self-pity. (I see no reason why self-pity should never be regarded as justly earned.) Paranoia, however, is another matter; I first suspected it when the grandest of the doctors, known as the Guru, brushed aside a suggestion that the couple might give up all idea of propagation by smugly admonishing them of their duty to continue the species. This strain continues to be heard in the play and damages it: as the victims of their own humanity the characters are tragic, but their predicament shrinks when it is blamed on bland officials or patronising relatives, however exasperating they may be. The limitations of the theme strike us all over again.

Much of Mr Rudkin's language is also, as the leader-writers say, grossly inflationary. Both protagonists soliloquise to excess; Peter McEnery and Lynn Farleigh play desperately well together in Pam Brighton's production, but turned loose on a monologue she congeals, searching too hard for natural pauses. He is more successful in riding the rhetoric, particularly at the end when he tells of a journey home. Home happens to be in Belfast, where his uncle has been killed by a bomb. Mr McEnery's long evocation of the battered city and his despair at its dedication to future bloodshed are as moving as anything in O'Casey. They have, however, no visible connection with the rest of the play except that they artificially increase the husband's isolation. He is now a two-way orphan: no future, no past.

I should add that there is a rumour going around the Open Space that the whole play is a metaphor for the Ulster situation. (Unnatural conceptions and bloody miscarriages: it might fit.) Since this rumour reached my ears only after I had seen the performance (a preview) on Monday, I cannot say how the play would stand up to detailed inspection from this angle. You might like to go along and try it.

Sizwe Bansi is Dead (Royal Court) will withstand inspection from any angle. Since this production is already legend may I register one small reservation? John Kani's opening monologue is too long. His devastating account of a day in a South African Ford factory is justly famous and a necessary introduction to the play's treatment of apartheid. But when Mr Kani, as Styles the photographer, began to re-enact his most memorable sessions I got restless. I was anxious, remembering The Island from the previous week, for Winston Ntshona to enter, both for his own sake and for that of the partnership.

On neither score was I disappointed. Mr Ntshona, posing for his photograph as a man of the world with a pipe in one hand and a cigarette in the other, is at first sweet, shy and bewildered. Later his bewilderment intensifies in the face of the damning stamp in his pass-book (which, by the way, as he reiterates in the most resonant line of the evening, he cannot read); then it burgeons into a towering assertion of his own manhood and finally settles into a mature hopefulness, infinitely qualified. Meanwhile the mockery that seethed behind Mr Kani's Louis Armstrong grin is released into the full bitterness of urban experience, painful to acquire but almost unfairly exhilarating to watch.

Messrs Kani and Ntshona devised the entertainment with their director, Athol Fugard. If we are to regard it as his play it is his best; there may be too many lines, but there is not a single false one. And truthfully, you have never seen such acting.
