Jul 10, 1983

Pleasant Warmpth

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Pleasant warmpth

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a musical--and an opera.

I ALWAYS approach Fiddler on the Roof (revived at the Apollo Victoria) with apprehension: something you may put down to a phenomenon known as Jewish anti-Semitism.

Knowing the territory from the inside, I dread being smothered in sentiment; and nothing looks more sentimental in prospect than a Broadway musical whose theme-song is called Tradition, whose setting is a Jewish village in Tsarist Russia, whose chief character is an orthodox patriarch with the endearing habit of chatting up the Deity, and whose narrative runs to three love-stories, one of them culminating in an onstage wedding.

I should have learned by now. Fiddler is an immensely powerful show. Its climax, as must be generally known, is the eviction of the villagers from their home of Anatevka. Many, including our hero, Tevye the dairyman, set off for America. Among the things created by their descendants was the American musical, of which Fiddler on the Roof is one of the culminating achievements. In a sense the show is its own happy ending. It obviously had to be written sometime; that it was written and staged so well is a blessing.

I can think of only three and a half things to hold against the show. (1) In accordance with Broadway and Hollywood tradition, the word Jew is hardly mentioned; I am not sure whose susceptibilities this is meant to protect. (2) The Russian authorities are amazingly gentlemanly, and their idea of a pogrom is to knock over a couple of tables; the average family regularly inflicts more damage on itself. (3) Some tiresome gossip scenes pad out the second act.

My half complaint concerns the divided nature of Jerry Bock's score. The Hebraic part is fine and stirring; the rest is conventional 1960s Broadway. The matchmaker song is really a jazz waltz, and what would Anatevka know of jazz or indeed of waltzes? But it is a delightful example of a delightful form, so who's counting.

Joseph Stein's book is a remarkable piece of craftsmanship. The affairs of Tevye's three marriageable daughters are cunningly overlapped, with the larger drama kept looming behind them. Take for example the wedding song, Sunrise Sunset. It crystallises the present moment; it enables the parents to recall the past; and it sets the next couple dreaming of their own future.

It also presents a piece of ritual that will survive, but whose significance is about to be altered by both history and geography. A young radical is brought on to criticise his elders' stoic acceptance of their fate; he is obviously right, but the authors are not out to attack their grandparents. They are concerned to record and to admire. They present history; something the musical--a broad form--can do without embarrassment.

I once saw a film cartoon about a singer whose manager despaired of his lack of emotion; "What you need," he kept crying, "is warmpth." Fiddler obviously has plenty of warmpth, but it is not stifling; Tevye never forgives the daughter who has married out. The show's most memorable image is of his rejection of her, a slowed version of his characteristic movement: arms upstretched and bent, belly forward, legs astride, everything swaying, tradition.

Topol has, over the years, grown right into the role; he makes every gag and every monologue a part of the man. The rest of the cast are ordinary, and Maria Charles as the matchmaker embodies one's worst fears of the show as a whole.

In many ways the revival is tacky; in the giant pavilion of rip-off that is the Apollo Victoria, Boris Aronson's Chagallian sets look panto-mime. But Jerome Robbins's choreography retains its glory: this is not obviously a dance-musical, but scene after scene explodes into motion. And the bottle-dance--four men waddling at a wedding--is a wonder of the modern stage.

*

I have had a musical week. I went to Mozart's Idomeneo as a treat (my first time at Glyndebourne), but I can justify it as a means of keeping tabs on Trevor Nunn, making his debut as an opera director. Maybe I am projecting my own reaction on to him, but I felt him becoming increasingly excited by the story (more perhaps than he had expected to be), increasingly delighted with the problems of telling it.

Faced with a line-up of self-sacrificing Ancient Greeks, expressing themselves in monologue and duologue, he found a Racinian severity and--in Philip Langridge and Carol Vaness--Racinian passion. This figure has little in common with his recent theatre work, which has tended towards overflow, but it recalls his studio Macbeth in its use of simple techniques to gain terrifying effects.

The sea-monster is represented by a huge black shadow, encroaching from the front stalls. It sweeps over the huddled crowd on stage, to be succeeded by a black and menacing white light. Drama directors are often accused of sacrificing musical values, but I have never listened to a score more intently. I have certainly never been so held by recitative. The Japaneserie of this production has been over-stressed; many of its scenic motifs are authentically Cretan.

*

Stiff upper-lippery returns with The Winslow Boy (Lyric, Hammersmith), Rattigan's meticulously well-made play about the cadet accused of theft and expelled from a naval academy out of which he seems to be well out. It still grips, less from its plot-mechanics than from its determination to be fair, to subject even professions of decency to decent scrutiny.

The Winslow boy's close examination by the eminent barrister plays less well when you know the upshot, but Ian Hogg is ferociously lionised, the least silky of silks. Buried in it all is the portrait of a marriage; Barbara Jefford is beautifully unobtrusive at the start, beautifully obtrusive as both her doubts and her excitement increase. Winslow pere, who sacrifices many of his family prospects to his quest for justice, might be a spikier personage than Alan MacNaughtan presents, but what he does he does finely.

The play, I think, comes down on his side, but it is to its credit that it never stops worrying. All that worries me is that it has--as John Whiting said of one of his own plays--no private parts: none of the torments of The Browning Version, and after. Michael Rudman directs it; maybe it takes an American to make this so emphatically a play for England.

Arnold Wesker has never lacked warmpth. His Annie Wobbler (Birmingham Rep Studio) is, in a modest way, loaded with it. It is a sequence of three monologues for ladies who share the author's initials. The fullest and, after a disconcerting start, the subtlest, is for a lady novelist who knows she should have opinions, but is not sure what they are.

Nichola McAuliffe, a young actress with a robust talent for self-satire (and also, though it is not used here, a robust singing voice), plays all roles; she starts with competent senile impersonation, goes on to a notable appearance in black undies, and a ginger wig, and ends stark and stunning as the scribe.

I may return next week to Undrowned (Prince of Wales), which shows mayhem on the District Line. If you're planning to be murdered on a train, better take the Orient Express and go out in style.
