Updating Tartuffe

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Updating Tartuffe

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

The Victorians may not always have practised what they preached, but the fact that they did preach, and expected to be listened to, makes the nineteenth century a useful and agreeable refuge for any director seeking to update a classic comedy. Moral issues become clear-cut; and the treatment works particularly well for Tartuffe. For a British cast the release from seventeenth-century wigs and gowns is a blessing; they look as if they are activating a play, rather than stumbling through a charade.

David Thompson's production of Tartuffe at Greenwich is the best I have seen, providing the players with a congenial context and letting them get on with it. His translation is notable for strength rather than glitter; couplets are kept for special clinching occasions such as Orgon's eulogy of Tartuffe: "And since I've given him his bed and board/The moral climate in this house has soared."

The play accordingly becomes both funnier and more serious than it seemed in the elaborate Roger Planchon TNP production, which we saw a few weeks ago at the National Theatre. I was admonished by one or two readers for assuming in my review of the Planchon version that these two qualities, in Molière as in all great comic writers, must necessarily go together. Tartuffe, I was told, is a play far above such considerations as laughter, though Molière doubtless threw in a few gags for the incurably frivolous. This reaction was anticipated in the play itself by the sternly puritanical Madame Pernelle, who crushingly remarks that some people will laugh at anything; indeed they will, and Molière, who could laugh at her and at Tartuffe, was one of them.

The characters for once make up a recognisable household (British actors in this play usually look equal and anonymous) with unusual prominence given to the forces of reason; Denys Hawthorne gets an excellent hearing for the rational Cléante, Freda Dowie's Dorine is promoted from maid to spinster governess and it is made plain that she represents the biggest threat to Orgon's complacency; "she's turning my world upside down," he complains.

Ewan Hooper's Orgon has been built up from the text's few references to his distinguished military record; he becomes a Bible-punching general (rather like Sir Henry Havelock) adrift in civilian life, a little afraid of his young family and an easy mark for Tartuffe. The end of the first half finds them on their knees together, a disturbing reminder of Othello and Iago at the end of their temptation scene.

Leonard Rossiter's Tartuffe, from his first command to his servant ("you may put away my hair-shirt"), is bland and unreachable, immovable even in defeat. Only in his attempted seduction of Elmire does emotion seep out, and even here lust wrapped in self-satisfaction preserves a kind of sobriety. He is partnered by Ursula Mohan, demurely anxious as she warns her husband that she may have to lead Tartuffe on a little, "perhaps quite a long way."

The officer who brings rescue at the end is now a Wilkie Collins policeman, just a bit too nudgingly reassuring, so that Dorine's exclamation "perhaps the world just sometimes does make sense" retains its proper irony. Mr Thompson has in fact furnished his version with a built-in commentary: unnecessary perhaps in a perfect world, but much appreciated in ours.

*

Harold Brighouse wrote other plays besides Hobson's Choice, though on the evidence of Zack (Royal Exchange, Manchester) which came a year later, not so very other. This is another play about a turning worm. Zack, like Willie Mossop, is an exploited young man made aware of his own talents by the attentions of a determined young woman. This, the starting-point of Hobson's Choice, is the whole action of Zack, since the girl is less sure of herself, and a slower operator, than Maggie Hobson: though from the moment half-way through that she removes his glasses (a beauty treatment Hollywood was to appropriate for mousy heroines rather than cart-horse heroes) the end is apparent.

What the play loses in narrative grip, it gains in character; Zack ("I'm not as frequent at the soap as I might be") is drawn in more detail than his predecessor, Willie Mossop, and Trevor Peacock, though he can hardly help being huggable, never sentimentalises him; his is a long, slow awakening. His restraint is matched by that of Patricia Routledge as his bullying mother (Zack being, seasonally enough, a male Cinderella) and Cheryl Kennedy ties the package prettily together as his rescuer. The problems of the Exchange's arena stage are still not fully solved, but Edwardian Manchester realism takes to it better than the more stylistically demanding plays with which it opened.

*

The least-known of the week's three revived comedies is the most simply delightful; I doubt if any audience has been more surprised by joy than that for Wild Oats (Aldwych). The author, John O'Keeffe--a prolific stalwart of the late eighteenth century--does not make a good initial impression. Two young seafaring gentlemen are entrusted with the unfolding of the plot and the very best endeavours of Norman Rodway and Joe Melia foundered on the timber-shivering dialogue.

But much choicer jargon is in store; for the hero, though he will eventually prove to be someone's long-lost heir, is at present an actor whose profession has likewise gone to his head. The writing of his part is a tour de force of shameless plagiarism; if a storm breaks, he falls into Lear. Alan Howard, putting some of his own more orotund performances into quotation-marks, makes a great thing of him (though he does unflatteringly suggest that Rover has in his time made Hamlet, Coriolanus, Edgar, Richard III and Peter Quince sound the same) and whips the play into life.

In continuing the age-old war between players and Puritans (see Tartuffe, supra), O'Keeffe placed his hero among Quakers. One of them remains his inveterate enemy; he is called Ephraim Smooth, and in Patrick Godfrey's performance he lives up to his name; more can hardly be said. His fury ("this mansion is now a Temple of Baal") is matched by the delight of his young and very rich patroness, Olivia, to his Malvolio, who is easily inveigled into appearing as Rosalind in a home-grown As You Like It. Lisa Harrow plays Judy Garland to Mr Howard's Gene Kelly with charm and witty economy. The same qualities characterise Clifford Williams's production all the way to a remarkably happy ending.

*

I Gotta Shoe (Criterion) is likewise full of quotations: the songs of the celebrated Trad (in his black American guise) fit surprisingly well into a cabaret-style retelling of the Cinderella story (again). Archness threatens, but is more or less kept at bay. It makes a nasty mess, though, of the first half of the evening, a self-styled revue in which four performers (the fifth, Elisabeth Welch, condemned by laryngitis on the first night to be a beneficent but mainly silent presence) offer new black showbiz clichés for old. Some classic songs (Gershwin and a Harold Arlen medley) briefly relieve some newer stuff with lyrics (by Caryl Brahms and Ned Sherrin) too precious for this world.
