Mar 28, 1976

The Pure In Heart

Robert Cushman · The Observer

This week's musical is quite the most feathery and unperturbed of shows. In the escapist musicals of the twenties and thirties, some disturbance--a couple of gangsters, say, or a team of fortune hunters--was generally admitted to menace the well-being of the virtuous. But Very Good Eddie belongs to an earlier age when it seems the pure in heart had things entirely to themselves. It was first staged in New York in 1915, though the programme at the Piccadilly dates the action as two years earlier. Even in its cradle, it seems, the show relied on period charm.

It may have taken 60 years to cross the Atlantic, but of late it has been a fast mover. Last July it was revived in Connecticut at a place called, prophetically, Goodspeed. A few months later, David Merrick had it on Broadway. A few months more, and we have it here. At least five other current New York shows--all of them, on the face of it, demanding importation more urgently than this one--are still struggling to get through Customs. But then, they are heavier laden.

Very Good Eddie fits into the current West End scene with twofold neatness; it is light and it is old. To take the age first: I suspect that the omnipresence of revivals in the West End owes less to audience nostalgia than to managerial neurosis. No sane impresario could doubt that there is still a profit to be made on new plays, but how to choose them? Anyone can make a mistake, and play-readers doubtless make more than most. It is obviously safer to unearth a proven success from the past, even if you forfeit not only the good opinion of the critics but any chance of the big money. Please note that even in the last couple of years no old play has run as long as the new ones of Alan Ayckbourn and Simon Gray. The only revival that looks like challenging their longevity is Murder at the Vicarage, whose popularity has nothing to do with its age. I doubt if its audiences notice anything old-fashioned about it; it is timelessly terrible.

Very Good Eddie moves on a higher plane, so its survival cannot be guaranteed. It is a work of composite authorship: one composer, one librettist, and nine lyricists, among whom Schuyler Greene may be credited with the hardest work, Elsie Janis with the neatest joke, and P. G. Wodehouse with the most famous name. Wodehouse turns out to have been an early rhymer of "life" with "care and strife," but otherwise his "Bungalow in Quogue" (pronounced Quog) is one of the best numbers celebrating innocence with a touch of aerated fantasy. His colleagues are generally content just to smile, but at least their scansion is irreproachable.

The music is early Jerome Kern: post "They Didn't Believe Me," but pre everything else. It has plenty of grace but--like much of Kern's work before his brilliant flowering in the thirties--it lacks spice. "Good Night Boat" has a suave lilt and "I've Got to Dance" prefigures the Kern who wrote for Fred Astaire. It's good to have neglected songs in circulation (the real justification for productions like this one usually is the original cast recording), but at a first hearing these tunes, blandly arranged, are disappointing. They sound, in truth, strangely English.

Some of them have been interpolated from other Kern shows of the period: a succession of entertainments which were praised, in a period of inhuman extravaganzas, for their modesty and wit. The modesty is now more in evidence than the wit, and it is well preserved in Bill Gile's production, which itself contrasts refreshingly with the overblown style in which other old musicals have been revived. The performers never patronise their material, but it is difficult for the audience to avoid doing so. One wonders how they coped in 1915; did they too find it deliciously simple-minded, did they take it for real or for granted?

Nothing dates faster than realism in musicals (consider South Pacific) and the proper objection to Guy Bolton's script is not its lack of credibility but its lack of energy; it is half-hearted farce. The verbal humour is, on a strict count, awful; but it is the kind of awfulness that a bright vaudevillian--John Blythe in this case--can make irresistible. Mr Blythe's professionalism puts you in touch with the ages.

There is a deal of charm around, but it rarely makes any dramatic point. In fact it does so twice in the final revelation that the title of the show actually means something, and in a dance-duet which Richard Freeman and Prue Clarke turn into an enchantingly fleet-footed demonstration of falling in love. They play (if you can bear to hear the plot at this stage) two short people apparently condemned to marry two tall people. This is taken to be a particularly alarming kind of miscegenation; I don't know that I agree. Miss Clarke apart, the women in the show are inferior to the men. (What can be done about the British chorine?) Robert Swann is engaging as a chap who was at Yale with all the other chaps and who sings two songs, one at each end of the show, and both saying exactly the same thing. Act One takes place on a boat and Act Two in a honeymoon hotel. There you have plot and atmosphere.

*

Patrick Hamilton's Gaslight has both, but atmosphere is the more important. The plot is a drawn-out anecdote; what counts is Hamilton's relish for the details of his Victorian setting: the murk, the orotund speech, and the studied cruelty, class and connubial. Most of this is scuppered in the Criterion revival. There are three major roles, all radically miscast. A man is attempting to drive his wife mad; she fears and worships him. Anton Rodgers offers bloated sulks for calculated murderous caprice. His victim is Nicola Pagett, unalterably balanced and self-reliant; in the horrible final scene, where the worm turns, she has no reversal to make, and merely displays an anxiety to get the play over as soon as possible. An avuncular policeman, who adjures her to look into his face for comfort, is played by the greasily menacing Peter Vaughan.

*

Sur Le Fil (Greenwood, London Bridge) is the first Arrabal play I have seen which amounts to more than a heap of self-regarding neuroses. Usually his directors indulge him; Jorge Lavelli keeps him on a tight lead and, miraculously, he ceases to whoop, or to whimper. His dramatic landscape takes on a sculptural reality--a ghost town, the end of the line--excellently realised in Max Bignens's set, and in the performances of Daniel Ivernel, Pierre Constant and Georges Chorafas. Poise is not a quality I expected to grant an Arrabal work, but here it is (M. Constant walking the tightrope of the title) in abundance.
