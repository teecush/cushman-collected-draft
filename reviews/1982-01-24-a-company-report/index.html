Jan 24, 1982

A Company Report

Robert Cushman · The Observer

A company report

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN muses on the RSC and the National.

A few weeks ago I rhapsodised unconscionably about Shakespeare as the guiding light and salvation of the company that bears his name. I had artistic standards on my mind, but it frequently occurs to me, when musing as critics do in idle moments with a column to fill on the respective qualities of the RSC and the National Theatre, that the Stratford bunch also derives from their favourite son considerable administrative advantages.

The Stratford season gets longer every time and now stretches beyond 31 December into the following year, but it has so far avoided bumping into the one in front. It's possible to talk about a Stratford season, a finite one, with the same actors present throughout. They then proceed to the Aldwych (it is still just possible to write that in the present tense) and play another finite season. They know in short who they are and for how long; and so, more importantly, do we. Both parties can take a breath occasionally.

Compare the National, with its three auditoria open all the year round, staffed by actors on a contract system that doubtless makes sense to the organisation but baffles everyone outside. The result is that, despite long service from various individual players, the National has never, since arriving on the South Bank, had a recognisable company.

I attach no magical connotations to the word 'company': to me it means simply something that can be supported like a football team. It is no guarantee of good work; the National, with its ad hoc methods, may even have had the edge this past year. But what is missing is the promise in a good production that another one will follow.

Even when continuity exists, it is dissipated. Last year Peter Gill arrived with his own group who appeared in three shows; those actors then departed, and it is as if they had never been. Among the press of plays in three theatres, they hardly registered; anyway, they were at best one small company among several. The RSC at any given moment is two companies, one in Stratford and one in London, but the distances are great enough for them to get away with it. With three stages under the same roof the National's lack of unanimity is very visible.

I gather that the National's plan for the coming year is to have individual groups under individual directors, but for each to work in all three theatres; that seems a step in the right direction. Certainly on paper the National's list of associate directors is extraordinary: Hall, Gill, Rudman, Bogdanov, Eyre, Pinter, Schlesinger, Bryden, Morahan, Wood. What is interesting is that every one of them (with the possible exception of Bill Bryden) was a known metropolitan quantity before arriving there.

If you inspect the RSC's roster you find that apart from the guru figure of Peter Brook everyone on it--from Trevor Nunn to Adrian Noble--was virtually unknown before arriving in Stratford. Probably they all fight like hellhounds. They have had the same grounding and it shows. And if the RSC seems almost neurotically reluctant to hire star actors from outside, the National seems to find it difficult to hire anyone else.

Both are inevitably bemused by star writers; the RSC last year gave an inert Solzhenitsyn, and the National a feeble Sam Shepard. The latter though may be part of a constructive reappraisal of American drama; having rehabilitated Arthur Miller, they proceed to dispose of exalted claims for Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? and to lay the Shepard myth, I should think, forever. If I had to choose one National production from last year it would be the generally snubbed Ticket-of-Leave Man; with the RSC I would be tempted by All's Well That Ends Well, which lived up to its name in finally raising the tone of the Stratford main house, but would give the nod to almost anything at the Other Place.

Despite this miniature theatre's high standards (here if anywhere is the proof that other playwrights can be Shakespeare's beneficiaries) I think it represents a threat, or perhaps a capitulation. Nobody puts on a play in a small theatre if he thinks he can get an audience for it in a large one. The prestige of the Warehouse and the Other Place, like the studios in the big reps, is a tacit admission of a shrinking public. Occasionally a production comes along that would only work in a small space; like the Nunn Macbeth, which proved it the hard way by dying briefly in the big one. It is economics, not aesthetics, that keep the others from transferring.

I believe in large theatres and large audiences, the more heterogeneous the better. Community theatre--whether for bankers or factory workers--is another admission of defeat. I have never understood the scornful phrase, 'consumer theatre.' It seems to me the only honest approach: here is the show, come and get it. Whoever you are. That is why the West End--a place nobody lives in, but everybody visits--is worth preserving, and why the RSC's departure from it will be a sad blow.

There is a new community theatre in Deptford, or rather a new version of an old one. The Albany Empire, burned down and (by the Arts Council) turned down, nevertheless got itself rebuilt on a different site at a cost of £3 million. I know this because the performers in the initial show, All Who Sail In Her, keep mentioning it. They seem as far as I could follow some turgid dialogue turgidly delivered to feel guilty about the money and half-inclined to give it back, instead of (as they presumably did in real life) saying thank you and getting on with the job.

This is a breakthrough in theatrical self-pity, and a more inbred entertainment than anything the most highly-subsidised theatre would dare contemplate. The actors talk about the show they should be giving instead of giving it; I fled at half time. I had a few regrets; I like cafe theatre and this space--a cross between the King's Head and the Talk Of The Town--is very pleasant; Keith Allen is an extremely bright comic; and Debby Bishop makes her songs sound good, though she still confuses acting with belligerence.

Behind the Lines (Tricycle) is a poetry double act by Roger McGough and Brian Patten. Mr McGough is in the Spike Milligan holy fool tradition: for love and against the bomb. As a performer he has turned unassumingness into a style; Mr Patten, at a preview, had not.
