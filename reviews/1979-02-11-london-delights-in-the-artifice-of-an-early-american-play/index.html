London Delights In The Artifice Of An Early American Play

Robert Cushman · The New York Times

London Delights in the Artifice of an Early American Play

By ROBERT CUSHMAN

LONDON

Eight years ago the Royal Shakespeare Company, at London's Aldwych Theater, struck out in a new and -- as it proved -- profitable line. The RSC unearthed a forgotten comedy by the Victorian playwright Dion Boucicault entitled "London Assurance," and the title seemed an apt description of the style with which the company brought the play to the stage. The production ran the entire season at the Aldwych, played for a further year in the West End, and finally came to New York -- where it lasted a mere couple of weeks.

At home, though, "London Assurance" was regarded as a landmark for the RSC. Its success encouraged the company to cultivate what had previously been an underdeveloped sense of humor, and to indulge in further archaeological exploits. The RSC next resurrected the William Gillette version of Arthur Conan Doyle's "Sherlock Holmes," and this time had as great a triumph with it in America as at home. The RSC then went further back in time to "Wild Oats," a 1791 comedy of intrigue by an Irishman, John O'Keeffe. This was perhaps the biggest success of all. Opening at the Aldwych in 1976, "Wild Oats" ran in London for the next year and a half, and is about to be revived.

Now the RSC has revived another rarity, this time of American origin. "Saratoga" dates from 1870 and was the second play of Bronson Howard, who was later to earn the title of "Dean of the American Drama." Like its Aldwych predecessors it is a stock piece; the characters are types, and are blatantly proclaimed as such in the cast list. The hero, played by Dennis Waterman, is the traditional susceptible rake. Howard, with the 19th-century love of a pun (especially one with literary pretensions) describes him "One Who Loved Not Wisely But Four Well."

We meet three of the hero's four loves in the first act, which is set in a New York art gallery. They number one very short girl, one very tall one and a medium-sized widow. We learn that all three of these ladies, with their respective families, are off to take the waters at the newly fashionable Saratoga Springs. For the rest of the evening we join them at the spa, as does the hero, Robert, now on the trail of his fourth flame, a beautiful young lady chaperoned by a distinguished gentleman whom Robert takes to be her father but who is, in fact, her husband.

Halfway through the play, Robert's quadruple life catches up with him; each of his conquests learns of her competitors' existence and each calls on her menfolk to vindicate her honor. What with fathers, husbands, stepsons, and just plain swains, Robert finds himself the object of no fewer than seven challenges. (The play's subtitle is "Pistols for Seven.") Naturally he ends up more or less unscathed and in possession of his number-one girl, with consolation prizes provided for the others.

"Saratoga" is obviously an artificial piece, but its artifice is the key to the pleasure it provides. The RSC has never mocked its resurrected comedies -- it has always approached them too affectionately for that. Their style rests on a complicity between actors and audience. The RSC enjoys playing old tricks, and we enjoy watching them at it; each side of the footlights humors the other and each feels, atavistically, that in these routines resides the most basic kind of theatrical appeal.

This archaeological playfulness is at its most elaborate in Ronald Eyre's production of "Saratoga." Mr. Eyre was the director who started the whole cycle with his production of "London Assurance" -- a text on which he carried out extensive doctoring. He has not done this with "Saratoga"; rather, he has emphasized its period origin. Jocelyn Herbert's sets are an elegant series of cut-outs that provide an explicit, affectionate comment on the 19th-century stage itself. Mr. Eyre has also arranged a series of divertissements between the acts; the three black waiters who figure in the play are brought on, rather like the porters in "On the Twentieth Century," to sing and strut between the acts. Their best number, the misanthropic Bert Williams classic "Nobody" (in which they are joined by Mr. Waterman), brings down the house.

It is the undoubted highlight of the show, though there are passages of more than routine interest in the play itself. The first act in particular reflects Howard's own position as an American playwright wishing to strike out in a new and native direction but still in thrall to the European comedy of manners and intrigue.

Previous RSC revivals have been built around star performances: Donald Sinden and Judi Dench in "London Assurance," John Wood in "Sherlock Holmes," Alan Howard in "Wild Oats." "Saratoga" suffers by comparison, since Mr. Waterman's acting here is -- except in that one interlude of loose-limbed hoofing -- curiously tense. But there are enough high spirits and good humor all around to carry the evening.

Robert Cushman is theater critic of the London Observer.
