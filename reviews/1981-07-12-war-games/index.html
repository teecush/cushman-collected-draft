Jul 12, 1981

War Games

Robert Cushman · The Observer

War games

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Stratford.

THERE ARE two ways of delivering the Prologue to Troilus and Cressida, and they determine the tone of a production. One is to ring out the polysyllabic proper names. The other is to undercut the heroics by stressing the absurdity of the entire Trojan War: 'The ravish'd Helen. Menelaus' Queen/With wanton Paris sleeps, and that's the quarrel?'

Terry Hands at the Aldwych takes the second course, though he takes the rough irony rather than the smooth: inevitably, since he gives the prologue to Thersites. Melia's summary of the action ('all wars and lechery') would convince any audience. He remains with us to the end, cringing on a battlefield otherwise occupied by an armed cadaver (Mr Hands has never been shy of symbolism) and Pandarus, who delivers his farewell speech while impaled on barbed wire.

Mr Hands has set his production in a time-warp. His Trojans are medieval-classical, his Greeks World War I, conferring on mud-caked fields and playing mournfully on mouth-organs. Farrah's design spans the aeons, via a majestically bronzed Trojan escarpment; wings swing up and down as they used to in the Wars of the Roses, and a pair of slatted frames lead us in and out of domestic scenes. We always know where we are, and--thanks to Mr Hands' extreme care with groupings and solo spotlights--who is who. This is the RSC's most lucid and exciting 'Troilus' since the famous 'sandpit' production.

Carol Royle's sportive Cressida establishes herself from the start as that most dangerous commodity, a knowing and flirtatious virgin. She convinces both in love and in betrayal; but she is also a victim. A moment in which she is posed alone against a united masculine front of Troilus and Pandarus reminds us how the cards are stacked against her. Nature cannot have intended Tony Church for camp comedy, but he still makes a fine, flexible Pandar. Two normally obscure warriors spring into life: Richard Cordery's blockish cuckold Menelaus and Pip Miller's Diomedes, seducer and cynical commentator. In the higher echelons, Bruce Purchase's Hector, Trevor Baxter's Agamemnon and Oliver Ford-Davies's Nestor all strike a proper balance between the sublime and the ridiculous.

Some of their gentler moments are achieved, it seemed to me, in the teeth of the production. Despite his proclaimed belief in Shakespearean plasticity, Mr Hands is apt--especially in comedy--to strike a single note very hard. Here he is out to rub the generals' faces in the mud; Nestor is particularly close to it, here, bent double under his shield. This is to remind us that Nestor is very old, a fact the text would hardly let us forget: any more than we would fail to notice, even without the face-paint, Hector vandalising everything not nailed down, that Ajax is dangerously vain. And when Pandarus turns to the audience to describe love as 'a generation of vipers' we ought really to yell back that we are quite capable of noticing that for ourselves, and would he kindly get on with his business.

More damagingly, John Carlisle shouts monotonously through the great speeches of Ulysses. Now it's perfectly true that Ulysses's 'policy,' like Troilus's love and Hector's chivalry, proves useless, but it should be given its due.

On which matter, Mr Hands has carried over from John Barton's 12-year-old production the conception of a preening berouged Achilles. The actor is David Suchet, who can cope with anything. But though this reactionary equation of homosexuality with decadence once thrilled the critics, it destroyed the company's credibility with everyone else for nearly a decade. It's hardly worth reviving now. Finally, James Hazeldine is a gruff, unmoving Troilus. The last battle, though, is superbly sustained; you come out feeling you have eaten a masterpiece.

*

'What made you act like this?' someone asks Krogstad, the blackmailer, in Ibsen's A Doll's House (Other Place, Stratford). The answer seems to be the director, Adrian Noble, who has drawn transforming performances from his entire cast. He has woken up Marjorie Bland, nervously alive as the confidante, Mrs Linde; quietened down Bernard Lloyd, whose Krogstad is an anxious schemer, not a gloater; and let us see, in John Franklyn Robbins's dying, frustrated Dr Rank, the lineaments of despair.

He has also drawn out to the utmost physical extent Nora's near-seduction of the doctor (tormenting him with the feel of her silk stockings), the shoulder-stroking uxoriousness of her husband Torvald invested by Stephen Moore with a passionate narrowness that never seeks easy laughs, and the Tarantella, in which Cheryl Campbell's whirling golden tresses almost consume the stage. This is a very sexy play; 'Hedda Gabler,' which it foreshadows in numberless details, is merely the de-gutted version.

Nora's revolt (known in New York 10 years ago as the 'right on' section) no longer seems detachable. It is the logical outcome of the battering she takes from the earlier intrigues; and Miss Campbell constructs a magnificent passion at bridge. Her determination visibly emerges from bewilderment which lifts only when she declares--realising it as she speaks--that she doesn't love her husband. It's a pole-axing moment, because we, like him, assume that in an eight-year marriage there must be something; there are amazingly few plays about the breakdown of a relationship, and this seems the greatest. We long for the final slamming of the door as a relief from what has become almost intolerable tension.

*

Of the four Jacobethan plays given this year by the RSC and National, three have already been done at the Mermaid, where the adventurousness of the repertoire is only matched by the carelessness of the presentation; the reopening production, Eastward Ho!, is typical. A barbed city comedy--by Jonson, Chapman and Marston--has been turned into a standard romp, interrupted by musical numbers.

The appropriate musical style would be super-Bart; Nick Bicat offers sub-Sondheim, with rock implications. This works just once--when the mock-repentant apprentice Quicksilver (a shaven, spectral Richard O'Brien) takes off into a born-again cantata. Howard Schuman's lyrics match the thrust of the dialogue, but not its style, and include the remarkable couplet, 'If you wed a man of rectitude/he'll grow to be a horse's neck to you.'

In War with the Newts (Riverside--appropriately) there is an actor called Andy Rashleigh, who can swim underwater and impersonate David Bellamy. These two ingredients--water and mimicry--are the chief attractions of this Ken Campbell production, based (distantly, I imagine) on a Capek novel. Three green-suited actors are kept continually afloat as salamanders, who threaten to take over the world. The threat is never really felt though, Mr Campbell being more concerned with fun than the story. Fun it is, though less than seems promised.
