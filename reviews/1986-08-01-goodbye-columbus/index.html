Goodbye Columbus

Robert Cushman · The Guardian

Goodbye Columbus

Robert Cushman goes to the Ritz to meet a new master of the classic show tune, Michael Feinstein

"IF YOU'RE interested in show music you don't have much outlet in Columbus, Ohio," said Michael Feinstein, over tea in his suite at the Ritz.

This boy from Columbus is the latest in the line of propagandists for the classic show tune to play late-night cabaret in the Ritz dining-room. He wears a tuxedo, plays piano, and sings Porter, Coward and (his speciality) Gershwin. He is not a leathery survivor of the golden-age 1930s. He is 29, and has been a full-time entertainer for only four years. Before that he was an archivist; in a way he still is.

He will be at the Ritz until August 29 after a record-breaking season at the Algonquin Hotel in New York, a trail previously blazed by Julie Wilson and Steve Ross. They share a repertoire, and a standardised sartorial elegance, but their styles are different. Feinstein does not have Julie Wilson's ashen theatrical intensity or the Grande-dame slyness of the late Mabel Mercer or the fizz and drive of Bobby Short. He does share with Ross an appealing boyishness of voice and manner. Ross is the eternal Princeton man, a vocal Scott Fitzgerald. Feinstein suggests more the bright scholarship kid from the Bronx. He has yet to find his own style. In the meantime his strengths are enthusiasm, musicianship and knowledge.

Father sang as an amateur with local bands; mother had been a tap-dancer. When he was five they bought a piano. "I started playing it by ear -- to everyone's amazement. I played Do Re Mi from The Sound of Music. My mother said, 'When did your father teach you that?'; I said I'd taught myself, and got sent to my room for lying."

His contemporaries of course were listening to rock, and he grew up "a lonely, isolated child" which only pushed him further into show music. By the time he was 10 he knew who George Gershwin was, and what he'd written; the rest of the pantheon followed. He had to know who had written what, and for which musical. He still collects this information, and details it in performance. This sets him apart from most singers, who don't know anything.

He began his opening show at the Ritz with a statement of purpose: a song called Wanna Sing A Show Tune. I thought this self-conscious -- I'd rather he'd just gone ahead and done it -- but there is something to be said for a lyric that rhymes "stylish" with "Kurt Weillish." But then he was on to a totally forgotten antiquity called China (America's congratulations to the old country on becoming a democracy) that was captivating, and made me wish he would do more comedy songs.

His set was heavy on ballads, some of them throbbed too much, but on one, Oscar Levant's Blame It On My Youth, he was truly touching, finding the vulnerability the cabaret medium needs. There was a medley from Of Thee I Sing, full of verve, and a lot of other Gershwin, including famous songs with unfamiliar lines. I never knew before that "'S Wonderful" contained the couplet, "Say, what's all this dizziness/I thought that I came here on bus-i-ness," or that How Long Has This Been Going On climaxed with the declaration, "Heads may roll but bless my soul." In both cases I was delighted.

Feinstein has access to this classified information because he spent his early twenties working for Ira Gershwin, George's lyricist and elder brother, at his home in Hollywood. First he was engaged to catalogue Ira's record collection; then, as his employer realised the scope of his knowledge of the Gershwin oeuvre, he became his full-time archivist, friend and "the son he never had."

And of course through the Gershwins he encountered other musical greats. ("I always seemed to meet them a few days before they died.") He also met Liza Minnelli, a Gershwin god-daughter and his second great patron. She threw parties for him, opened doors. On his first night at the Ritz she sent him a gargantuan first-night bouquet that now dominates his room.

Ira Gershwin, Feinstein says, "had an endearing, whimsical quality." Like his songs? "Exactly." It's that innocently sophisticated delight in the choice of words that Feinstein communicates in his singing of Gershwin songs.

He's chosen his moment well. Next year is the fiftieth anniversary of George's death, and already the record albums are pouring out. Porgy and Bess is thrilling Glyndebourne audiences with the forbidden aroma of show music. Feinstein himself is planning a book setting down Gershwin anecdotes hitherto unpublished. Goodbye Columbus; hello world.
