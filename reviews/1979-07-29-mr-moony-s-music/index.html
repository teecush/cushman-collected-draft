Jul 29, 1979

Mr Moony's Music

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Mr Moony's music

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Songbook.

MOONY SHAPIRO, the hero of Songbook (Globe), is a famous songwriter who never existed. At least he never existed as a whole person; bits of him may be found dotted over the history of twentieth-century popular music.

Moony's career extends from Busby Berkeley to the Beatles; and his creators, Monty Norman and Julian More, have drawn freely on showbiz lore. He is reminiscent of Irving Berlin in his jackdaw adaptability, of Jule Styne in his unquenchable resilience, and of just about everybody when you set about examining his songs.

Mr Norman has furnished Moony's music, Mr More his lyrics, and the two of them have collaborated on the Songbook book. They have not been much heard from since the fifties, when they were variously involved in Expresso Bongo, Irma la Douce and Make Me an Offer; add those together and you have probably the most impressive track-record of any of those involved in the brief renaissance of the British musical.

Songbook reaffirms their craftsmanship. It is also an excellent advertisement for the Cambridge Theatre Company, which originated the show, and for its director, Jonathan Lynn. It is a pleasure to see wit, economy and commonsense again at work in the staging of a home-grown musical. It's been a long time. Taken all in all, Songbook is the pleasantest thing to have happened in the West End this year. All the same, there are nagging flaws.

It lacks a centre and, though it has plenty of incidental styles, an overall approach. Working on a combination of grapevine and guesswork I would say that it originated as a spoof on the anthology shows--Cole or Side by Side by Sondheim. But there is not much in those spare presentations that can be parodied, other than the songs themselves. Songbook has one basic turn-around idea; all previous anthologies have dealt, by definition, with successful writers, but Moony is depicted as a life-long failure.

We hear his contributions to the Broadway revue Feldman's Follies of 1926, to the Warner Brothers extravaganza Pretty Faces of 1934 (bad title--sounds more like stage than film), to the wartime weepie Bring Back Tomorrow (good title--sounds like Fox), to the folksy post-war stage musical Happy Hickory (Finian's Rainbow set in a tree) and to the ahead-of-its-time protest musical Red White and Black, which yields I Accuse, an Oriental duet pitched somewhere between Flower Drum Song and Pacific Overtures. In between these selections comes the odd number fashioned for Marlene Dietrich, Cicely Courtneidge and Adolf Hitler.

Oh yes, the plot. Though Moony began his days in Liverpool and ended them in Ireland, he lived mainly in America, where he was adopted by a Jewish couple named Shapiro; having adopted the name he nearly adopted the religion too, but stopped short. Success brought him an English, left-wing aristocratic wife, named Bella, and an English, right-wing aristocratic sister-in-law, named Charity. (No, prizes.) Charity introduced him to Hitler at the 1936 Olympics. His observation of the Fuehrer's fury at Negro athletic victories yielded the deathless number, You're a Nazi Party Pooper, Jesse Owens.

That very funny song is not a parody of anything; Messrs Norman and More have written it, so to speak, off their own bat. The same is true of much of the best material: a rather good torch-er that develops out of a running gag about a constantly rejected ballad and a savage--actually too savage for its context--complaint about Nostalgia; but the essays at actual pastiche tend to be vague or thumping. There are exceptions: the Depression chant Mister Destiny You Done Us Wrong is dead on the target and April in Wisconsin (rhymes with "dancin'" and "entrancin'") is beautifully orchestrated forties Sinatra.

The hawk-eyed may have noticed that I have referred to Moony both as a success and as a failure. If I equivocate it's because the show does. It exists number by number, joke by joke. Most of the jokes are good, but when they falter the script is apt to seem more knowing than knowledgeable.

Gillian Lynne's choreography is up to her usual spanking level and the performers are superb. Anton Rodgers is actually more, his frayed casualness as compere alternating with a series of knife-edge impersonations: an impeccably haughty chansonnier, a Ringo Starr drumming with hilariously unskilled abandon. Gemma Craven as Bella adds her own layer of satire by resembling Kate Nelligan at her most icily aggressive, and is also a charming and versatile singer. Diane Langton in ATS togs makes a commanding Courtneidge and Andrew C. Wadsworth is splendidly utilitarian. David Healy cannot bring Moony to full life, but is a pleasing personality on his own account.

*

The version of Euripides's Hippolytus at the Warehouse is described as "a realisation of the text by David Rudkin." This mysterious phrase turns out to mean that Mr Rudkin--who, if we confine our criteria strictly to the selection and arrangement of words, is the best dramatic writer we have--has rendered the play into stern, concrete English that rarely sounds like a substitute for anything and is never pushily colloquial; and that he has made the characters more explicitly aware of what is eating them than they are in the original (or rather, to be strictly truthful, in any other translation I have read).

So the hero's servant rebukes him for disregarding his sexuality and meets the stiff reply, "I acknowledge the erotic principle." This means that the opposing goddesses of love and the chase, being personifications of tragic forces anyway, have the easiest roles. Juliet Stevenson plays them both and her Aphrodite gets the play-off to a merciless, enticing start. Thereafter everything is valiant, but anticlimactic; a schoolroom aura descends as the actors battle to fill out the naked text.

Michael Pennington's Hippolytus comes off best; Natasha Parry makes a guarded Phaedra, while Patrick Stewart's rages as Theseus seem to come second-hand from Shakespeare.

The eternal chorus problem is tackled by cutting the ranks down to one, who naturally emerges, unprotected by convention, as the most inexplicably voluble character on stage. No particular fault of the actress, but one starts wondering, "Who is this woman?"

*

Regarding cricket-bores: as just that, I may be the wrong audience for Outside Edge (Hampstead), a quite promising farce by Richard Harris that takes us into the Ayckbourn territory of heedless husbands and neglected wives, obsessed by off-breaks and tea-breaks respectively. Mr Harris manages to keep four separate actions going, but he flogs them in the process.

There is a strong cast, though the men do little more than mark time. The ladies, being oppressed, have more rewarding tasks and Julia McKenzie delivers her best, straight performance to date as the most downtrodden. Maureen Lipman too is in good form as a tall tart with a short but sexy husband; this is the one joke in her role and she reiterates it with resource.
