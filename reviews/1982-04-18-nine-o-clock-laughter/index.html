Apr 18, 1982

Nine O'Clock Laughter

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Nine O'Clock laughter

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Not in Front of the Audience' at Drury Lane.

FEARFULLY we await the stage version of 'Over the Top.' Meanwhile the Not the Nine O'Clock News collection have gone flesh-and-blood with Not in Front of the Audience at Drury Lane. (Do they despise the theatre? Their television title was impeccably truthful; its stage equivalent is a flat lie.)

With 'Not' (etc.) Oxbridge comedy reverts to type, or at least to 'Beyond the Fringe.' Both shows ridicule public figures, not through any strong conviction, but just because they are there. (The public figures, I mean, not the performers, though it works with them as well.) The Fringies knew that the mere mention of C. P. Snow was funny; and 'Not' (and so on) knows the same about the Duke of Kent.

'That Was the Week That Was,' for all its opportunism, was more serious; its very presence on television, then the most cocooned of media, made it so. It had axes to grind, and it helped to topple a government. It used up its anger fast and became indistinguishable from its pale imitators.

The Monty Python team, the next innovators, reacted by forswearing explicit topicality, relying on imagination, whimsy and the breaking of taboos--which, politics and sex having already been commandeered, meant violence and death. Oh yes, and advertising. They consumed the territory, leaving 'OTT' to flail raucously and helplessly around and the atrociously smug 'Whoops Apocalypse' to rant dementedly as if the end of the world were such a self-evidently groovy subject that it made comic skills irrelevant.

Fitting in just ahead of this last crevasse are the crew of 'Not' (u.s.w.), purveyors of shock to the unshockable. When the TV series appeared at the Lane, they interleaved a re-run of favourite material; a cosy laugh-along. The new numbers have worked harder and, though they incorporate some recycled numbers, theirs is a real show. But they too are cosy. They scream at us, but their screams really go over our heads; we join them in mocking faceless, nameless conventionalists situated somewhere outside the theatre.

Compare Barry Humphries, the previous attraction, now prostrating himself on the road; when he picks on his audience, getting ladies on stage to cut up salads and barbecue steaks, he makes us quiver--partly because we could be next, partly because we recognise the ritual of instant stardom. It is the game-show phone-in routine taken to its extreme with Edna Everage, the housewife who got away, as both its exemplar and its celebrant.

The 'Not' quartet often imitate punk-rock groups; they enjoy the aggression, but they enjoy the send-up more. They don't, as the more fanciful Pythons did, mean business--or not often; they do say the last word on 'The Romans in Britain' by solemnly lowering respectable trousers and bending over. (What has the law to say to simulated simulated rape?)

They inhabit the same shrugging, showbiz-by-proxy world as the rest of us, presenting a papal visit as a massively sponsored production involving dance-routines and a blank pontifical performance of Mike Yarwood jokes. They issue cod fire-warnings over the PA system and offer Freddie Laker as the hero of an 'Evita' musical. They also realise the contribution of brand-names to the great old British tradition of the absurd; any word or object becomes surreal if you stay at it hard enough.

Rowan Atkinson as something from space outlines the differences between his civilisation and ours: "We have no death, no gravity; we have a different shaped gear-stick in our Mini Metro." This was my favourite number, though Mr Atkinson's great strength can seem out of place in a team-show; I remember whole TV episodes going by without finding much for him to do. Pamela Stephenson's mimetic and musical accomplishments still don't quite come together, but Griff Rhys-Jones keeps improving.

The most useful participant, though--as link-man, actor and co-director--is Mel Smith; Smith the slob and Rhys-Jones the juve-lead sometimes exchange roles, breaking down still further the ancient distinction between clown and straight-man. In modern comedy, as in modern life, everyone does both. In modern comedy, as in modern life, jokers write their own jokes; I am always mildly disappointed to find this lot, like so many politicians, relying wholly on other people's.

*

Some barriers remain impregnable. Michael Frayn's much-advertised failure to find a resolution for the hilarious backstage drama Noises Off (Savoy) suggests that he has barked his shins on one of them. The leading character--anyway the star-role--is a director, but the piece concerns dress-rehearsal, and performance, the period at which the director begins to disappear. Bringing him back on becomes increasingly difficult; rewriting the last minutes has not solved the problem.

This should bother future historians more than present audiences; once again, as in 'Donkeys' Years,' Mr Frayn moves easily from verbal comedy to physical farce; only this time he has been critically blessed, instead of cursed, for the transition. We learn slowly, but we learn. That both modes are firmly rooted in reality may be judged from the impeccable performances of Michael Blakemore's cast; their commitment extends not merely to the play-within-the-play (a sex farce that Mr Frayn must get round to completing some day), but to the programme-within-the-programme: each supplies a biography of his own character whose glosses on long periods of failure and unemployment could only be so feelingly parodied by adepts.

*

Not even C. P. Taylor's untimely death can obscure the fact that his characters habitually talked too much. In Give Me Sunshine, Give Me Smiles (Shaw) they assail the ears of a tolerant Geordie whom they finally drive to distraction. His own chats to the audience had much the same effect on me; when action threatens, he stops it in its tracks. Some lines are funny, most seem truthful, but the total is (a) too much and (b) not enough.

The visiting Tyne Wear Company perform efficiently; Rosalind Bailey is sharply alluring; Tim Healy is comfy as the recipient of everyone else's sexual recollection. Sometimes he remembers these memories, creating flashbacks within flashbacks. He commences the evening by making tea--high and Northern--for his womenfolk; a breakthrough for something or other. He attracts wholehearted sympathy when, in his greatest need, a whole parade of public telephones die on him.

The best 'La Ronde' yet is offered by Shared Experience at the Drill Hall. In Mike Alfreds's production even the costume-changes are stylish; as, with two actors dividing 10 roles, they had better be. Jonathan Hackett transfers amazingly well from unfeeling Soldier to primly adventurous Young Gentleman, though the older characters give him more trouble; Pam Ferris makes a commandingly seductive Actress, a trembling and perpetually virginal Housemaid, but her Sweet Girl comes across as a distinctly Tart Girl.

Though generally excelling the RSC's version in wit and honesty, they fall surprisingly short of it in the brutal restaurant scene: Comedy having been on my mind this week, let me recall that in 'The Tempest,' and especially in 'The Soldier's Fortune,' Arthur Lowe, who died last week, gave two of the funniest classical performances I have ever seen.
