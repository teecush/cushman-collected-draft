Aug 29, 1976

Dear Old Pal

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Dear old Pal

A LAST-MINUTE change of plan by the Edinburgh Festival and the English Stage Company (for which they blame each other) resulted in audiences turning up at the Royal Lyceum for the new David Storey and being greeted instead by an old Richard Rodgers.

Some of them departed in dudgeon and with their money back, thus dampening the proceedings even before they could proceed. As punters they had the right; though of course no responsible critic would countenance them. Even if we forget that Mr Rodgers must have given more widespread pleasure than any other theatre writer alive, it is difficult to believe that anyone seriously regards him as a lesser artist than Mr Storey.

Pal Joey, the last-but-one of the musicals on which Mr Rodgers collaborated with Lorenz Hart, is generally reckoned to be a seminal piece. I know of no American musical prior to its premiere in 1940 which stuck so conscientiously to its own line; the story it tells and the manner of the telling are both uncommonly bleak.

The script was fashioned by John O'Hara from his own short stories. Joey is a night-club compere who performs more notably in bed than on the floor. Rescued from oblivion by the wife of a Chicago tycoon who had made his bread in bread, he's dropped right back there when his protectress, who has staked him to his own club, is blackmailed by a gangster. Clubland is seen as an extension of the underworld. This show never tries to compromise by making its characters lovable.

This results, admittedly, in a certain monotony. O'Hara was not a particularly adroit dramatist and the second half, when the screws begin to turn, is heavy going. Nor, judged simply as a collection of songs, does the score - apart, perhaps, from Bewitched - rank with the best of Rodgers and Hart. They seemed to have jumped at this assignment with masochistic enthusiasm; the brassiness of Rodgers's music is unique in his work, but it is brusque rather than astringent and the melodies are run of the mill. Hart's lyrics are uneven; after a career of writing what were essentially one-shots, he faced the demands of character and situation with more determination than resource. Even Bewitched, in its unexpurgated version the confession of a nymphomaniac, overflows its context while other numbers fail to fill theirs. Partly this is because they lack proper support from the book. Partly it is because every song in this show is in form a conventional prop, and partly at Edinburgh it is the fault of the production. It comes from the Oxford Playhouse and will probably look better when it returns there, but as a Festival offering it is shrivelled.

Some blame also adheres to Time, which might be defeated if the performers could take possession of the material. Here only one of them does: Anna Quayle, aggressively deadpan, splendidly renders Zip, a satire on the intellectual aspirations of Gypsy Rose Lee. We see far too little of Miss Quayle these days; nor do we see much in this show, since this one mock strip is her only contribution to it.

Many of the other numbers are actually nightclub routines, and not intended to be very good ones. It may have given the authors an excuse for writing below their best, but Philip Hedley's production grants them a kind of remorseless integrity. We spend all evening in the club, done up in bricks the colour of tandoori chicken; all other locations are insets. At his best, Mr Hedley creates the bustle of a real timeless and placeless rehearsal (though the jokes are in the text rather than the staging). If the nightclub numbers are to be purposely bad, the straight songs must compensate, and here they do not. Bob Sherman's Joey is a talented performance but wrong; tall, fair and teasing, he almost makes Joey's stories of a college education believable. They should be incredible; what gives Joey his appeal (and also limits it) is not his plausibility but his barefaced cheek; he is a real no-hoper. There is an atmosphere in the staging, but none in the performances; no challenge in Pat Kirkwood's predatress, no menace in Dick Wilson's gangster. And when he has Mr Sherman break into a couple of unscheduled choruses of The Lady is a Tramp (same composers, different show), Mr Hedley sacrifices even his own credibility.

Ever since 1947, the Assembly Hall has proved a fit setting for moral comedy. It is a wonder that Measure for Measure (staged by the Birmingham Rep) has never been seen there before. In his programme note, the director, Stuart Burge, claims the comedy to be as important as the morality, and for once programme and production agree.

The scenes of regulation humour are not - despite the warm presence of Russell Hunter, an impudently strutting Pompey - unwontedly hilarious. But the blustering irony of David Suchet's Lucio leaves its mark on the stricter characters, particularly on the Duke whom Bernard Lloyd presents as a glib, roguish manipulator, taking to the Friar's hood (Shakespeare's friars being always something akin to pandars) as if he were born to it. He bounds genially from plot to revelation; but the relish he projects is more properly the author's than the character's.

In getting us to giggle at the Duke's schemes, Mr Burge ignores the fact that, in the main, they work; both Angelo and Isabella are respectively knocked off their perches, and, being knocked, learn charity. Anna Calder-Marshall's voice and manner, at once shy and decisive, do very well for Isabella; the rebukes of overreaching authority have never sounded more searing.

When David Burke's Angelo lunged, there were shocked gasps from the stalls, and the laughers were surely right; Mr Burke's interpretation, if a trifle stodgy (a kind of blank verse Malvolio), in no way diminished the agony. When he whispered of his gravity "wherein - let no man hear me - I take pride," he was as vulnerable to ridicule as to contempt. He shows, too, a powerful talent for self-abasement, dropping to the deck overcome by guilt or desire. This Angelo's puritanism was not perhaps of the livid sort to conquer a city, even Edinburgh, and the play lost accordingly part of its full power. But many productions have fallen further.
