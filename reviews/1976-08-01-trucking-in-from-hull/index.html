Trucking In From Hull

Robert Cushman · The Observer

Trucking in from Hull

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

ONE member of the Hull Truck Company claims in his programme biography to have been thrown out of LAMDA; another made a similar exit from Manchester Polytechnic School of Theatre. It is tempting to call Hull Truck a dropouts' group; that phrase belongs of course to the last decade, but it is no less appropriate on that account. The Truckers' most engaging quality is their independence of fashion. They are not themselves anachronistic; the characters they present may be nostalgic for the 1960s, but then that is one of the distinguishing marks of the 1970s.

So, at least, our playwrights keep telling us; Stephen Poliakoff's disc-jockey in City Sugar blamed his present cynicism on the wreck of his past dreams. Mr Poliakoff's Leicester looked like hell; Mike Bradwell's Hull (it is Mr Bradwell who drives the Truck) is more companionable. It tastes of keg beer and instant coffee, and it is unquestionably a university town; whatever their actual status, whatever their age, its inhabitants are leading a student life. They talk, since Hull Truck's plays are improvised, a great deal.

Their last show, Oh What, derived its unity from its characters' enslavement to past enthusiasms. Something of the same theme persists in their latest, Bridget's House, seen last week at the Young Vic, but here it seems peripheral, almost a mannerism. There is a difference - the characters are aware of passing time, they are consciously nostalgic - and there is pleasure to be derived from hearing pop-fans scrupulously distinguishing between the trends of the sixties and the early sixties. There is a pleasant song on the subject, too. (Hull Truck's use of music as commentary between scenes might one day lead us to a real native musical.) But it seems a long-winded, conventional way of establishing the two participants as, in Bridget's words, "emotional cripples."

Bridget is their landlady, as she is to most of the cast, and her estimate of them all is the same. It is the play's business to make us see that the words apply just as strongly to her. The trouble is that we can see this from the start. Bridget is an embittered divorcee, who has collected three properties by way of alimony. A psychology lecturer pursues her; she rejects him and takes a musician to bed, to the fury of his girlfriend.

Sexual situations are even more vulnerable than political ones to dramatists' distortion; the group improvisation method is a good way of preserving balance: We can see everybody behaving badly, weakly, embarrassingly, and no favourites are played. The dialogue is extraordinarily tight (theoretically it may vary in performance, but on the first night it seemed to have cast-iron consistency) and, most unusually in an improvised play, the actors know when to shut up.

Quite often the wit is sharp enough to make you wonder if Hull Truck are harbouring a playwright on the premises. The construction, though, cures you of that idea; Bridget's House, unlike Oh What, tells a story, and cannot raise interest in its outcome. It also differs from its predecessor in concentrating on middle-class characters and it has difficulty getting on terms with them, though David Hatton's lecturer is a clever study in ratty frustration. Rachel Bell's Bridget is clever, too, and as far as possible from her adenoidal tripper in Oh What. Here she shatters egos in a voice coated with iodine, stretches a leg across the lap of her intended with the brazen timing of a Maggie Smith.

This kind of acting (and I state it with no prejudice; in fact with all the approval of enjoyment) is, in the strictest sense, artificial, and needs a text to support it. If Hull Truck are going literary, Miss Bell, their most distinctive player, is going to get there first. The show is due at the Bush in November and the smaller space (though the Young Vic is hardly the Palladium) may suit it better. If the script of Bridget's House were written down, it would undoubtedly make a better read than that of The Canning Town Cowboy (Half Moon). This has an author, Shane Connaughton, and he brings off a few bright gags, but the play really consists of a single, rather arbitrary, conceit extended over a stretch of conventional community-theatre material. A ball of string is being unwound and it might be cut off at virtually any point. The hero is an East Ender who wants to be, and dresses like, a Wild Westerner; his non-conformity is the vehicle for some mild satire on bank clerks, policemen and such that might just as well exist without him. The acting is brash and genial, though it totters whenever the performers try to get a grip on surfaces too thin to allow them a foothold. Jill Richards, urgent and disdainful as the hero's girl, stands firmer than anyone else, though John Joyce, in various guises, grapples well.

*

The Only Way Out (Theatre Upstairs) is by George Thatcher, serving a life-sentence on a murder charge, having originally been condemned to death. It deals with a man in the early stages of the same situation: in the condemned cell, awaiting his appeal, protesting that he was framed, prophesying that the law, though it will spare his life, will be far too careful of its own ever to declare him innocent. Some of the action - notably the defiance of a bullying screw - comes over as wish-fulfilment, but most of it, in a well-controlled production by David Halliwell, is starkly convincing.

The inevitable bond between the prisoner and his unvarying attendants is traced without sentimentality or overt bitterness. Only his outline of his defence rings false: not the matter, but the manner. This may be how Mr Thatcher thinks now, but not how he, or anyone, would have spoken then; the pat phrases sink the actor, Brian Croucher, though earlier he is excellent, his accustomed casualness hardened into last-ditch defiance. I don't know if Mr Thatcher has another play in him, but it might be worth the while of a subsidised theatre to find out.

*

A few words of tribute to Side by Side by Sondheim (now at Wyndham's; I was away when it first opened at the Mermaid). Stephen Sondheim's best songs are built on fine gradations of tone and meaning, and the singers here are often more scrupulously aware of them than were the original interpreters. The duets - David Kernan and Millicent Martin in You Must Meet My Wife from A Little Night Music, or Mr Kernan and Julia McKenzie in Barcelona, the marvellous song of sleepy seduction from Company - become, as they should be, musicalised conversations. The emphasis is maybe too exclusively on sour urban wit (there are occasional cheerful songs in the Sondheim oeuvre), but Miss McKenzie, sad and hopeful, mines deep into Broadway Baby, the song of an unemployed showgirl, so that it passes pastiche and says, more movingly and economically, everything that is in A Chorus Line.
