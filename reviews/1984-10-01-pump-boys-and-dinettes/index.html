Oct 1, 1984

Pump Boys and Dinettes

Robert Cushman · Plays and Players

Pump Boys and Dinettes

I have a dream, same as most English people, of driving westwards across America, stopping off in small towns, sleeping in motels, eating in diners: plotting my course not so much from maps as from the music on the car radio which would grow more countrified and westernised with every mile. It is well known that, once past the Rockies, country and western takes over the airwaves. The snag is: I can't stand it. That's a generalisation. I really mean that I can't stand the lachrymose, whining type (was ever a singer better named than Tammy Wynette?). I used to like Johnny Cash being tough or funny or (preferably) both; and I acknowledge that recent country songs have done something to restore to pop music the traditons of narrative and of realistic, even sardonic portraiture.

The film Coal Miner's Daughter had me rooting for the life and music of Loretta Lynn, even though I would never give her records house-room. A flavoursome lyric can sometimes conquer a boring tune; and even the syrupy songs have some value as a demonstration of how America likes to think it feels. I still tend to turn off the car-radio when a country song threatens but that's because I do most of my driving in London, where who needs it. In the theatre it's another thing. There is actually a respectable tradition of Broadway composers (Irving Berlin, George Gershwin, Richard Rodgers, Cole Porter for a gag, Arthur Schwartz repeatedly - Mr. Schwartz, one of the greatest of modern melodists and an octogenerian of amazing youthfulness, died last month and we should mark his passing - Burton Lane and Cy Coleman) adapting rural styles to their own sophisticated ends; and some ten years ago, a show called The Robber Bridegroom began a superb score in the eighteenth-century backwoods fiddle.

More recently The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas confirmed that country, intelligently adapted, has a lot more to offer the theatre than rock. Its rhythms are relaxed enough for the words to come through, and it presupposes people and a way of life. So I approached Pump Boys and Dinettes with hope. I expected a rich slice of Americana. The stage of the Piccadilly, and a small portion of the auditorium, have been transformed into the stuff of any highway-riding dream. Stage left is a luncheon counter, staffed by Kiki Dee and Carlene Carter. stage right is what looks like a music store, but is apparently the inside room of a gas-station, whose employees just happen to have left a piano and assorted guitars lying about.

These workers are Paul Jones, Brian Protheroe, Gary Holton and Julian Littman. It seems that the restaurant and the garage are actually the same establishment, designed to feed car and driver at the same time. (A motorway service station OCTOBER 1984 is compartmentalised in comparison. A niftier title might be Cooking with Gas. Opening song is called Highway 57'. That tells us where we are. A snatch of dialogue is enough to tell us who the characters are. Mostly they just sing: about small loves and jealousies, about going fishing and falling in love with the girl on the sales counter at Woolworths. I felt very distant from it. but then I was sitting halfway back in the dress circle.

For the first half I told myself that if I were watching this at the King's Head or the Warehouse or even in the front stalls of the Piccadilly I would love it. In the second half I acknowledged that it would stupefy me wherever I met it. Such plot as there is was apparently added between off-Broadway and on; but it is so embarrassingly slight you wish they hadn't bothered. What we are offered is an evening of songs, a form to which I have absolutely no Reviews objection. (Well, I couldn't, could I?). But if songs are to work in a theatre they need wit and they need dramatic fibre, in themselves and in their relation to one another.

Those in Pump Boys and Dinettes have neither; and the music, despite some blues and rock injections, is soul-sappingly monotonous. All my prejudices reawoke; the rest of me was sent to sleep. Mr. Jones, who lent a whole new dangerous dimension to the National's Guys and Dolls when he took over as Sky Masterson, is here thrown back on charm, with which he is mercifully loaded. At one point he has to chat up a member of the audience; on my night he got an actual American tourist, which immediately set up two levels of stage reality. Mr. Jones commuted between them with more humour than was in the rest of the show put together.

He plays the good boy of the outfit; the bad one is Mr. Protheroe, who has the best (or at least the raunchiest) numbers and growls them out well, on voice and piano. Nobody else reached me but then, as aforesaid, they had a long way to travel. And on American highways, as this show demonstrates, there are very severe speed-limits.
