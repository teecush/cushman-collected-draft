Breaking bonds

CARYL CHURCHILL'S Fen (Almeida) is about broken links, chiefly family ones. It takes place on an East Anglian farm, whose owner regards it as a money-machine, but also, quite genuinely, as a heritage. He is obliged to sell out, becoming a highly-paid employee on what was his own land. As someone remarks, nobody knows who owns anything any more. Probably, to judge from a sprightly prologue, it's the Japanese.

The main drama unfolds among the farm's potato pickers, mostly female. One, Val, leaves her unseen husband for a tractor-driver who is not strong enough for her. She also leaves her two small daughters. She cannot, however, keep away from them, and the conflict finally kills her. Another, Angela, has a teenage step-daughter whom she torments. She can't stop herself, but she wants to be stopped. Two lines sum her up: 'I have to hurt you worse' and 'Why do you like me, I don't want you to like me.'

Perhaps I should have made it broken bonds. Topographically, this is Edward Bond country, and so it is dramatically as well, far more a rural Saved than another Afore Night Come. Like Mr Bond, Miss Churchill writes bluntly, but not coarsely, about passion. Like him, she can put cruelty on stage (she does it, in the Angela scenes, with remarkable tact), and a classical death scene, and ghosts; this last as a very straightforward way of reminding the farmer of the sins of his fathers. She also has a good line in elders who remember, but cannot necessarily be trusted.

Top Girls was, is, a hard act to follow, and this play is far less high-spirited. But it picks up some of its predecessor's themes. It is, again, about women having to make up the rules as they go along. Val opts for a kind of liberation and finds there are taboos she cannot escape. The traditional comforts are no good to her (a sadly amusing prayer-meeting scene makes that clear), but she can find no new ones. Anyway the community in which she lives is tradition-steeped and family-fixated; people never stop talking about their relatives or their ancestors.

Apart from a whimsical finale everything in this play fits, no matter how eccentrically shaped it may seem. Miss Churchill tells us all we need to know; not necessarily, though, all we would like to know. But that may be the fault of the production; or, rather, of the kind of production. It is from Joint Stock, whose actors, though they did not improvise the play, researched its background and, presumably, assembled its details. But despite the title, and despite Annie Smart's wondrously earthy set, the play is not finally about the land, but the people on it.

Six actors play 21 roles, which means restless doubling and no chance to get to know anyone, this last compounded by an ostentatious chastity of outline. The method actually works against a sense of community; there are simply never enough people around. Jennie Stoller is fine as Val -- a real tragic figure, bewildered but whole -- but she is helped by having to play no other part save that of the ghost, which can reasonably be done as a guest appearance. My frustration at a potentially superb evening was aggravated by the theatre itself, which was freezing.

* * *

Pinter and you have some idea of Peter Gill's Kick for Touch (Cottesloe): two men, buddies; a woman, married to one and desired by both; all three characters Welsh instead of just one; the dialogue allusive, but moving occasionally into direct emotional statement. This shift of gears Mr Gill handles very well; he can make realistic dialogue work for him (cf. last week's remarks on Tony Marchant, who can't quite).

The three characters don't move about much; they sit round a table, and even when one is presumed to have left the stage, that one remains. Scenes blend into one another, disregarding time and space; the relationships dissolve and reform (neither man is called Jules, but one is Jim); mates are revealed as brothers, and wives as eternal mothers. It is all too compressed and too generalised to make any impact, and though it starts surprisingly un-boring, it does not continue so.

There are three very good actors present, though only one is at the top of his form. This is James Hazeldine, toused and disruptive. Kenneth Cranham is subdued, but rises to a rumbling climax; Jane Lapotaire's only noticeable acting blemish, a tendency to sing-song, is compounded by Celtic. Three actors, however good, is not many; in general practice, more means merrier, and the National is in a better position than most to act on this observation.

If a three-hander is The Caretaker or American Buffalo or (upping the number slightly) Mr Bond's Summer, then all is forgiven; if not, not. Minimalism is being confused -- especially at the Cottesloe -- with poetry; puritanism with integrity. And who wants integrity anyway? Not that spectacle is everything. At the Sheffield Crucible there is Don Juan the Lover (to be distinguished, of course, from Don Juan the Marine Biologist). This is set in a bull-ring, which certainly looks good and could hardly be more Spanish. Just to reinforce the point, the Don and his servant go through numerous matador's passes, a peasant girl says 'Ole' as soon as entering, and a wedding is accompanied by keening music, though that sounded oddly Jewish.

In addition there is an onstage audience that applauds the Don every time he says or does anything especially typical of himself, and there are frequent references to the fact that he is a legend in a pre-ordained play: scripts are consulted. Michael Boyd, the adaptor-director, is inventive, but I don't like any of his inventions.

* * *

What he has adapted are Moliere's Don Juan and the first play on the subject, Tirso de Molina's The Trickster of Seville; the story is told in alternate scenes from each. However you treat it, Juan had a one-track career; and by depriving each playwright of his own rhythm for disguising the fact you merely compound the monotony. Anyway they don't match; Moliere's Juan never gets it on, and Tirso's never fails to.

At the end Mr Boyd abandons both, to leave the eternal woman-chaser on stage with the eternal woman of his Catholic upbringing: i.e. at the foot of a large statue of the Madonna. It's a point. And, yes, it's more enterprising than doing Agatha Christie. And Siobhan Redmond as seduced fishergirl looks, acts and sings luscious.

James Hayes, a National Theatre actor of excellent character, is out on parole for two weeks at the Gate, performing A Horde of Unemployed Ventriloquists. This recital is drawn from the works of Flann O'Brien, in his journalistic Myles na Gopaleen hat. Some -- several -- of his erudite japes are too convoluted to survive theatrically, but Mr Hayes's delivery of them is impeccable, his pleasure communicated. His enunciation, while disguised as a remarkably seedy Dubliner, of the words 'Horse d'oeuvre' is a rich entertainment in itself.
