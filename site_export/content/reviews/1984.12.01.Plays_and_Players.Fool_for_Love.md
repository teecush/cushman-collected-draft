Fool for Love

Fool for Love is my Smirnoff play, until I saw it I thought that Sam Shepard's vociferous followers were so many sheep. My conversion came last year in New York, when I saw the play staged at the most consistently excellent of off-Broadway theatres, the Circle Rep. Their current production of Lanford Wilson's Balm in Gilead is one of the most exciting orchestrations of a large cast in a small space that I have ever seen. I relished it the more having witnessed the previous day, uptown at the Promenade, David Rabe's Hurly Burly, three hours of all-star tedium in what had become officially known as Mike Nichols's wonderful ensemble production: the kind of rave bestowed on any show in which a collection of well-known actors avoid actually stepping on each other's lines or bumping into the furniture.

Where was I? Balm in Gilead really was an ensemble production, order drawn out of engulfing chaos, and so, at the same address, was Fool for Love. Mr Shepard directed it himself, and created extraordinary tension. The air was full of the threat of violence. This was remarkable since the stage was full of actual violence, and the two are generally incompatible. But somehow Mr Shepard had succeeded in setting a play at what we were assured was one end of the civilised world - the edge of the Mojave Desert, the barest of motel rooms - and filling it with emotions to match. Here, from the comparative civilisation of Wyoming, comes Eddie, a cowboy seeking and finding his lady, May.

She ran off in disgust at his infidelities. He wants her back. Someone else wants him back. It seems (Mr Shepard offers no firm assurance) that his latest conquest, referred to by scornful May as 'the Countess' will stop at nothing to get him back. Outside the window, lights blaze and guns resound. When a harmless hick arrives to take May out to a movie, he walks onto a hotter stage than he can ever have expected. There is an explosive charge in the May-Eddie relationship anyway. They are half-brother and sister. When Eddie says that May can never get away from him or he from her, he is probably right. The violence of the play is its appeal: better face that.

The stuff that critics have fastened on, about Eddie the cowboy representing a doomed type of American manhood, an extinct American buffalo: that you can keep. It is the kind of windy pronouncement that echoes through and around Mr Shepard's worst and most static plays: The Tooth of Crime and whatever that thing about Howard Hughes was called. Fool for Love does have a sort of exotic geographic appeal, what with the 1950 country hit I'm the One Who Loves You' welling sardonically but quite sincerely up at the final curtain (somewhere the world of 'Pump Boys and Dinettes' is calling), but it is important less for what it tells about the American landscape than for what it tells about the American theatre and its continuing capacity to go to extremes.

These extremes pose (I hate to drag out a traditional phrase but I know no appropriate other) a severe challenge to an English production. I wonder if the National, who are rather hot on authors directing their own work, attempted to bring Mr Shepard over for this one. In the event, Peter Gill directs loyally. He seems to have followed the stage directions to the letter; the slam of the motel-room door - a constant motif - is as furiously loud as Mr Shepard demands, and rather more so than in his own production. But only half the play well, two thirds - is there in the acting. David Troughton provides fine shy comic relief and Julie Walters is superlative as May: majestic in her anger, her fears, and her whiplash putdown timing.

She seems able to do anything: the best actress to appear among us since Judi Dench. She gets not only the accent but the temper. Ian Charleson as Eddie is determined but ineradicably soft-grained, though he twirls a mean lasso (taught, the programme acknowledges, by the superbly named Rex Roper). I don't know what other British actor one would cast in the role, nor, short of resurrecting Wilfrid Lawson, can I think of one to supply the swaggering rye-whisky authority missing from Tom Watson's performance of the Old Man. This Old Man, he plays dead. He also plays dad, May's and Eddie's. He sits at the side of the stage, abrasively commenting and rosily reminiscing.

Mr Shepard suggests in his commentary that he exists only in his children's thoughts, that he is a thing in their dreams. But, as the play unfolds, they seem like things in his. He is the guv'nor, and unless he does govern, the play sags. Mr Gill's production ends curiously like his Venice Preserv'd: an uncompromising but unavailing attempt to capture an alien, extravagant style. Still, the Cottesloe has here the second-best contemporary play in London; and in Glengarry Glen Ross, of course, still the first best. Both, for our chauvinistic comfort, are indebted to Harold Pinter; but it is no wonder that, despite the success of sundry British plays on Broadway, Americans are losing their long-held sense of dramaturgical inferiority.

We could yet return to the post-war situation in which Arthur Miller and Tennessee Williams (with whom Messrs Mamet and Shepard have, respectively, obvious affinities) were the envy of the English-speaking theatre. Nobody, not even Tom Stoppard, writes dialogue as good as Mr Mamet's, and we have nobody who can do melodrama, and even incipient tragedy, as well as Mr Shepard. No other current playwrights create characters who stand this tall.
