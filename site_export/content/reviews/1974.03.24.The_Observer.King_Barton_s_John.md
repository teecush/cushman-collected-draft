King Barton's 'John'

THIS year's season at Stratford on Avon began on Wednesday with William Shakespeare's King John. So, at least, declared the programme, thereby exposing the Royal Shakespeare Company not merely to criticism, but to possible prosecution under the Trades Descriptions Act.

I doubt if more than 50 per cent of the lines spoken in this production could be found in the received text. For the rest the director, John Barton, has had recourse to an early Tudor morality play, King Johan, which depicts John as a Protestant hero, defying the Pope; to the anonymous Troublesome Reign of King John, which was Shakespeare's immediate source (a generous gesture, this, to give back to one's author much that he had already rejected); and, Mr Barton modestly adds, to "additions of my own." I would guess that among these is the allusion to the state of the nation made by some baron or other in justifying a change of allegiance: "The price of goods soars meteor-like to heaven."

A meteor was a favourite Elizabethan image for over-reaching ambition, and on this occasion Mr Barton has gone in for a certain amount of soaring himself. Apparently, he began with the intention of making a few interpolations to clarify the actions and significance of a muddled play with (in recent years) a spare and disappointing stage-history. The fit grew on him, and he found himself refashioning the entire piece. It is possible to sit at this production and relive the experience with him. We witness the enthronement of the adaptor-director, transformed as the contending princes of the play are transformed when they assume (as in this production they constantly do) the golden mask of kingship. The title acquires a double edge.

The process is gradual; the first half is easy and even familiar. The turbulent but monotonous rhetoric of the opposing forces of France and England is given a satiric twist; the self-interest that underlies their stately protestations is glaringly exposed. Mr Barton did the same thing 10 years ago in The Wars of the Roses and the main change since then has been in the mode of design, which is Brechtian in the airy, as opposed to the earthy, manner. Into this ironic context the cynical comments of the Bastard Faulconbridge fit quite naturally.

That is not necessarily a good thing, since he is meant to be an irritant; here, despite Richard Pasco's forceful performance, he seems redundant, deflating the already deflated. As the play proceeds he dwindles, and is finally reduced to reading the play's famous, uncuttable, but embarrassingly positive last lines on national unity from a hefty unidentified volume.

Before this point is reached, however, we have been through a great deal, much of it brilliant. Believing unity of style to be an overrated virtue (if it is a virtue at all), I was beguiled by Mr Barton's breezy mastery in alternating realpolitik with emblematic morality. Pictures multiply: young Arthur (excellently played by Benedict Taylor) sitting cross-legged and silent while his elders manipulate his future; a young couple, betrothed for reasons of State, disappearing into gunsmoke; the arrival of the Papal Legate (Jeffery Dench, also excellent), a walking skull encased in scarlet.

I do believe, however, in unity of idea; and I was thus dismayed by Mr Barton's attempts to turn King John into a play about everything. I suspect that rehearsals were partly fuelled by the company's memories of past productions and preoccupations. The self-justifying barons come straight out of Murder in the Cathedral. The proclamation "God save King Arthur" seems to have set off recollections (which can't have been altogether happy) of John Arden's Island of the Mighty and its musings, as of a cynical anthropologist, on the theme of monarchy. Almost for the first time I believed in the RSC's protestations about the influence of their modern work on their Shakespeare; usually it has seemed to me to be the other way about.

Kingship, with its disparity between the man and the office, the player and the role--as in last year's Richard II--seems to be on Mr Barton's mind; so does the state of England among the nations: at one point the play threatens to become an allegory of the EEC. There's a line about little Albion dodging in and out between "Holland, Denmark, Germany and Rome." The production takes on the quality of a brainstorm; it finally bursts when John's poisoning is staged as a tableau of the Last Supper. I flinched, not merely at the pretentiousness, but because the evening had already lasted three hours, and Last Suppers can take a long time. This one has carols.

Shakespeare's play, as several reviewers hastily remarked last week, is no masterpiece, but it does have its points: pace is one of them and humanity another. On the few occasions when the original script is employed at any length Mr Barton proves, infuriatingly enough, that he can still direct it better than anyone else. Constance's grief (Sheila Allen, subtly distanced, gives the most judicious performance of her career) is beautifully handled; so are John's tempting of Hubert and the Bastard's soliloquies. (Though even in the commodity speech there are interpolations, provoking audible, if probably unconscious, changes of gear in Mr Pasco's delivery.) John's great death-scene is more or less intact, but it is too late for us to take much interest in it. Emrys James is foxy, freakishly amoral, and thoroughly compelling, but he cannot hold this production together; nobody could. For once Stratford seems to have a company with personality and weight; they need a text to support them.

* * *

We know from Ghosts and The Seagull that Jonathan Miller has a strong company for his Family Romances at Greenwich but on the first night of Hamlet (which last week was a shorter play than King John) they were floundering in visible distress. Robert Stephens's Claudius will probably grow into a fine study of a beleaguered ruler, oppressed equally by the memory of his brother and the presence of his nephew, but on Tuesday it was difficult to distinguish the King's nervous mannerisms from the actor's.

Peter Eyre and Irene Worth strike sparks off each other in the closet scene (for which, after all, they have been warming up all season); otherwise the performers make no contact. Mr Eyre, a sulky prince, never talks to anyone, though his soliloquies make internal sense; at the end of his last one he just stops himself from launching into a self-defeating tirade and settles for quiet resolution instead. Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are as good as dead. Nicola Pagett is a notable and surprisingly straightforward Ophelia, regressing to babyhood in madness, Antony Brown a properly humourless Polonius and Anthony Nicholls a remarkably solid Ghost, who sits down beside his victims and chats them up in a most unnerving manner.

Mr Miller has no truck with atmospherics; he stages the duel and the play-scene well, though he cannot make a virtue out of the economic necessity of a small cast. I was sorry that the players were interrupted before we could see how the murderer got the love of Gonzago's wife; it would have been fascinating, since they are both the same actor.

* * *

George Kelly's The Show-Off (Hampstead Theatre Club) features a physically witty, but vocally overwrought performance from Al Mancini, and some compensating comic reality from Doreen Mantle. A respect for reality is the play's distinguishing feature; her daughter cannot visit her parents without mentioning that she was on her way to pay the electricity bill. If this occurred in a new play at the Royal Court I would dismiss it as a tiresome reflex; in this comedy, from 1920s Philadelphia, it strikes me as honest and delightful. This is known as critical bias.
