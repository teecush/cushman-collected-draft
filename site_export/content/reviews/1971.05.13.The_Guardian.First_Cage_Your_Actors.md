First cage your actors

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

AS MUCH rubbish is talked about "ensemble" as about any other topic in the English theatre. It's something we all wanted for a very long time and we are now not quite sure whether or not we've actually got it. If we have, the feeling goes, then it's not all that it was cracked up to be.

Our biggest mistake, it becomes increasingly obvious, was in believing that the building of a company was principally a matter of time. It does seem to be true that if a sufficiently talented group of actors are caged up together for two or three years some interesting work will result in the brief period (a further year, not much more) before they begin to file their way through the bars. The Royal Shakespeare Company (founded 1960) reached its peak in 1962-3 and the National Theatre (founded 1963) reached its in 1965-6. But since those dates both organisations have zig-zagged and we have hardly known from one production to another whether we have a company or not. I am here concerned with the National currently going through one of its good patches. I'll get to "Coriolanus" later.

The critics have been sniffing a renaissance in the last two productions but misplacing the emphasis. Since "The Captain of Koepenick" has a larger cast than any previous National production it was pronounced an ensemble triumph. But nobody bothered to use that commendation for John Dexter's production of "A Woman Killed with Kindness" which, in its balance, sobriety, its exact sense of psychological and spatial relationships, was as worthy of it as anything in years. Dexter builds a whole world in this production and he builds it with every actor in the cast. The performances of the servants, many of them non-speaking, are jewel-like reminders of what permanent companies are supposed to be about. Hardly anybody seems to have noticed.

I enjoy watching the patterns created by a collection of actors in varying juxtaposition. I even enjoy revisiting productions and collecting performances denied for ever to the first-nighters.

The worst sort of situation for actors is when someone like, say, Patrick Garland cuts the ground from beneath the actors' feet by destroying the texture of a play like "Cyrano," not so much by deromanticising it as by neutering or eliminating the panoply of small parts handed to him by the author. It seems pretty pointless, too, to have a fine, watchful actor like Edward Woodward breeze into the company, play two enormous roles at present beyond his range, and leave without having the opportunity to consolidate anything.

Consolidation can often take place outside the company. It is obvious, for example, that Maggie Smith made a radical advance between leaving as Miss Julie and returning as Mrs Sullen and Hedda; and Anthony Hopkins the new Coriolanus, whom I sometimes think of as the Ruby Keeler of the company ("you're going off that stage as a spear-carrier and you're coming back a star") reached the heights in "A Woman Killed" with the minimum of warning.

What warning there was occurred in "The Architect and the Emperor" where his performance looks in retrospect like an impressive piece of muscle-flexing. Coriolanus should have completed the triptych and though a much less finished piece of acting than his Master Frankford it brings him within sight of full heroic stature, incorporating a sense of playfulness which suggests an ability to learn creatively from Olivier in an ambience where more or less unconscious imitation has become the norm. (And now that they possess, in Scofield, the other great figurehead of the actor's theatre the ramifications could be fascinating.) Hopkins's obvious technical faults (and Coriolanus is as demanding a role on this score as any in the canon) are only those of which Olivier himself was judged guilty at the same age. All the same, he is playing the part by default and at short notice and he can hardly be said to have been prepared for it.

Hopkins's progress offers perhaps the nearest parallel in the Waterloo Road to the process familiar in the RSC where the advancement of an Alan Howard can get to look like a military campaign. But development can be haphazard. Derek Jacobi, say, could hardly not have progressed in his eight years with the National. But it took him long enough to reach his summit in "The Idiot" and recognition was grudging enough when it came.

Maybe the new influx of resident directors (Dexter and Michael Blakemore to join Olivier and Frank Dunlop) will make future passages smoother. Although the actors seem to have few enough complaints about the constant stream of guest directors, each, in recent months, equipped with an interpreter, I cannot feel they have been well served in "Coriolanus." It's a far better production than "Cyrano" (and Hopkins's performance alone would keep it miles from disaster) but it suffers from the same disease. To cut a play to size means, almost invariably, to thin its human texture and to deny its supporting players their legitimate opportunities. Morale, I have been assured on all sides, has never been higher at the National. It should be used before it gets away again.
