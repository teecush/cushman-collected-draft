Exiled in Hollywood

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Edinburgh

MICHAEL GAMBON has sleepy eyes, a jocular build and the tones of an amiable clubman who would probably be too civilised to show emotion and would get befuddled if he tried. Yet he brought off much of Lear and most of Galileo; and those roles have enlarged him.

In Christopher Hampton's Tales from Hollywood he effortlessly commands an Olivier stage as wide as only a Peter Gill barebones production can make it. His exterior is bland, lazy, innocent, but the mind is sharp, the voice can sting, and the comic timing is—though this has always been known—perfect.

He plays Odon von Horvath, the Austrian playwright who met a famous end in 1938, when a Parisian tree fell on him, but who in Mr Hampton's play is spared this bizarre death in favour of a bizarre life: as one of the refugees from Hitler who made up the European colony of wartime Los Angeles.

For Horvath's genius we in Britain have only Mr Hampton's word: literally, since he has twice functioned as Horvath's translator. He convinced me then; and he does so now. His Horvath is undogmatic, observant, ironic, a believer in kindness, but not always an exponent of it, and a failure as a screenwriter. Mr Hampton opposes him to Thomas Mann, very famous and very smug, and most especially to Brecht.

Last decade Peter Handke founded a fashion with an article called 'Horvath is better than Brecht'; Mr Hampton agrees and dramatises the preference. His play has about the best jokes in London, but the funniest accompanies Brecht's entrance. The house-lights are brought up and Horvath delivers the last word on the alienation effect: 'Brecht always liked people to be aware that they were in a theatre. I said to him more than once—but Brecht, what makes you think they think they're anywhere else? But he had a way of not answering questions he didn't approve of.' I'd like that on a T-shirt, to be supplied to theorists everywhere. Unfortunately Mr Hampton repeats the gag with a relentlessness worthy of Brecht himself.

Brecht, played by Ian McDiarmid as a mincing goblin with no tincture of sage, emerges as the predestined successful rebel, Horvath as the loser, living on borrowed time. Brecht's strident commitment vs. Horvath's guilty understated concern, with points given to both sides: being fair to himself, Mr Hampton declares himself of Horvath's party, since Brecht never bothered about fairness.

Mr Hampton also shows Horvath as ineffectual when faced with the aching marriage of Heinrich Mann (Philip Locke, moving), Thomas's patronised and underrated brother. His wife, young and outclassed, is a sketchily written role which throws Billie Whitelaw back on routine barmaid acting.

What is Mr Hampton's Horvath doing in their ménage? The answer is nothing; and, honest though it may be, nothing comes of it. The play begins to splinter; various themes pass by—little old things like the bad conscience of the émigré survivor, and the history of the modern world—and float out of reach. Horvath could provide a focus for his own plays, but not, it seems, for somebody else's.

Tales from Hollywood could be practically a generic title at the National these days, what with Guys and Dolls and You Can't Take It With You (I know, both Broadway, but it's the same old showbiz) and the Jean Seberg musical looming up; and Mr Hampton throws in some satirical knock-about on the madness of the movies. It is, in all senses, a decent play; and Mr Gill's direction, despite the unaccustomed glitz at its edges, is lucid and elegant as ever. And there is always—since he is on-stage throughout as our guide, the author's philosopher and everybody's friend—Mr Gambon.

To Edinburgh for more tales from the Vienna woods, since the city of the schnitzel remains the focus of this year's festival in the city of oat cuisine. (Tom Stoppard was a prophet in reverse when he set On the Razzle in Vienna during Scottish fortnight.)

The second week has been a mighty improvement on the first. Philip Prowse has transformed the Assembly Hall with his designs for his own production of Rosenkavalier: not the Strauss opera, but the Hofmannsthal libretto spoken. The stage is glistening white, with numberless candles at its three edges; it looks like a giant wedding-cake, with a canopy billowing out over the auditorium.

The production, like all the best Glasgow Citizens' work, balances the crude and the exquisite, horseplay and heartbreak on-stage at the same moment: something you often read about but rarely see. Robert David Macdonald as the lecherous Baron Ochs shows off the wit of his own translation: 'Call this a chambre séparée?' he splutters as a dozen musicians in animal masks invade a restaurant (yes, another one) to come between him and the object of his intentions.

There are rough patches in Katherine Kitovitz's Marschallin (the prime role of mature lady regretfully surrendering young lover), but there are also grace and unusual command. Acting libretti should not become a habit, but Hofmannsthal was a special playwright, and this is the finest boudoir bittersweet.

Even Mr Prowse's design is excelled by that of Max Bignens for Jorge Lavelli's production of Lorca's Doña Rosita, the Spinster, brought to the Lyceum by the Nuria Espert Company of Spain. A cool semi-circle of a room—a draped skeleton—is used to frame a production notable not for the smouldering passion we vaguely expect of Latin actors, but for its unearthly precision. The language barrier adds, if anything, to the effect.

Espert herself brings off a complete transition into a hopeful young girl; then ages back into a forsaken woman whom love and a new social order have passed by. Lorca plucks new resonances from a simple classical line. Wonderfully acted, sung and danced, this is the finest production to have visited us in years. There must be a London stage for it.

I don't know how it would be greeted by the 'new modern audience' envisaged by John McGrath in a programme note to his production for General Gathering, a new department of 7:84. (Send Mr McGrath one of those T-shirts.) They might be bored, but they would not be insulted, as they would surely be by Mr McGrath's Women in Power, a re-working of two Greek comedies that is ham-fisted, ham-footed and—the greatest blow of all from Mr McGrath—ham-brained.

Desperately it sends itself up in a manner only tolerable in the very best productions. There must be a way—though I have never seen it—of making Aristophanes work for us, but this Harry MacStophanies, rudely grabbing at anything that might be called 'relevant' (ie, anything involving women and/or politics) is a non-starter. I missed the second half. There were, after all, 750 other shows on.

I picked from the fringe an off-Broadway revue, Starting Here, Starting Now, an assemblage of love-and-nervousness songs: good lyrics by Richard Maltby Jr, and from David Shire music recognisably in the Broadway tradition, but free of the clichés: rich in melody, compulsive in rhythm. I counted 11 first-class numbers. Three American kids, calling themselves Castle Productions, sang them delightfully and the staging was really neat.
