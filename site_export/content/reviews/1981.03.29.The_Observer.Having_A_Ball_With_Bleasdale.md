Having a ball with Bleasdale

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on an ingenious new comedy, a stand-up comic and Scottish Molière.

It happened at the beginning of the century and it's happening again. Northern comedy is providing us with the best and the most entertaining images of the way we live now. At one extreme, Alan Ayckbourn; at the other, Hull Truck. And somewhere in between, penetrating the capital with increasing frequency, the Liverpool crowd.

After the sexual and surgical frolics of Funny Peculiar, it was a cinch somebody would write a comedy about vasectomy. After Flying Blind, a tragic farce about Ulster, it was on the cards that a playwright would take a further step and write in similar style about the possibility of nuclear war. The surprise is that both these possible plays have been written in one: Alan Bleasdale's Having a Ball, due at the Lyric, Hammersmith, in May, scheduled for broadcasting as part of Radio Theatre '81 (barring intervention by the censorious gremlins who have bedevilled this project so far), and available right now at the Coliseum, Oldham.

The Coliseum is a theatre that has found an audience and saved itself from extinction by putting on new plays, rather as the Glasgow Citizens' has done by finding an individual style for putting on old ones. The presumption in both cases is that people are being offered an experience unobtainable anywhere else.

Any audience, faced with a comedy on vasectomy, might well feel that it could stay home and write all the jokes itself. One of Mr Bleasdale's achievements is that he manages to avoid the obvious right the way through: up to the final curtain, in fact, when he slips in an expected pun as a sort of festive farewell.

He does devote a fair amount of time to expected fears. His play is set in a Greater Manchester private clinic, with three male candidates in its waiting-room. One is attending for the third or fourth time this week, having fled at the last moment every day so far. His name is Lenny and he goes through life in a state best described as perky panic. He boasts 'such a flair for words that I've recently been described as that clever twat who thinks he knows everything.'

Some playwrights would let him get away with it, too; Mr Bleasdale makes him a figure of slapstick, and by doing so makes us respect him. He delivers his most eloquent speech in a state of terror, with no clothes on, overheard by a drunken lady called Doreen, whose appearance causes him considerable embarrassment, much compounded when she asks him to make love to her.

Lenny and Doreen are united in terror. Both have enemies. Lenny's is the tough smoothie who bullied him at school and who is now awaiting the same operation. Doreen's is her husband, the third patient, who happens to be a county councillor and the focus for her own horror of the bomb -- the same horror that has decided Lenny to have no more children. Both Lenny and Doreen can be irritating people, and Mr Bleasdale is fair enough to let us see that -- even to show us Doreen in a wife-battering scene, where it's her husband, alternating violence and remorse, who is truly his own victim.

Meanwhile the surgeon, a woman, is having troubles with her nurse, a man (and in this reproduction a dead ringer, vocally and physically, for Russell Harty, though this provides only incidental amusement). One of the clinic's specialities is abortion, which he is conscientiously against, and she is conscientiously for. The play is building up a series of arguments on -- to put it frigidly -- the prevention of life, while creating involving characters and building up a fine farcical structure. That is quite a lot.

It builds to a steaming climax, involving official plans for post-catastrophe survival. These plans are both frightening and hilarious. Mr Bleasdale goes for fright, which he achieves, but at the price of unbalancing the play. He has to slide to a conclusion which is that, where possible, life goes on. I wasn't sure what weight he wanted us to attach to this and I wasn't sure that he was sure. Kenneth Alan Taylor's Oldham production is something to catch or to envy; David Ross's Lenny, rabbity and rabbiting, is a hero for our time.

* * *

One of his sprucer spiritual cousins was in London last week, prior to returning to Lancashire, where his gigs this week include the NUS conference in Blackpool: Ernie Dalton of the North West Spanner company, in a routine called I A Mongrel. I caught him at the Hemingford Arms in Islington; appropriate enough since he's actually a Londoner, in the Jim Dale mould.

He does too much shouting into the mike, but he tells good jokes, though unfortunately he suffers from the delusion that there are certain hate-figures whose names are good for a laugh in themselves: as the Ayatollah for American comics, so Thatcher for him. He's a bit more subtle on Heath, but he's best, naturally, when attacking his own side, whether N.W. Spanner itself ('We'd tell the people, "Socialism that way." They'd say, "y'what?"') or his old man who used to talk about the long march to victory or the iron heel of capitalism. ('Politics was all feet to him.')

* * *

There's a new company in Scotland: the Scottish Theatre Company, masterminded by Ewan Hooper who founded Greenwich (the theatre, I mean). They aim to put leading Scottish actors either into new native plays or classics that can plausibly be given a Scots slant, and to tour the big theatres with them.

Their first offering is Let Wives Tak Tent, adapted by Robert Kemp from Moliere's School for Wives. I saw it at the magnificent Theatre Royal, Glasgow. This week it plays the King's, Edinburgh. It sets the play in fact in Edinburgh, behind one of those grey stone facades that look like fortresses, with embrasures for windows; a fearsome place for a girl to be locked up and one which lends considerable point to Moliere's comedy of incipient marital tyranny.

I greatly enjoyed what I could understand (almost the first word sounded to me like 'polygateeries') and found both shape and bite in a work which had previously seemed to me strictly a one-joker. In another sense this production does boast one joker: Rikki Fulton with a full mastery of avaricious double-takes, and considerable grotesque poignancy when he realises himself to be a victim of love. A good week for comics.
