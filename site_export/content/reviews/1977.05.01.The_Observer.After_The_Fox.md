After the fox

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

A CRITIC once described the late Donald Wolfit as 'the master of Renaissance excess.' It is not a mastery which anyone else in our theatre has visibly inherited. Two of the Elizabethan supermen whom Wolfit patented during his lifetime have now reappeared at the National Theatre in productions by Peter Hall; Jonson's 'Volpone' followed Marlowe's 'Tamburlaine' into the Olivier last Tuesday.

They are both outstandingly well-ordered productions. 'Tamburlaine' took full note of Marlowe's debt to medieval tradition, and 'Volpone' pays more of the same dues, along with a few more ancient. Jonson the classicist would certainly have appreciated the clarity of Mr Hall's production, both as narrative and morality. He does particularly well by the closing trial scenes, with their lightning series of reversals of fortune; I have never before seen these staged comprehensibly.

Earlier he lets us see exactly what is at stake in Volpone's attempted seduction of Celia. White innocence (well aware of its own merits in Morag Hood's delicious performance), is stalked by red luxuriance, a fittingly ambiguous term denoting both wealth and lechery. He woos her with a display of gold, and with an even more impressive display of learning, proposing that they re-enact together most of the erotic myths in Ovid. It is, until the last violent moment, aesthetic rape.

With Volpone the aesthete Paul Scofield is, as you would expect, thoroughly at home. The sonorous voice caresses the cadences, and there is enough heroic pride in him to make his final reckless self-exposure a believable (and thrilling) moment. He is the most bushily resplendent of foxes. But it is not enough. Critics are supposed to declare their prejudices, so here are mine. Memories of Wolfit's Volpone (which I saw only on television but that was enough) have conditioned my reading of the play: a greasier, dustier one than the Hall-Scofield combination provides.

Theirs is a world in which vices are emblematically displayed, verbally anatomised, but never solidly realised. Again, Jonson might approve (such restraint is perhaps the comic equivalent of keeping your tragic deaths offstage) and the production probably does full justice to his icy intent. It fails to account, though, for his less calculated qualities: for the gusto (not just the intellectual excitement) of his plotting, and for the excess spleen which turns his stereotypes into monsters. Thus Hugh Paddick's Corbaccio is a pretty neat sketch of senility but hardly virulent enough to justify the rhetorical abuse he evokes. Michael Medwin goes through the motions of Corvino's jealousy without ever probing its brutality. (The casting is as acquisitive as Volpone himself; just about everybody comes direct from a big success in something or other.)

Belief in Mr Scofield himself snaps when he attempts actually to ravish Celia; if that magniloquent figure were had up on a rape-charge he would not have to resort to Volpone's extremes to get off. He misses the earthiness of Volpone as Albert Finney missed the airy part of Tamburlaine; if these two might somehow be combined we could have a Wolfit-and-a-quarter, capable of versatility within a role.

Ben Kingsley makes a very nifty Mosca. John Gielgud gives a charming sketch of the English tourist Sir Politic (his fear that he may become 'a thing for tavern talk' is the evening's most memorable line, an improvement, as it happens, on Jonson's original) and Elizabeth Spriggs has no trouble with his spouse, tyrannically rouged and fearsomely loquacious.

* * *

Meanwhile at the Cottesloe we have had The Passion, derived from the York Mystery Cycle. There seems to be a belief, lurking somewhere at the National, that 'popular theatre' can be achieved simply by performing these plays in modern dress with reconstructed Pennine accents. Since the original performers were amateur and indigenous, where these are professional and from all over, the belief is ingenuous; there are actually many levels of artifice involved here, and the deployment of a folk-rock band, an artificial construct in itself, emphasises the point. Nothing is more calculated than an atmospheric pause for song.

The strength of the plays is altogether different; it arises when the authors almost lose sight of the Biblical story and write out of their own lives. Here too there is chronological confusion (are these torturers, British workmen who can't even get a crucifixion done on time, operating in the first or the fifteenth century AD?) but of a different order from that which the directors, Bill Bryden and Sebastian Graham-Jones, have introduced. Their concoction, though, is enjoyable, sometimes impressive, best seen from the balconies above, best experienced from ground-level where you mingle with the actors. The Albion Dance Band provide both joyous and chilling moments, and Brian Glover is a sensibly native addition to the company; he and Pitt Wilkinson make the priests the most convincing figures around. Richard Johnson's Pilate, a befuddled Lord Mayor, is a pleasing performance but unpersuasive; I don't believe that is how the authors actually saw him, though I appreciate that it would be nice to think so.

* * *

Oh, Mr. Porter (Mermaid) has the misfortune to be a double sequel; wittingly to 'Cole,' unwittingly to 'A Chorus Line.' Here the performers, duly employed, arrive for rehearsal to find themselves deserted. They break into a series of Cole Porter songs, 40 in all, and we could do with more as a relief from Benny Green's dialogue. This is mainly composed of random bitching, and even on that level it sounds inauthentic. It has nothing to do with the songs (compare Peter Bogdanovich's film 'At Long Last Love' which furnished characters fit to sing them) and seems intended as a parody of some form in Mr Green's head. Even his understanding of the music seems defective; the serenely triumphant 'I Concentrate on You' is introduced as 'an unrequited love song.'

The cast are required to spend a good deal of time looking indulgently at one another; none is better at this than Kenneth Nelson, who perches and lounges with a seraphic smile as if the others were things in his (happy) dream. He looks more like Porter now than when impersonating him in 'Cole,' and he phrases his share of the lyrics with becoming care; only Jeanette Ranger, of the rest, comes near him. Concerted numbers can be fun; having disinterred Porter's worst song ('Ladies in Waiting') from his worst score ('Les Girls') they perversely brought down the house with it. The show closes with more choruses of 'You're the Top' than I have ever heard at one sitting before; that may sound an arcane recommendation but enthusiasts will take the hint.
