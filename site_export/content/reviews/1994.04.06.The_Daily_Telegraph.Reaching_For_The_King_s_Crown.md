GOODBYE, Renaissance Theatre Company. Or, at the very least, au revoir. The announcement of the company's closure stresses that there will be no more Renaissance films but says that the theatrical end of the operation will retain a nominal existence "in case we want to resurrect it". So there could be a renaissance of Renaissance. But I doubt if anyone is betting on it.

The obvious thought is that the company has been the victim of its own success. Or, more precisely, of Kenneth Branagh's success. The company's moving spirit is currently directing Robert De Niro in Frankenstein. When you are in demand for that grade of movie project, running a theatre company must be difficult to fit in. The wonder may be that he has managed to keep it going at all for seven years.

Some more obvious heavyweights have done less well. When the young Orson Welles went out to Hollywood he took his Mercury Theatre actors from New York with him. The Mercury label was attached to his first two movies, Citizen Kane and The Magnificent Ambersons, but neither the name nor the acting ensemble were heard from subsequently. Albert Finney, despite associations with the National Theatre, the Royal Court, and an outfit of his own, has never found a long-term theatrical niche. Ian McKellen originated from the Actors' Company in the 1970s and devoted himself to it for three years. Despite its co-operative ideals, it was never the same after he left.

Renaissance started with some of the same professed aims as the Actors' Company. It was to be an expression of actors, rather than of directors and administrators. It was to present an alternative - though without that word's political overtones - to the established classical companies. It never claimed to be egalitarian.

Branagh has directed and/or starred in all the productions and he even wrote the first play. It has been not so much his showcase as his springboard. It has propelled him into areas of success that he might have reached in any case, but never as quickly. The big leap, of course, was Henry V. To direct and star in the film soon after acting the role on stage was to court inevitable comparisons with Laurence Olivier. And however British critics debate the differences between the two, on a global scale that courtship has succeeded.

I live in Canada, where it is taken for granted, even by people who have never seen him, that Branagh is the world's leading Shakespearean actor. And, like Olivier, he owes that position to his films. He has made only two Shakespeare movies to Olivier's four, but that is two more than most actors can claim. And he has gone beyond Olivier in one respect: Olivier filmed two histories and two tragedies, all with himself in the title-role, all of them - as Shakespeare plays go - natural movie subjects; Branagh went on to do Much Ado About Nothing, riskier because a comedy (dependent on atmosphere, full of obscure jokes) and an ensemble piece. His own laughing-boy performance of Benedick was actually one of the weaker elements. But the film worked.

Olivier once said that directing movies was the most exciting thing he ever did. But he came late to it and only directed one mainstream (that is, non-Shakespearean) picture: the undistinguished The Prince and the Showgirl. Branagh, starting young, has moved insouciantly on to Hollywood's own turf, directing thriller spoofs and horror pictures. He seems to be, as Olivier never was, a career film director.

But Branagh is not, as Olivier was, a career film actor. Here it is worth looking at the two actors' early careers and at what they tell us about British theatre now and between the wars.

Olivier staked his claim as a classical actor at the Old Vic in 1937 and 1938, beginning with Hamlet. He was 30, already known in the West End as a juvenile lead and with a solid grounding at Birmingham Rep.

Branagh, like most of the meteors of his generation, bypassed rep (which barely exists now, in the permanent-company sense); although, with his chipper solidity, he is one of the few who can be imagined doing time there. He made his mark, straight out of drama school, in the West End play Another Country, and was next heard of at Stratford playing Henry V. He was 21, and brought to the role a heaven-sent mixture of beamish youth and iron-clad confidence. After that he founded Renaissance, which has kept him, despite the filmic excursions, constantly in the theatre: in London, Stratford, Chichester, and on national and international tours.

The young Olivier was more fickle. After the Old Vic he went to Hollywood, only returning to the stage towards the end of the war, again at the Old Vic, which he and Ralph Richardson (co-stars and co-directors) took to unprecedented heights. After that it was movies again; plays, too, of course, but never for long runs. In fact it was not until he became director of the National Theatre in 1963 that you could be sure, theatrically, where to find him. From 1974, when he left the National, until the end of his life he was wholly a movie man.

It's a smaller world now, certainly for British actors. Paradoxically, while American actors have to choose between the East Coast and the West, stage and screen, Britons seem able to hop continents with relative freedom. The end of Renaissance may signal Branagh's intention to devote himself to films, but it doesn't have to - and if it does, it is more likely to be as a director.

One obvious difference between the two films of Henry V is that Olivier's was made as wartime propaganda while Branagh's came out of the more customary 20th-century mood of military disillusion. But that is not the only reason why Olivier trumpeted his speeches, while Branagh talked them. They were playing in their personal styles, Olivier's the more exciting and memorable, Branagh's the more congenial and credible.

For a star actor, Branagh is uncannily unmanicured, which may mean that he is not actually a star. Or, more exactly, not a movie star, which Olivier certainly was. Although the cinema is supposed to reject rhetoric and rejoice in naturalism, it has never been too keen on ordinary chappiness. (Dustin Hoffman is not ordinary, and neither, even when young, was Marlon Brando.) Live audiences, however, like to be talked to in their own language, especially when it's done with flair and cheek.

Branagh is a terrific comic: something the movies have so far failed to capture. I loved his performance as Peter Quince in his own production of A Midsummer Night's Dream: a village savant who, when it came to rehearsing his rude mechanicals, suddenly mutated into Erich von Stroheim, complete with megaphone. Critics protested at the inconsistency, apparently not realising it was precisely the image this Quince, who had undoubtedly read too many books about directing, would think appropriate. It was also a nice joke on his own position as actor-manager. Renaissance established no individual style or ethic, and it did disappointingly little to bring on young actors, but it did do something equally important: it recharged the batteries of older ones, encouraging Judi Dench and Geraldine McEwan to try their hands at directing and establishing Richard Briers as a major classical performer.

Olivier's record as a commercial manager was far less distinguished; people forget that he even had one. Branagh must be sick of the comparison, but he must be aware that if an actor is ever again to run the National, he - with the exception of McKellen - is the only one to have staked a claim.
