How the Dickens?

Robert Cushman on Our Mutual Friend

A TELEVISION serial of the late 50s first turned me on to Our Mutual Friend (hereinafter referred to as OMF in deference to one of the novel's heroes, Eugene Wrayburn, who use MRF as spoken shorthand for My Respected Father). Since then I must have read it three or four times. So I came to the new version with both the book and the earlier dramatisation strongly in mind.

OMF Mark One followed highly successful television accounts of David Copperfield and Nicholas Nickleby and was inhabited by much the same group of players. Esmond Knight and Fay Compton, a previous year's Squeerses, were redeployed as stumpily villainous Silas Wegg and virtuously independent Betty Higden. Their invaluably fruity character work reinforced the efforts of youngish players (Paul Daneman, Zena Walker and Rachel Roberts) from the general direction of the Old Vic, in the sober bits.

Some of the juice of the book was drained out

Despite all this talent, that attempt at OMF was not a success. Dickens's last completed novel has about five complex plots all inter-connecting remorselessly enough to intimidate the boldest script editor. The early serial succumbed with hardly a struggle and merely put the book on the screen blow by blow, personage by personage. The result was bewildering: there was too much to keep up with.

The 1976 adaptors were more sophisticated. They cut through the novel's chronology, slicing the action up into large packages, allowing us to lose sight of a given strand for two weeks at a time instead of trying to juggle them all at once. There was a gain in clarity, but some of the juice of the book was drained out. For by the time he wrote it the abrupt juxtapositions of Dickens's narrative lines had become more than the necessary mannerism of a serial novelist: human interdependence was itself his subject. Rich and poor, solemn and farcical, batten on each other and will hardly be prised loose.

This version heroically managed to lop off one group of characters; the adventuring Lammles, who marry each other's non-existent money, were sacrificed, and with them the smooth-faced Fledgeby, the Christian usurer. Unfortunately the exiled figures took with them Dickens's sharpest treatment of his central theme -- which, since so much is focused on the riches of a dust contractor, may quite precisely be defined as money-grubbing. The production was certainly aware of its importance; an actor had only to mention money for the camera to zoom in on him as sharply as if it were his agent. BBC Dickens is far more intellectual now than in the 50s; modern critics have been read and their emphasis on symbols and images taken to heart.

Peter Hammond is certainly the director to realise them; if he made little of the famous dust heaps, he did a fine job on the Thames which, if you tuned your set right, looked grey, green and greasy enough for the Limpopo. It was once said that you could film a Dickens novel using the book itself as your shooting script, and this might have been Hammond's method. His police station, for example, had just the cold monastic air prescribed, complete with priestly inspector. The nouveaux riches Veneerings and their dinner guests were first glimpsed in a mirror; just as Dickens -- in one of his stylistic tours de force -- presents them to us. This was a momentary treat for purists but it made no point and heralded no thorough presentation of the characters. No one unfamiliar with the book would have known why there were so many of them. They are the devil to dramatise -- most of their life comes from the author's metallic commentary -- and they have no effect on the plot; but they do matter to the structure. The voice of society, heard in a series of self-contained, satiric episodes, affects our attitude to the more volatile characters who get on with the action.

And strange things happened to this action. The visual style and Carl Davis's screeching music were keyed to melodrama, but the acting was not. It was naturalistic, and, in its own way, truthful. Lesley Dunlop was believable as Lizzie Hexam, teenage daughter of a Limehouse water-rat; Nicholas Jones as Eugene certainly looked like a dissolute briefless barrister. Neither of them, though, was much like a figure in Dickens. They lacked fluency. Mumbling or groping for words they were like Shakespearean actors ignoring the verse. Eugene's banter with his friend Mortimer was unforgivably subdued, losing both their entertainment value and their meaning.

Dickens has been rescued from teatime viewing

It was in humour and in traditional warmth of personality that this serial compared least favourably with its predecessor, which was at least a highly seasoned muddle. Only Boffin and Wegg were large enough. Leo McKern may have been too authoritative for Boffin, but he acted richly enough to conquer even my preconceptions; Alfie Bass was possibly too lovable for Wegg, but Dickens's own execution here is at odds with his conception. The sad truth is that Dickensian actors are now unfashionable actors, and the growth of a subdued television style has made them so. Casting was easier 20 years ago.

Maybe producers should note that Dickens criticism is already changing tack and looking with renewed favour on the extrovert early books. I would not myself trust a Dickensian who didn't laugh aloud at Pickwick Papers, and now that Dickens has been rescued from teatime viewing and restored to his rightful place among the grown-ups, it is here -- at the other extreme of his development -- that the BBC might venture next. It deserves all the time in the world and should get it. But, after two attempts, OMF still remains uncracked.

Robert Cushman is drama critic of the Observer.
