Stars that don't shine

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on O'Casey, at the National Theatre

THE National Theatre production of The Plough and the Stars has set two platitudes warring against each other in my mind. The first is that foundations should be built upon; the second is the one about lightning not striking twice in the same place.

Bill Bryden's success with The Playboy of the Western World suggested a natural progression from Synge to O'Casey. On this occasion, though the contrary principle asserts itself; the plough lumbers forward, and the stars refuse to shine.

Much of the blame must go to O'Casey. Reaction to the Playboy was certainly coloured by Kenneth Tynan's reference, apropos of an earlier revival, to "quaint pastoral rumbskulls." I don't know that they are any worse than the quaint urban numbskulls who people O'Casey's drama, and at least Synge saw his characters steadily and unsentimentally.

O'Casey's are liable to turn, quite arbitrarily, into heroes. That's life, we are obviously meant to think; that's theatre, we actually respond. For, until they are needed to make some gesture or other, the Fluther Goods and Bessie Burgesses of this play function purely as comic relief. It is amazing, considering how high his reputation stands among modern clowns, how little the play -- as opposed to the stage-directions -- tells us about Fluther. His one characteristic -- his pretence that he has given up drinking -- is strictly from stock.

Cyril Cusack's performance of him here is discretion itself. He shows, uniquely in this cast, the ability to stand silent and yet, unmistakably, to belong. He can trip off a neat bit of alliteration ("fluther a feather of Fluther") and bring the house down.

But such a downbeat rendition can hardly carry the play; not in the face of the support -- ranging from pallor to outright nonentity -- that his colleagues offer here. Anna Manahan's mountainous Bessie is an exception, but even she plays the avenging angel to the exclusion of the squabbling street-trader. She attaches herself, in fact, to the "serious" half of the play, an area surely better avoided. Here dwell Jack and Nora Clitheroe, he devoted to the Irish Citizen Army, she to keeping him home and out of it. He is killed in the Easter Rising of 1916, she loses her baby and runs mad.

This last scene suggests how much of Bryden's dramaturgy O'Casey learned from Shakespeare. Shakespeare, however, left no characters safe from irony; the compartmentalisation of his plays is more apparent than real. O'Casey, carefully fencing off the Clitheroes, wound up with traditional melodrama. What, remembering her fine Pegeen Mike, can one say of Susan Fleetwood, the National's honorary Irishwoman, except that a character who talks of herself with such winsome consistency in the third person ("your little red-lipped Nora") scarcely allows the actress a way in.

All this might cease to matter if the play's essential counterpoint could be established; if the small dramas on stage could be highlighted by the greater events off. It might easier be done in a small cockpit theatre, but in the vast Olivier it is difficult to imagine an off-stage; everything has to happen on. The play accordingly becomes a failed epic; we hear occasionally about bloody street battles, mob orators or rampaging looters; we actually see three actors standing at the corner of a wide stage, unable to fill it with their own drama, let alone to suggest another one.

And O'Casey's fabled richness of dialogue becomes impossible to judge, since it is so difficult to hear; a fault which, in my insular way, I would blame less on the Olivier acoustics than on a team of Irish actors taking their own idiom for granted.

* * *

The production is marginally slower than Troilus and Cressida (Aldwych), but it's a close-run, or close-plodded, thing. For the first couple of scenes the Royal Shakespeareans seemed to be taking their time from some faulty metronome in the wings, one which so absorbed their attention that they hardly remembered to speak to one another.

As the evening proceeded they came to life a bit, though David Waller's Pandarus surrendered no jot of deliberation. He also retains (in a production that has, by and large, put away childish things since it left Stratford) some mirthless comic business with a phallic pillow. This play, too, is hampered by its stage; a far bigger piece than The Plough and the Stars, it might yet work suggestively well in a genuinely intimate area; here it has a goodly-sized one hemmed in by the season's permanent set.

Outsize Meccano strips inserted between the timbers do little to suggest either the plains or the palaces of Troy, and the whole thing has an undeniably shoddy air. Only one performance, Francesca Annis's Cressida, is a full-scale creation, but Tony Church (Ulysses) and John Nettles (Thersites) make the most of their set-pieces, as does Michael Pennington (Hector) when forced unwillingly to contemplate Troy's destruction.

At other moments, Mr Pennington retreats into recent RSC history and intones; so, too often, does Mike Gwilym as Troilus. He is best when intently outlining the limits of love; worst when lamenting its loss.

* * *

Willy Russell in Breeze-block Park (Mermaid) makes obvious points about consumerism, narrow-mindedness and family tyranny (all different faces of the same monster) as experienced on a northern council estate. He makes them, though, with considerable wit, even if it is not the bounding ingenious kind of the same stable's Funny Peculiar.

He has been almost too generous in the creation of his central monster, played by Wendy Craig with contagious relish if not always with total authenticity. The plot revolves around her daughter, who wants to leave her and experience the good life (long tables loaded with wine and cheese) with her student boy-friend. Mr Russell never actually says that this life will be as empty as the one she wants to leave, but the hint is there and, if taken, it enriches the play.
