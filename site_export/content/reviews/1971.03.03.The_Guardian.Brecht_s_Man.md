Brecht's man

Robert Cushman talks to producer William Gaskill

THERE MUST be those around the Royal Court who cannot quite believe that Brecht's "Man is Man" is actually being staged there. In fact, the present production might almost be regarded as the tenth anniversary celebration of an idea which has been in train since 1961. At that time, of course, the Royal Court's director was the late George Devine, and his principal colleague Tony Richardson. They were assisted by a clutch of young producers, prominent among them Devine's eventual successor, William Gaskill. "Man is Man" is his first Brecht show in some years; all the same, he ranks as the leading practitioner in the (English) field with productions--variously successful--of "The Caucasian Chalk Circle," "Baal," and "Mother Courage" to his credit.

"Man is Man" sets out to demonstrate that a man can be anything you make him. (You are society and you have a lot to answer for.) To survive, Galy Gay, the most pacific of civilians, is forced to take on the identity of Jeriah Jip, the most bloodthirsty of soldiers. Here at any rate is one part that cannot be type-cast. To prove the point: the Devine-Richardson idea was to mount the production as a vehicle for the mountainous Zero Mostel; Gaskill's lead is the infinitesimal Henry Woolf. There was a halfway point at which he envisaged Tony Hancock in the role, a scheme which, alas, went the way of all attempts to involve Hancock in the Higher Drama.

With all this intriguing casting in the air, the decade's delay seems almost inexplicable. High-flying ideas can, of course, be self-defeating and the task of tying down Zero Mostel to dates across the width of the Atlantic must have been a daunting one. Anyway, in the spring of 1962 he went, impenetrably, into a musical. "Only if it flops could he be available," wrote Eric Bentley to Devine. "The New Haven notices, for what comfort it may give you, are bad!" But the show was "A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum" and the New York notices were good.

Bentley's angle was the possibility of the Court using his translation (or one of his translations; he is a tireless reviser). And it was the translation problem which proved even more of an obstacle than the casting. For one thing it hindered the choice of a director, Richardson disliking the Bentley version (which Mostel approved) but unable to come up with a substitute.

Another factor was Stefan Brecht. Brecht's son, American-based, controls the English-speaking rights of his father's plays and he is inclined to hold rigid views about them. For "Man is Man" he stipulated a "Kiplingesque sophisticated music hall quality." One can see the point of the music hall bit, if only because the play contains songs and is constructed as a series of routines. As for Kipling, the action is presumed to take place in British India (though hardly in any era that an historian would recognise) and the association is obvious.

All the same, "Kiplingesque" is an easier word to throw round than to realise in a text; Devine and Gaskill (who by now was slated to direct) thought that Henry Livings might be the man to do it but since Stefan Brecht insisted on vetting a translation before actually agreeing to it, and since Livings was well past the stage of writing on spec, nothing came of it. John Arden, another bright idea, was too tied up with his own plays to undertake it, and in 1968 the most obvious choice of all--Charles Wood--found himself faced with the same unacceptable conditions as Livings.

Besides, Gaskill himself was far from sure that the translation could, or even should, be Kiplingesque.

Gaskill eventually came to think that even if Charles Wood were available "it would mean writing a new play and I am rather keen to do the play as written." Stefan Brecht's thoughts were still of Kipling. "There must," he declared a little puzzlingly, "be many talented young Irishmen around" who would leap at the assignment.

As far as is known no Irish versions arrived on Gaskill's desk but a straight translation by a virtually unknown young playwright named Steve Gooch did. It proved satisfactory to Gaskill, and even got past Stefan Brecht. Maybe he reckoned that 10 years was enough. Gaskill (who declared after his unsuccessful "Mother Courage" at the National Theatre that he would never tackle Brecht again without a first-rate translation ("I think he's too good an author to muck about")) has come to like the Gooch version very much.

"It's very speakable and he has a London ear which has stopped the actors from giving the peasant characters the usual Summerset treatment. English actors just don't look or sound like peasants anyway but they have a very strong 'Dad's Army' tradition of playing soldiers."

The mention of "Dad's Army" prompted a thought about the casting. Henry Woolf has a good deal of the stand-up comic in him and the other leads (Georgia Brown, Trevor Peacock, Roddy Maude-Roxby) are widely experienced in light entertainment. There was no schematic planning about this but, says Gaskill, "the play needs a strong, warm, earthy sort of actor." And he himself feels freer with it than he did with "Courage" since here he has no Berliner Ensemble production to provide a model.

The production might indeed mark a break-through for him. Allegiance to Brecht has given his previous work a dryness which has sometimes run to pedantry. He now wants to move into something rowdier. His notes to the actors have tended to boil down into the classic comedy injunctions to "keep it loud, fast, and funny." (One interesting maxim: "If you have a line like 'It's so dreary' sound cheerful about it.")

"Man is Man" is early Brecht and not strongly political, though the central image of a man brainwashed into another identity has as many reverberations as anyone could wish. "There's a very strange line: 'The difference between Yes and No is not great.' That bears looking at in the light of his later temporising heroes like Galileo and of his own life. The great Brechtian theme is survival. Survival whatever."

IN JULY 1926 Bertolt Brecht abandoned the play he was writing about the American wheat market. He discussed his reasons for doing so with his secretary who noted down a couple of sentences. They were: "Such matters aren't dramatic; and if one writes them up they aren't true any longer, and the drama ceases to be anything of the sort: and as soon as one sees that the modern world is no longer reconcilable with its drama then the drama can no longer be reconciled with the world..."

By "such matters," Brecht meant the way in which the market worked: the social structure; the machinery; the weather; the movement of history. Brecht is essentially a social writer. When he explores the nature of individuality (as in "Man is Man") it is in relation to the society to which the individual belongs.

Brecht's critics often shrug this off as "Marxism" but it is important to realise that his principal ideas were written in both plays and criticism (his "alienation theory" was first propounded in 1922) some years before he became a Marxist. In other words Brecht revealed the degree to which the thought of the twentieth century has been permeated without even realising it by Marxist social and historic ideas. He is a constant reminder that one should distinguish between Marxism and communism as between Christianity and any individual Church.

In the past 150 years Western civilisation has begun to study emotions, history, and social structures in a more systematic manner than before. No doubt there is a connection between the growth of these "scientific" studies and the decline of religious belief; that is not so relevant here as the fact that relatively new disciplines such as psychology, sociology, and economics have transformed the terms by which people define themselves.

Shakespeare's history plays, for example, actually succeed in dramatising a philosophy of history and are based on the best research work of their time. Their argument is that the overthrow of a however foolish legitimate order leads to universal discord. What is important is that the theatrical conventions of the day allowed Shakespeare to express these ideas without any strain on credibility. But the gardener's speech in "Richard II" is a classic example of intellectual ideas conveyed in a way that neither mocks the story nor challenges the suspension of disbelief.

Shakespeare could write plays like this, and through his minor characters suggest an entire social fabric, because his age regarded history as a book from which one could draw morals. The illustrations were the deeds, wicked or otherwise, of great men. The emphasis here is upon "deeds": history was a succession of incidents determined by will and character, and thus the very stuff of drama. Representations of decisive human actions are what Brecht means by "dramatic" in the discussion with his secretary.

Acting out a story is the thing, and when society believes that the most significant truths are displayed in the actions of one man the drama has its high moments, as in "Oedipus" and "Hamlet." The essence so far as the drama is concerned of the intellectual changes of the past 150 years is that we no longer believe that the significant truth about even one man can be told through his actions alone. We look not simply to willed action, but to a complexity of social, psychological, and historical facts of which the man himself may be entirely unaware.

Bernard Shaw was just as alive to this as Brecht but his solution was a trick. He wrote the play as though it was still the eighteenth century and put the intervening and vital information in the preface. Brecht knew that to be serious one must put the information in the play, and it is his grappling with this problem in both theory and practice that makes him so important. The whole "experimental" side of his work as a writer comes down to an attempt to pack in more information than the theatre had previously been able to carry.

The trappings of many Brecht plays, the placards, songs, bizarre locations, projections, films, and intoned narrations are all attempts to convey information: so really are the alienation effects and the theories about sport and the smokers' theatre. The idea here is not just to make the audience receptive to more information, but to persuade them to employ what they already have.

Consider for example, the BBC's "Henry VIII" series. Are we really to believe that the English Reformation and great shifts in political power, wealth, and economic organisation came about because a woman refused to be divorced? Or is the movement of history more complicated? Brecht's challenge would be (see his classic critique of the film of "Gunga Din") that if we conveniently forget what we know to enjoy a romantic diversion why should we not remember it again for a serious work of art?

To this attitude there is a vital qualification. For all his commitment to socialism and scientific appraisal Brecht was not a utopian. He believed in the survival of the human spirit but not that it is perfect or even very reliable. This is the source of his most characteristic ambiguities, like the end of both "Galileo" and "Man is Man." It is in the bitter sweet tone of his best work the flitting from emotion to emotion that he most effectively presents a new kind of information about life.

This is to say that he faces the problems of what is "dramatic" more squarely in his theory than in his actual writing: after all, he gave up the play about the wheat market. His best plays are traditional poetic images for the world his theories describe. They deal obliquely so that for all its dazzling newsreel analysis of a political upheaval the opening of "The Caucasian Chalk Circle" is still about a mythical central Asia and not the present age, just as "Man is Man" is about a never-never India. Yet what happens to Galy Gay in "Man is Man" is truly a nightmare.

It is true that for a Marxist Brecht is melancholy, and for a poet pedantic, and that sometimes he is very boring and makes his point too many times over. He is also a genius.
