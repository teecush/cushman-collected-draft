Dizzying fantasy

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a joyous revival by the RSC.

IT ASSUMED that Once in a Lifetime (Aldwych) would turn out the best thing of the year, but I wasn't prepared for it to be this good.

It is the play in which a movie executive upbraids a subordinate with the words, 'Do you know what you have done? You have made the wrong picture.' He then proceeds to announce a new studio rule: 'Every scenario we produce, somebody has got to read it.' I have quoted my two favourite lines - probably my two favourites in the whole of twentieth-century drama; there are plenty more where they came from.

America between the wars produced two great genre comedies - The Front Page and Once in a Lifetime - and both have now been given superlative British productions. The Front Page is much the grittier play of the two; its authors were writing, with as much cynicism as affection, out of their own experience in journalism. Once in a Lifetime approaches Hollywood at the onset of the talkies with awed disbelief.

Moss Hart, the original author, had never been anywhere near California. George S. Kaufman, who rewrote it with him, was wholly Broadway-bred. Of course they both knew the legends. So the play is a fantasy: an informed fantasy. This is its strength. It is not much good at sustained speeches. When the moment comes for a savage denunciation, all they can come up with is, 'I think Hollywood and this darling industry of yours is the most God-awful thing I've ever run into,' which is to the point, but hardly inspired.

It doesn't matter. There is a terrific energy about the play, as well as an enchanting buoyancy. Wisecracks are unstoppable, and so is the parade of characters. For the most part they are self-made monsters, the progeny of the silents, living by their own extravagant rules, which are only just beginning to be questioned by the talent necessarily imported from literature and the stage. Everybody who isn't actually in movies is either a fan, a parasite, or - most likely - an aspirant, filling in time as a bellboy or cigarette-girl. ('There's a call out for prostitutes... talking prostitutes.') Impromptu auditions - coming at him from the least expected quarters - are the hourly hazards of a producer's life.

The mogul we see most of is Herman Glogauer, who lives in fear anyway, since he is the man She once turned down sound. He is now inclined to say Yes to everything, and even to bargain with his arch-rivals, the Schlepkin Brothers, of whom there are 12, marching two abreast. (The last one, in this production, is black, which must have been a surprise to Mr Schlepkin Senior.) Out West come a washed-up vaudeville trio: ambitious Jerry, good-hearted May and George. George, though dapper and affectionate, is the complete chump. But it is he, achieving dizzying success through equally dizzying incompetence, who ends up running Hollywood. The Kaufman-Hart thesis seems to be that he still does, and always will. Could be.

Jerry is the least rewarding role of the three, though Peter McEnery, white-suited, pipe-smoking and sporting the most stylish of profiles, wittily hits off his attempt to play the Hollywood big-shot. But it is the other two who make the running; May will come up with a sardonic metaphor, George will take it literally, and that routine, repeated, accounts for much of the verbal humour.

The timing of Zoe Wanamaker and Richard Griffiths is quite unfaultable; and so are their characterisations. Miss Wanamaker, marvellously hard-boiled, also takes care in a properly brisk manner of the love-interest; and George is the crowning glory of Mr Griffiths's line of gentle buffoons. Indeed no jokes - which is to say no points - are missed in Trevor Nunn's joyous production. The supporting roles are all lovingly done, from David Suchet's glowering Glogauer and Paul Brooke's squeaky, paunchy genius-director (from Germany, natuerlich) to Carmen du Sautoy's angular, dismissive secretary and the horde of extras and electricians.

One of the delights of this generous show is that it actually takes us on set. John Napier's designs also run to a train, a Gold Room at the Hollywood Stilton, and - most dazzlingly - the reception room at Glogauer Studios, whose logo appears to be an elephant's head in glory, here flanked by two other tuskers, sitting up and begging. Even when the play ends, the show goes on, Gillian Lynne having choreographed the best closing number seen in London since A Chorus Line. This is the more remarkable when you consider (a) that Once in a Lifetime isn't a musical and (b) these are all straight actors.

* * *

But not every play can use such adornments and The Government Inspector (Old Vic) is merely impeded by having the local officials' dreams of St Petersburg periodically played out before our eyes. (This may derive from the Russian production of another Gogol play, Marriage, seen at last year's Edinburgh Festival.) It isn't even consistent since Klestakov, the supposed inspector, who fantasises just as much (hence the play's greatness), is allowed to do the job on his own: just as well, since Ian Richardson, after a slow start, plays a delicious cadenza of befuddled innocence.

There are also ripe Yorkshire performances by Hugh Sullivan and Barbara Jefford as the mayor and his wife; their colleagues gradually get in line behind them to present a believable homogenous picture of a comically corrupt society.

* * *

From the Gogol gang to The Gorky Brigade (Royal Court). These are the members of a youth colony in post-revolutionary Russia. Originally juvenile delinquents, they are converted to ideological solidarity (i.e. to replenishing the grain-store instead of depleting it) when it is pointed out to them that extorting tribute from rich peasants can be not only fun but politically admirable.

The lapse of a few years finds them in communal charge. Disillusion has set in; theirs is the only colony there is, they have no prospects outside it, and a visit from Gorky himself, on his return from the West (nobody asks him why he went), ends with him being belaboured by a college student whose professor is being prosecuted for reaching unauthorised scientific conclusions.

An interesting enough story is efficiently enough told by Nicholas Wright, though for the most part in a false-naive style (I hope and believe it's false) that fills the air with self-righteousness. It gets a soft-edged production from William Gaskill; simple sentences (one actress's role seems to consist almost entirely of slogans) are not helped much by having emotion pumped into them, or by hopeful attempts to make them sound conversational. Philip Davis and, off and on, Elizabeth Estensen do best among the actors.

* * *

There is a considerably more bracing look at youth in Welcome Home Jacko (Riverside Studios), another good one from Mustapha Matura. Rastafarianism is helpfully expounded and caustically but understandingly pulled to bits. The writing and some of the performances (notably Trevor Laird's) are fine advertisements for the Black Theatre Co-operative. Mr Matura has written a taut and explosive final sequence, but here - on the smaller of the Riverside stages, which is still pretty big - it huddles in a corner.
