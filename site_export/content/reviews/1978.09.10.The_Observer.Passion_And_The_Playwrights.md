Passion and the playwrights

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'The Changeling' and 'The Philanderer'

THE playwrights of three eras last week redelivered their views on sex: views in the main deeply discouraging.

For Middleton and Rowley in The Changeling (1652), passion is an aberration born of ugliness and/or self-absorption and issuing in murder; best symbolised by the caperings of madmen. For Shaw in The Philanderer (1893) it is a character deformation bred especially in women by the false values of society and opposable only by a planned heartlessness almost equally indefensible. Only Aphra Behn in The Rover (1677) suggests that it might be the basis of an enjoyable relationship.

The Changeling (Riverside Studios) has not been seen in London for 16 years and its return is a joy and a relief. There is no verse played outside Shakespeare possessed of such natural resonance; none in which a man can declare "Can you weep fate from its determin'd purpose? So soon may you weep me" without making you feel, as Webster would, or even Jonson, that he knows he is rising to a great moment. The Changeling is in fact a play of unusual plausibility, if you can swallow (as the heroine has to) the odd, dubiously scientific virginity-test.

Beatrice, daughter of the governor of Valencia, falls in love with a handsome stranger, just after becoming engaged to someone else. To rid herself of her superfluous fiance she hires a villainously plain (and in her eyes plainly villainous) servant De Flores, whom she has hitherto humiliated. He cannot believe his luck; he has always, and hopelessly, wanted her -- now he can have her. The scene in which he claims her, making plain what she has done and where it has placed her (she being stronger on maidenly indignation than moral imagination), is an unmatched duet, an explosive series of revelations. Only the central scenes of Measure for Measure compare with it.

Criticism of the play has tended to take Beatrice at her own valuation: an innocent ruined by a tempter. (How dare De Flores poke his ugly mug round the door, just as she happens to be contemplating murder?) Performance upsets this; you could make out a good case for De Flores being the victim (a dab hand at murder, admittedly, but there is no suggestion that he does it very often) whom she corrupts. At any rate, he gains the theatrical sympathy that always goes to a realist; he knows what he is doing whereas Beatrice maintains a self-centred ignorance. Treating others as objects she keeps talking, as he contemptuously points out, of her "honour." It is this counterpoint that keeps the play darting about on its feet until the end.

The play's other surprise is its sub-plot, which acts far better than it reads. It is set in a mad-house, where gallants disguised as patients lay siege to the keeper's wife, who knows, unlike Beatrice, how to get rid of them. In Peter Gill's production these scenes are kept not only lively (special credit to David Troughton as a cynical clown who has his own stab at defloration) but relevant: at one point the two leading ladies pass each other as like ships in the night as you could ever hope to see on dry land.

Mr Gill's direction, on a wide, grim, flagstoned stage, has exemplary clarity and considerable emotional force. It lacks however in wit; it moves too deliberately both for the double-entendres which mine the text and for the giddy capriciousness of Beatrice that springs the action. She is an actress's problem -- a shallow character who suffers -- and Emma Piper offers a sedate though accurate sketch rather than a full portrait.

Brian Cox's De Flores emerges more likeably than even I would have expected: a strong, wracking performance if limited in its choice of notes. (A word in memory of the late Robert Shaw who gave his best performance in the role at the Royal Court in 1961 -- in a production that actually smacked far less of the Court than this one does.)

Julia Craven in The Philanderer might be Beatrice's lineal descendant; both ladies have indulgent fathers and latch on furiously to the men of their choice. Julia's tactics stop short of murder, though she speaks of it; her method is to throw tantrums and make scenes. Often they work, though a rival responds to them with a roof-raising line: "Do you suppose I am a man, to be imposed on by this sort of rubbish?"

At the Lyttelton, Penelope Wilton plays Julia in accordance with that response: as a calculating neurotic, keenly and hilariously observed. Christopher Morahan's production, the National's best this year, realises the play admirably down to its last stage direction, which happens to be "the rest look at Julia with concern, and even a little awe, feeling for the first time the presence of a keen sorrow." It is arguable that we should have felt it earlier, and that our laughter should have been edged with discomfort, but that is a small point. (Or rather, it's a large one, but it doesn't matter.)

The philanderer himself is another casualty of the sex-war: more engaging (like De Flores), since he has both wit and common sense, but studiously cold. He is a sexless satyr, and if such a thing cannot exist in nature it can in Shaw. Dinsdale Landen (bearded and sandalled, but not Irish or, as far as I could discern, vegetarian or teetotal) plays him with consummate airiness. Basil Henson again demonstrates his command of the military virtues, Barbara Flynn is a taking younger sister (an apprentice New Woman) and John Standing is the embodiment of stuffy shyness as an amorous doctor, discoverer of "Paramore's Disease" and, by the grace of a pun, one of its prime victims.

Shaw thought paramour's disease curable, though the continuing topicality of this, his second play, suggests he may have been mistaken. Middleton and Rowley thought it might be halted, at least in sensible people who spoke prose in sub-plots. The best Restoration playwrights took it for granted, and welcomed it.

* * *

Where, these past years, has Aphra been? I once recommended The Rover to a theatre that was mounting a season of plays by women; if such a category exists, this is its prime exhibit. The best thing about it, though, is that you don't stop to consider the sex of its author; its sympathies are boundless, and its humour unsentimentally acute, despite a plethora of moonlight duels. Its rover is a practising philanderer who ends up with a girl designed to be a nun; there can have been few such lovers in the history of vocational guidance. It is given a lacklustre performance at the Mercury, Colchester; it, and its fortunes, should revive.

The Salisbury Playhouse looks almost identical to the Mercury, but its stage is currently more expertly occupied. Two things happen in Hardy's Under the Green-wood Tree as rendered for the stage by Patrick Garland: a young man wins the hand of the local schoolteacher, and the church string band, mainly composed of his relatives, are ejected from their place in favour of one of them newfangled harmoniums. Their story, and particularly their music, takes precedence; once you have got the hang of it, the method is very agreeable.

Kevin Moore gives a charming account of the vicar who loses the girl but gets the organ, and Frank Shelley as the oldest inhabitant acts, sings and plays his instrument with equal authority.
