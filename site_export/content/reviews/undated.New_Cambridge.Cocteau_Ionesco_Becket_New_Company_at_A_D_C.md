Cocteau, Ionesco, Becket

New Company at A.D.C.

By Robert Cushman

[Editorial note: The heading retains the clipping's spelling "Becket". The writer discussed is Samuel Beckett. The clipping has no visible publication date.]

Independent Theatre's inaugural triple bill, directed in undisclosed proportions by Philip Mansell and Robert Leach, is dominated by a glittering joke at the expense of Ionesco's 'The Chairs'.

No make-up and no senile voices are employed for Ionesco's nonagenarian couple. Instead, a pair of clever young actors play out an impressionistic vaudeville of old age: an old age of a piece with the invisible guests who occupy the chairs, and the great speech which reduces, in the Orator's mouth, to a few strangled grunts.

Some new laughs are guaranteed. Whenever the script calls attention to the textual age of the couple, we are rocked in our seats by the obvious disparity between what the actors say and what they look. This is pretty cheap; but the faster tempo allowed by the absence of the usual trappings enables large sections of the play to emerge as sparkling line-comedy.

**Serious Reaction**

Your reaction will probably depend on how seriously you regard Ionesco: if you take the current in-view of him as a tiresome fantasticator with a redeeming gift of surrealist humour, you will find this production refreshing. Certainly it is miles away from the turgid self-consciousness of 'Exit the King'. But I still feel that 'The Chairs' has more to offer. There is an intensity of desiccation which can fuse with the comedy to provide a deeply unsettling experience.

The big surprise of this production is that it does occasionally realise this fusion. The Old Man's relapses into baby-talk are more easily accessible, though ultimately less disturbing, when they do not have to break through layers of vocal senility; the final suicides, with their obeisances to an invisible Emperor, hit as hard as ever. There are even moments when the production comes close to imposing a new dimension on the play: a sense of youth and old age as one, caught up in the same futility, which intensifies the author's pessimism.

Anyway, go and see it. Robert Leach, rubber-faced and multi-voiced, mugs his way resourcefully through the play, and Sue Bendall, playing with less precision, partners him loyally.

**Less interesting**

The rest of the programme is less interesting. In a production of 'Krapp's Last Tape' which lays on its atmospherics with a thick trowel, Robert Leach gives an expert performance, which stops infuriatingly short of real excitement. His silent presence is more compelling than his voice, which makes the tape itself rather heavy going.

In Cocteau's 'The Human Voice' Sue Bendall's appealing and hard-working performance is defeated by the banality of this clumsily-translated show-piece.
