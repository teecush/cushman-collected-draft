Visitors from Russia

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

THE Malaya Bronnaya Company from Moscow have brought to the Lyceum a brace of programme-note productions. Our own directors no longer tell us so earnestly what they are trying to do, though one or two of them would probably like to.

Anatoli Efros presents a determined face to the plays he directs: to Gogol's The Marriage (not, he says disarmingly, "a precious find for the theatre of our time") and Turgenev's A Month in the Country ("a good play, delicately . . . written. But what is good writing and what is delicacy?").

We are likely to find the Turgenev more enjoyable because more familiar; there is no fun in seeing a classic desecrated if you don't know it in the first place. The Marriage should, perhaps, be trimmed and presented as half a double bill. Efros, though, has elongated it through the introduction of dumb-shows.

The play has a soliloquising heroine (it also has a soliloquising hero with a soliloquising best friend) and whatever she imagines, we see. She compares the merits of her four suitors and they appear: a typical Gogolian rout of snobs, hangers-on and minor civil servants, lined up patiently on a bench like long-delayed commuters. When she has made her choice she thinks of the future and of children; a dozen of them promptly appear. Her intended must have had similar premonitions, for he bolts. Gogol has him jump out of a window. Efros lets us see him land, and scramble into a waiting landau.

In this line of experimentation the Russians lag far behind the Germans, the French and even the British. Efros's actual staging is clumsy; his scenic ideas waylay his actors. But he has the priceless director's gift of making his performers think, and he has an excellent company to work on.

Olga Yakovleva, though capable of considerable physical exertion (a graceful backward bound and pirouette across the stage when proposed to), makes the disappointed bride a figure of great poignancy: far more than I can find in the text. Nor can I see the point of ending the play with a menacing wedding cortege, symbolising precisely the plight that the characters have escaped. Disdaining the trappings of farce, Efros has substituted those of melodrama; his production is hard-hitting but soft-centred.

His Month in the Country also has some violent externals; it takes place mostly on a wrought-iron semi-circular garden seat mounted on a revolve and set regularly spinning. At the end (comes, I suppose, the Revolution) silent, sinister functionaries close in, dismantle the set and even rob the self-pitying chatelaine Natalie of the child's kite she has been cradling (Yakovleva has just treated us to another virtuoso athletic display, this time denoting grief). Clearly, the giddy whirl of the land-owning classes is about to come to an end.

While it lasts, however, Efros gives us a hard, clear look at it: not, however, a callous one. Natalia says to her lover Rakitin that his conversation is like lace-making, beautiful but arid, less attractive than a drink of cold water on a hot day. It is easy to get lost in Turgenev's tapestry; Efros pours on the cold water instead. Yakovleva's blonde Natalia is attractive, sympathetic, potentially passionate -- like a film star ageing into mother roles, sometimes with relish, more often with resentment. Her menfolk restore one's faith in Russian male acting (rather shaken by The Marriage). Crowning all is Leonid Bronevoy's doctor, whom you might label an eccentric were he not so robustly confident that he creates his own world around him. It is understandable that Efros should want to transcend "Russian realism" but the rest of the world cannot wish him too much luck since for us that label denotes something so satisfying and so all-inclusive; these actors confirm all our most favourable prejudices.

* * *

The official festival this year is either Russian or Shakespearean. The RSC have entries in both sections, stopping off at Daniel Stewart's and Melville College ("patrons asked not to smoke in the school gymnasium") with their fit-up version of Three Sisters and Twelfth Night, the latter a nicely framed production.

It begins with Viola gazing fascinatedly through a mirror at the Illyrians: Olivia on one of her devotional trips to her mother's grave, Sir Toby grumpily bringing up the rear. The link between clowns and romantics is established, to be severed at the end; as Feste sings his last song the lovers embrace while the outcasts -- just about everybody else -- are sent packing with broken heads, or hearts, or both. Magic.

What goes between is lively but not always especially coherent. Viola (Emily Richard) and Olivia (Suzanne Bertish) are bright but lightweight though what they really lack is practice, nine times out of ten the determining factor in acting Shakespeare. Apart from Edward Petheridge's subtly felt mocking Orsino, habit is here on the side of the comics, who include the best couple Belch and Aguecheek I have seen.

Roger Rees's Andrew is a blazered incompetent with a Hitlerian forelock, who knows he is being strung along but cannot help himself. A kind word will do wonders with him, and it is just his luck that Ian McKellen's Toby knows when to apply it. This Toby, except when dealing with his niece, has an answer for everyone, a splendid, habitual air of innocence and a lachrymose family pride ("am I not consanguineous"), flaring into real anger with the upstart Malvolio (Bob Peck), whose romantic scene ("this does make some obstruction in the blood, this cross-gartering") is an excellent mixture of lust and cramp. Add a sad but not suicidal Feste (Christopher Hancock) and a fine deadpan Fabian (Clyde Pollitt).

In the Assembly Hall Shakespeare also rules but not OK: not at least in The Tempest done in modern dress which, since the play has so little explicit action, could hardly matter less either way. I still don't know how Ariel should be costumed but I can now add streaky body stocking to the list of discarded alternatives. The fairies in A Midsummer Night's Dream are similarly got up, though Taylerson's Titania -- more becomingly. This, despite kebab house music, is altogether a far more agreeable production, rarely stirring the imagination but amusing and efficient at ground level.

The Traverse have their usual massive festival repertoire. I saw Robert Holman's Rooting presenting the human face of The Archers, passions boiling and subsiding and just going on down on the farm among real pigs, whose presence has a soothing, distancing effect on the human action.

There is an annexe, the Other Traverse, which looks and unfortunately sounds barnlike: hence missed subtleties of John Byrne's The Slab Boys though those around me laughed enough to persuade me that the author of Writer's Cramp had lost none of his wit in moving to a factory setting, drawn from his own youth. The acting of both Traverse plays is, at worst, immaculate.
