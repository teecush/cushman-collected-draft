The Seagull flies to Ireland

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Chekhov and Moliere revivals, a musical and two new plays in Bristol.

WHEN I first went to the Royal Court, in the Sixties, the fabled great days were already over and the decline, so volubly lamented by John Osborne, had already set in. Nostalgic self-pity was the prevailing mood, taking the stage in a long succession of plays by Mr Osborne or his contemporaries just occasionally broken by something more urgent: as when William Gaskill or Peter Gill took it into his head to direct a Restoration comedy.

For their twenty-fifth anniversary production the Court have chosen The Seagull: possibly a wry admission on the part of the incumbent director, Max Stafford-Clark, that this theatre's greatest strength (as well as, often, its financial salvation) has lain in its classic revivals. Mr Stafford-Clark has attempted to salve his theatre's conscience, or save its face, by commissioning a new version from Thomas Kilroy that re-sites the play in Ireland, but it is recognisably the old Chekhov that he is offering us in -- to make matters worse -- the best production of The Seagull that I have seen.

Mr Kilroy's argument is that Chekhov's rural Russia -- with its fecklessly absentee landowners, resentfully resident estate managers, and peasantry still confused by liberation -- is closely paralleled by the Irish countryside of the late nineteenth century. An historian might be able to demolish that, but it's good enough for the theatre.

Mr Kilroy throws specific political references around -- to Parnell or the Land League -- where Chekhov himself would be more circumspect; and the playwriting inspiration of Konstantin -- one of the few characters able to hang on to his original name in this version -- has changed considerably. Where once he looked outwards, to an international style stigmatised by his mother as 'decadent' or 'terribly modern,' he now turns to his own national legends. This time his mama -- the famous actress Isobel Desmond -- responds with 'Good Lord, it's one of those Celtic things,' and in so doing brings down the house.

It's a laugh of recognition, and the evening is full of them. I suppose they could be cheapening, but they aren't. The effect -- as with doing Shakespeare in modern dress -- is not to supply any blanket new interpretation; but is rather to sharpen individual characters and incidents. It prevents vagueness in the acting, and that is a terrific blessing.

So a minor character like the schoolmaster Medvedenko (I will assume from now on that you don't want to be confused by the new labels) becomes, in Tony Rohr's performance, more interesting than ever before; the details of his poverty, and of his long trek home when denied a horse by his father-in-law, are immediately comprehensible.

For us Russia is Russia; it's all a long way away, and we don't see why the characters should worry so much which bit of it they are in. But for a London actress to be cooped up, far away even from Dublin -- Arkadina's neurosis becomes, if not sympathetic, then certainly recognisable. Freed from any compulsion to act the charming chatelaine, Anna Massey is able to give full weight to the lady's cat-and-mouse games with her son, to her patronage of everyone about her, and to her fearful abasement before her lover.

Alan Rickman's Trigorin is similarly uncompromising; he faces the world in general, and women in particular, with a sickly defensive smile. It's a hedge against emotion but it also works -- literally -- like a charm. The analysis of his writer's disease is wonderfully lucid; Nina would have to be not only star-struck, but a bit deaf, to fall for him. This, in Harriet Walter's performance, she most believably is; she wags a lecturing finger at him when she speaks of being 'a great artist.' She lives up to her principles, sacrificing her life to his art; 'my life is yours -- take it if you wish' marks a strange junction of the two ideas. And at the end she cries real, jagged, disfiguring tears: like a person.

Anton Lesser's Konstantin is a tough misfit, instead of the usual foredoomed romantic; and there are fine choric performances from Stuart Burge and T. P. McKenna. Quite possibly Messrs Kilroy and Stafford-Clark believe that the play is about its social context; it isn't, but in establishing that context they have enabled Chekhov and the actors to reveal, more powerfully than ever before, what and whom it is about.

* * *

Moliere's Don Juan (Cottesloe), now, is more of an abstract piece, though it presents its ideas through a primal double-act. In Mr Gill's production this is masterfully handled, with Nigel Terry's Juan rattling airily through his libertine's creed, while Ron Pember as Sganarelle tries to put the case of conventional morality and progressively discredits it. (He also amusingly garbles his master's arguments for the benefit of strangers.) Both actors superbly serve, and are served by, John Fowles's translation, which is much given to hilarious undercutting of its own elegance.

So far, so marvellous. The supporting performances are unremarkable (unless you count one that is remarkably wooden) and I wasn't much taken by the silent, sullen chorus of possibly damned souls who throng the back of the stage; though I admit they turn out useful in the end: acting as mobile scenery for Juan's visit to the commander's tomb, and finally assisting in his descent to Hell.

Why does he go? The favoured current answer is that Moliere approves of his rebellion but damns him for his last respectable sin of hypocrisy. The trouble is that his hypocrisy is so plainly a joke, which we share. Moliere's people, however powerful, are flat. If he wanted to show us a new facet, he didn't dig; he simply turned the page. This is a very un-English way of doing things and Mr Gill deserves much credit for leaving the play, in his scrupulous and efficient production, as glitteringly opaque as he found it.

* * *

The Bristol Old Vic has been running a play-writing competition. The winner was Ian Sinclair with a Dostoevsky trilogy, now on view in the New Vic studio. I saw Part Two, The Governess, which tells, in bookish but moderately holding dialogue, of a Humbert Humbertish sort of guy who is a sight more interesting if you keep telling yourself that one day he's going to turn out some pretty great novels.

Meanwhile, and reasonably, the main stage is held by a runner-up, Brian Jeffries's Beside the Sea, which shows, on adjacent sections of beach, the families of an accountant and a garage owner, suspicious of each other and of their offspring. I kept wanting someone to do or say something independent of their author's scheme but even that turns out to be part of the effect. One longs for a rapprochement, and appreciates the author's craft in making the most sympathetic character responsible for its ultimate impossibility.

Under Anthony Cornish's direction the play is splendidly acted: Bill Wallis and Barbara Young v. Peter Copley and June Barrie -- a rattling game of doubles.
