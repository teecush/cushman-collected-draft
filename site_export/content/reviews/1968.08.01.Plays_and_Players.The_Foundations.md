The Foundations

There is something unmistakably village-hall about beginning a production with a pop record, and even more so about reprising it at the beginning of each act. But *The Foundations* is or was a pretty village-hall production anyway, and the record was appropriate on several counts. It was the Beatles' “All You Need is Love”, or at any rate its trick opening; “The Marseillaise”, creaky but recognisable, giving way to the noble sentiments of the title-phrase.

Galsworthy's play is a weird but quite likeable relic, written in 1917 as a jokey forecast of what life was going to be like after the Great War. There was going to be a slump, and something in the nature of an insurrection, here represented by an offstage crowd of dynamic content (demonstrations were obviously not what they are) who indeed sing “The Marseillaise” and are countered by noble philanthropists who believe Dickensianly in kindness. And in love. “There bain't nothin' in life but a bit of lovin'.” Love is all you need. With unthinking brilliance the Beatles have compressed the whole play into fifteen seconds, and for all I know the revival has been staged purely because the record was ready at hand. True, the programme mumbles something about the Galsworthy centenary—but that was last year, wasn't it?

The play moves in neat pincer-fashion (Act 1 The Rich, Act 2 The Poor, Act 3 They Meet), the catalytic agent being the Press, lanky, cheerful and acting someway below its best. It lumbers less than much Galsworthy, and in its portrait of the stumbling, well-meaning aristocrat (Emrys Jones, smiling through) achieves a pleasing ironic balance, a quality for which confused liberals are always good. I hope that irony also underlies the scenes depicting the humble poor, which in this production are a sentimental morass, presided over by a serene Devonshire seamstress (“I sews,” she remarks simply and often) whom Mary Merrall probably makes as little irritating as is humanly possible. The only relief hereabouts comes, improbably, from a brash child unforgivably christened Little Aida, whom a Corona kid called Julie Booth punches across with infectious relish. “One of these days,” someone says, “you'll make an actress”, and I shouldn't be surprised. The others, regardless of rank, hang helpless in the limp grip of the production, which is as diabolical as I've seen in a reputable theatre, if that is what the Arts is. One or two of them deserve it.

Galsworthy's diagnosis of the way in which revolutions are disarmed—sweet words without and sentimentality within—is substantially that of his spiritual descendant Arnold Wesker, only more blandly expressed. It is the bland paternalism that sticks in the throat now. We had the slump and we had the strike (in the reverse order to Galsworthy's, incidentally) and they did not much resemble this genteel charade. If there is a sense of danger in the text, this production has bled it white. I would like to find an excuse for labelling the proceedings *The Hindsyte Saga*, but I can't, so we'll let it ride.
