CAPTAIN Jack BOYLE picks up a book belonging to his daughter and reads off the contents with disgust: "A Doll's House? Ghosts? The Wild Duck." Kid's stuff.

The laugh this got on the first night of Juno and the Paycock (Aldwych) may have had something to do with an awareness, at least in the professional section of the house, that a production of The Wild Duck was opening that same night at the Lyric, Hammersmith, and would shortly have to be caught up with.

The two plays have much in common. Each centres on a windbag's family, kept more or less on the rails by a realistic self-sacrificing wife. Each ends with the death of a child, sacrificed to somebody else's notion of idealism. O'Casey's Johnny Boyle, caught up in the most vicious and ludicrous stage of the Irish struggle, is shot by former comrades. Ibsen's Hedvig Ekdal shoots herself because she believes her father hates her.

On the surface the O'Casey play is the darker. Johnny's death breaks up his home. His mother Juno, who also has a pregnant deserted daughter on her hands, walks out on Captain Jack for good. Hedvig's suicide at least has the effect of drawing her parents closer together. There is however Dr Relling, the play's raisonneur, on hand to assure us that Hjalmar Ekdal's fine words over his daughter's corpse are only fine words and will lead to no new attitude. Nobody dares to say that of Juno's final oration. It moves us and in moving us comforts us. The Wild Duck is bleakly ironic to its roots, and there is little in Juno to match it.

The trick of the comedy in Juno is to preserve the human reality: to prevent it from becoming a dance of symbols expressing a doubtless universal truth but totally unbelievable as dramatic action. Trevor Nunn and Michael Blakemore manage these complementary assignments superbly. From now on if anybody asks what I want from the theatre I propose to answer "intelligent showmanship" and to offer these two directors as exemplars.

Mr Nunn takes Juno very deliberately; the assumption is that every moment counts, and that there is juice to be extracted from every action. Sometimes it is comic juice; there is an extended sequence early in Act One when Norman Rodway's poem of the hippo is interrupted at almost every line by a succession of bizarre visitors. You observe the precise workings of his relationship with John Rogan's Joxer who watches him ceaselessly to observe how best to flatter and cave in. Rodway's Captain Jack is actually dangerous, and is funny to the exact extent that he is dangerous.

Mr Rodway could scarcely be more remote from one's image of a man of integrity; the startling core of his performance is his violent tirade when he learns of his daughter's condition. His drunkenness is hilarious and his selfishness horrifying; the comedy and horror go together. Judi Dench's Juno also makes the comedy work, both in her tart rebukes to her husband and when she suddenly is given the frightfully important job of reading the will in the second act. But underlying everything in her performance is an immense practicality; everybody's sweetheart is now everybody's mother. As for her last familiar lament, doubt that at your peril.

The welding process is extended in Gerard Murphy's Johnny who is kept in focus throughout, tormented by life inside and out of the tenement room, and maddened as much by the jollity as by the horror. Dearbhla Molloy as his sister undergoes something of the same progress, and her two suitors are sharply drawn by Bryan Murray and Frank Grimes. All the characters save Juno are romancers; Johnny's "a principle's a principle" is the reflection of his father's imaginary career at sea. O'Casey deplored both; Ibsen would probably have condoned the Captain's fancies as a life-lie on a par with Hjalmar Ekdal's imaginary invention.

The trouble with Ibsen's play is that its idealist Gregers Werle and his idol Hjalmar are an unlikely combination. It would take a great fool not to see through Hjalmar, so Gregers is a great fool. This coalition might come about, and might well produce disastrous results, but it seems rather a specialised base from which to proclaim universal truths. Mr Blakemore's production sets energetically about normalising the two of them. Jim Norton gives full weight, and some reason, to Gregers's hatred of his father; he becomes one of the walking wounded with his own kind of plausibility. Richard Briers's Hjalmar has a stocky self-satisfaction that just might, in a suitable light, be mistaken for sincerity. When he goes to pieces he does so with surprising fervour; his comedy and tragedy are of a piece and ingrown. They almost co-exist in time; Hjalmar too is a comic set-piece over his breakfast and Ibsen, after all, a bolder playwright, brings it right up to the moment of disaster.

The domestic sphere is maintained by Nerys Hughes's Gina, and above all by the Hedvig of Michele Wade, who is a plain, bespectacled and fanciful schoolgirl. Not only does she not sentimentalise the play, but she actively robs it of symbolism. When this Hedvig talks about the depths of the ocean you can hear her mind wrapping itself lovingly around the books she has read. Gerald James makes a welcome Dr Relling, and Frances Cuka is quite outstandingly good as the kind and worldly Mrs Sorby; the solid relationships she establishes (with Gina especially) in two brief appearances exemplify the strength of the evening.

* * *

David Mercer's The Limits of Love is, posthumously, at the Warehouse. It presents an economically comfortable menage a trois; valid, since it is smashed by a newcomer who turns out to have been one man's analyst. Mercer gives them tirades that are moving, revealing; take your choice, but he never seems to have the characters properly in his sights; they are good for what he can get out of them at any moment (rather like Neil Simon with his one-liners and ultimately no more informative).

The lady in the case comes off particularly badly; Susan Fleetwood spits and preaches, but she is not given a life. John Shrapnel adds electricity every time he appears, as a bouncing bisexual don, though the bisexuality is hard to credit and the bounce rather close to professional foul. Bob Peck as an outrageously (to everyone else) passive cellist has a tellingly individual line in stump; Edward Petherbridge alone makes his memories of unseen characters in the past seem pressingly important in the present.
