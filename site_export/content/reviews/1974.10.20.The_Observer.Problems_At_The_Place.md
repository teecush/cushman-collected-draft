Problems at the Place

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

IN the programme for the Royal Shakespeare Company's memorable Richard II at the Aldwych, the regular profile of the company has been reworded. Its gait is rather uncertain; it begins with a strut ('The Royal Shakespeare Company is probably the best known theatre company in the world') and ends ('For your support tonight the company is grateful') with a grovel. But it omits the customary roll-call of Associate Artists (an imposing line, most of whom can invariably be found acting somewhere else) and has also dropped the brazen declaration that 'the RSC occasionally give short experimental seasons in which they challenge accepted forms of acting, writing and directing.'

This is tactful, and suggests that the company may at last be dropping its dangerous habit of believing their own publicity. The RSC's initial two seasons at the Place in Euston challenged no accepted forms, but merely mishandled them. The first was disappointing and the second (particularly in choice of plays) disastrous. The third is now upon us, and two of its four productions have already opened.

First is Strindberg's Comrades, concerning marriage and taking -- even for Strindberg -- an extreme view of it. Axel is a saint and Bertha unspeakable. They are living the artistic life in Paris, and both have paintings up for acceptance by the Academy. Axel, well aware that he is the more talented of the two, and anxious to spare his wife the humiliation of rejection, switches the name-tags on the two pictures; so she gets in and he doesn't. She, however, has no wish to spare him any humiliation; she piles it on till eventually even his good nature is exhausted. He reveals the deception; she collapses, writhing; and he finds that he now has the strength to leave her.

Structurally, the play is a fine, tight melodrama buoyed up -- at any rate in Jeremy Brooks's snappy adaptation -- by a raciness rare in Strindberg. It is also, despite the rigidity of its attitudes, enticingly topical. Axel and Bertha are ostensibly trying to escape from traditional roles and turn their marriage into a companionship of equals; hence the title. Bertha and her friend Abel belong to a women's movement and ask awkward questions. Why shouldn't Bertha paint nude men if her husband can paint nude women?

A modern director perks up at moments like these; or so Barry Kyle has done at the Place. Strindberg, however, lets him down. As a capable dramatist he gives these revolutionary stirrings a momentary hint of conviction, but as an obsessed misogynist he regards them as works of the devil. (The brief glimpse we are given of Bertha's bunch in session does actually look uncommonly like a witches' coven.) Partnership in marriage is an unworkable fraud, a ruse by which the wife may undermine her husband and embezzle the housekeeping. Sex (and here Strindberg goes beyond ordinary male chauvinism to something more individual and unpleasant) is the worst tyranny of all. Axel really becomes free when Bertha offers him her body and he declines it. At last, he cackles, she will know the male agony of pursuit and refusal. An orthodox liberationist would probably say that Bertha, in using her sexuality as a weapon, has betrayed her own cause. Strindberg differs in regarding this betrayal as a biological inevitability rather than a lapse to be blamed on social conditioning. He refuses to believe in female solidarity; Abel, carrying her own bundle of neuroses, makes Bertha a most untrustworthy ally.

Susan Fleetwood and Rosemary McHale are a spirited pair of harpies, though Miss Fleetwood is inclined to play for sympathy where her author can only offer her a contemptuous pity. Peter Eyre's Axel is the production's undoing. Earlier this year in Ghosts Mr Eyre played another Scandinavian artist in Paris. That one had syphilis and this one doesn't look or sound much healthier. He droops and whines, so that his charity seems merely wet, and his final triumph incredible. I assume that Strindberg saw himself as Axel, and Mr Eyre's performance is probably a fair representation of what Strindberg was really like. But it is not what he thought he was like, however much a modern director may jib at such explicit sexism. Besides, the play already contains a frightening picture of a defeated male (a cringing author, unsparingly played by David Suchet) and it scarcely needs another.

Having fudged the play's central, central relationship, Mr Kyle, seemingly uncertain of how funny he wants the play to be, misjudges its tone and fumbles with its action; unwanted actors, left crouching over pieces of furniture, are apt to look exposed in so small an auditorium. And when a senior member of so distinguished a company is inaudible throughout a long scene, as was Clement McCallin (who admittedly had his back to me at the time), one wonders.

* * *

Having failed with a good play, the RSC has, after a fashion, succeeded with a bad one. Victor Lanoux's The Can Opener is a waste of everybody's time, notably that of its adaptor Charles Wood. But it is possible, while pondering that the existence of the only two men left alive after an unidentified holocaust would probably be as monotonous as M. Lanoux makes it seem, to admire the interplay of Roy Kinnear and Joe Melia. One may reflect that Mr Kinnear, despite his excesses (not too evident here), is still an actor, while Mr Melia, who seems to find his lines interesting but not convincing, is not; which does not mean that I would like to see him drummed out of Equity or even the RSC. It's a bleak life at the end of the world: just a couple of good jokes, any amount of whimsical philosophising, endless tins of processed peas, and Mr Kinnear has lost the opener.

* * *

There are good comedies that present almost intractable difficulties to a modern company. To make its audiences really understand The Tragedy of Tragedies or the Life and Death of Tom Thumb the Great, the Young Vic would first have to revive several of the forgotten eighteenth-century tragedies which Fielding, an energetic playwright before the imposition of censorship drove him to the novel, was here burlesquing: and I doubt if we could spare the time. Bernard Goss's production is hit or miss; it hits most often at the close when the narrative takes on its own momentum and heads hilariously for general massacre. The personages of the scene range from Thumb himself, a voluble conquering midget, to a stoical giantess; big girls don't cry. Spoiled princesses do, though, and Zoe Wanamaker, a languorous Huncamunca, acts accordingly and very well. She seems to have a better idea of what she is about than anyone else on stage, though Alfred Lynch and Christopher Timothy have between them a multitude of moments.

* * *

A sterner comedy, Schippel (Germany, pre-1914 vintage) by Carl Sternheim has reached the Open Space, adapted -- which is to say, softened, elongated and finally travestied -- by C. P. Taylor. On its own terms the version is amusing and we are to see more Sternheim soon, so it may not be fatal.
