Living in the present

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

NOW IT comes on the winter of 1979 and there is much snow on the ground, and also ice, and it is a miserable time for one and all, and many citizens and even theatres are compelled to do the best they can.

There is one theatre in East London called the Half Moon, which is formerly a synagogue in the days when there is a big call for such places in East London and what do they put on but a show which is entitled Guys and Dolls and which is well known to be a musical comedy whose plot is lifted from one of the short stories written by Damon Runyon. This Runyon writes many such tales back in the 1930s, and is such a guy, as hates and despises the use of the past tense. This tense is in fact employed more than somewhat in Guys and Dolls but I excuse it since the stage is well known to be a bastard art form, while the musical comedy stage has practically nothing legitimate about it whatsoever.

Personally I greet the arrival of this show with much enthusiasm for many citizens of my acquaintance regard it as the best musical they ever see, including many who never actually see it at all. The thing is, it is first staged in London in 1953 at an hour when many of my friends are tucked up in their beds, with their mammas maybe singing lullabies to them; and I myself do not see it at this date though afterwards I play the record maybe a hundred times, and also take two or three ganders at the movie. One way and another I feel pretty well acquainted with Guys and Dolls and I wish to say I never expect to see it performed in an ex-synagogue by a cast of seven. But the Half Moon is what is called an art theatre, with few potatoes to spare, and its patrons are very tolerant.

A guy named Shakespeare once says to make a virtue of necessity or some such; and Robert Walker, who directs this item, is very virtuous, in fact he is practically a saint. Many characters in this show are gamblers, and one or two are no better than common criminals, and it will be a dreadful thing if any honest guys or dolls in the audience admire them or find them romantic. But Mr Walker sets them all in a barroom which maybe once sees better days, though personally I will not like to bet on it; and he brings them all on very slow and sour, and sends them all off even slower and sourer, and one way and another he seems to think the show is what they call a black comedy though this is by no means the case.

Furthermore, he cuts out some of the best jokes from Abe Burrows's dialogue, though the book is still the funniest ever written, for Broadway at that. But Mr Walker knows what he is doing and is bigger than me, or at least broader, so I do not pursue it.

At first he seems to have 10 actors, only three of them turn out to be nothing but musicians; though one of this trio is liable to leave his piano and stand on a box to impersonate Big Jule who comes from Chicago and who many inhabitants of Manhattan wish will return there on account of his hasty temper. Also visiting town is Sky Masterson, the renowned sportsman, who falls in love with a Salvation Army doll, though neither of them, according to the song, is ever in love before; Barrie Houghton, who plays Sky, is as rat-faced a juvenile lead as ever I wish to see, but of course this is part of the intention, and he is slick and oily and also sings pretty good.

Maurice Colbourne, who shrugs in a very eloquent manner, plays Nathan Detroit, who runs a floating crap-game and on occasion that attracts much opprobrium from the coppers, and also from Miss Adelaide, who is Nathan's fiancee of 14 years' standing, though to hear her tell it standing is not her usual position. Adelaide gets to sing about her plight as a female who is "getting a kind of a name for herself and the name ain't his," which is maybe the most comical line any lyricist ever writes.

Adelaide is played by Maggie Steed, a long lady who honks very high-toned and has plenty of attack and makes me laugh ha-ha practically every time she opens her mouth. There is also a guy named Robin Hooper who plays seven roles, male and female, which means he is frequently on stage with himself and has to keep changing his hat. He sings a quiet ditty called "More I Cannot Wish You," which is such a touching ballad as you would not expect Frank Loesser to write, he being famous for such comedy numbers as "Take Back Your Mink," performed by Adelaide and the Hot Box Girls, only three, and "Sit Down, You're Rockin' the Boat." But such chorus numbers as "Luck Be a Lady" and "Sit Down, You're Rockin' the Boat" are done with great ingenuity, and also glee; and it is of course a great testimony to Guys and Dolls that it can lose so much weight and still live.

* * *

A month or so ago I am sitting in a restaurant on 72nd Street with David Edgar, the playwright, talking of his piece Mary Barnes which opens in Birmingham last year and is to transfer to the Royal Court where it arrives last week. "You are," he says to me, "the only critic who notices that the play is not only about alternative methods of psychotherapy but about the counter-culture generally. But," he says, "I see that you say this subject is skimpily treated and I rewrite the play accordingly."

I will not of course mention such a conversation, being a modest guy at all times, if this aspect does not strike me as being far more effectively handled this time round. But I am puzzled, for I do not notice any extra scenes or speeches; in fact I suspect Mr Edgar trims his play, reducing the social and historical background to scale and stepping up the ironies. Hugo, the Supershrink, who many spectators believe to be none other than R. D. Laing, is played by Donald Sumpter, who is teasing and testing and also cool. Simon Callow meanwhile is kind and furious as Eddie who guides and coaxes Mary Barnes through her ascent from schizophrenia; and Patti Love's Mary is so complete that I get a shock when she takes her curtain-call since by then I honestly forget she is an actress.

Up above Mary Barnes, in the Theatre Upstairs, there is Ron Hutchinson's Anchorman, which also comes from what is called the Midlands, namely from Coventry. Now Mary Barnes is such a play as turns pain into pleasure by illuminating it, but Anchorman keeps everything dark, including the audience. Four British soldiers, who will be considered very rough customers even in the army, are present at the end of the world, or at least of this society, though of course not being dramatic critics they have little experience of such cataclysms, and panic accordingly.

The production is on the dingy side, and is such an event as a scribe like Ambrose Hammer, who dearly loves to heave the old harpoon into actors, will enjoy reviewing; but I am kind-hearted and wish no part of such action, so I shut up.
