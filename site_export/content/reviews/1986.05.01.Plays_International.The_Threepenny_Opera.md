Anyway Weill knew what he was about right through this work; I doubt if Brecht did. Hence his heavy rationalisations afterwards. One trouble with writing a satirical version of The Beggar's Opera is that Gay had written one already. It may be difficult to get a modern audience to see through his highwaymen to the crooked politicians beneath, but there are at least a few explicit lines to make it easier, and there is a lightness of touch, an uncaringness in Gay, that Brecht found hard to emulate. People who acclaim his irony always quote lines like what is the crime of robbing a bank compared to the crime of founding a bank' which isn't ironic at all, just heavy.

There isn't much Brecht can do to Gay's purposefully preposterous ending but underline and expand it; though here at least he has Weill's assistance. Where The Threepenny Opera gains over The Beggar' Opera it is because Weill is a more resourceful composer than Trad. The plot-line of both plays is confused and perfunctory; the coup-de-grace always comes with the last scene between Polly and Lucy: a new development when we would all much rather go home. Scholars of the original work and its offshoots have no cause to complain of the recent programming at the National: Gay and Brecht/ Weill and A Chorus of Disapproval and some would even include Guys and Dolls. (Anyone on for Beggars' Holiday by Duke Ellington and John Latouche?

No, I thought not.) It is clearly an area of work that sets people fantasising. In The Neophiliacs Christopher Booker coined the word 'nyktomorph' to describe the fantasies of violent artistic gratification common in the fifties and sixties. The image of satirical cabaret, as practised in Weimar Berlin, was one he singled out, and certainly one that was much touted by Ken Tynan: something to please the righteous, something in which to rub the noses of the bourgeoisie. Low-life musicals were a new and appealingly subversive form in the 50s; to Mr Shulman (a firebrand critic in his time) The Threeperny Opera must have seemed a successor to Pal Joey. It must at least have seemed a welcome antidote to Salad Days.

And the nyktomorph persists. Critics still expect The Threepenny Opera, a 60-year-old musical, to do something to them. Or at least to do something to someone else, while they have a bit of a gloat. At times, too, Peter Wood's Olivier production seems to be trying. 'What Keeps a Man Alive', the big savage number, is given the works, all downtrodden actors with shaking fists. It is at best dutiful, at worst embarrassing. And you may blame Mr Wood, and I do too, but I also blame Brecht.

Because what the text offers us is undigested moralising anger. It is a song about hunger coming out of a story in which nobody has starved. (Peachum's beggars are fakes.) I don't know whether Brecht had the talent for satire; he certainly had not the temperament. When he wants to make a point he makes it, directly. He tells us what to think. Mr Wood's production is another victim (after Saint Joan, The Trojan War Will Not Take Place, Mandragola and other small, interesting plays) of the Olivier stage. It is probably possible to make the show work in so large a space — one hears enticing tales of an expressionist production at New York's Vivian Beaumont, a white elephant of white elephants — but it has not been achieved here.

The thinking, I imagine, was that the Olivier needed to be filled with another hit musical, and here was one with cultural credentials. But it would have had to be made over into another animal, and this has not happened. Mr Wood's production is actually pretty faithful, with no obvious distortions or omissions. (I missed the line about the banks but that was probably my inattentiveness rather than the result of Citicorp sponsorship.) There is for example no softening of Macheath in Tim Curry's performance. Would that there had been. Mr Curry is an actor who has come a long way on charm — I mean that as a compliment - and when the surroundings were perverse, as in The Rocky Horror Show, the charm was all the greater.

He tries to play Mackie nasty and heartless as required, and doesn't have it in him; so nothing comes over. But with this company, to judge from this show and Love for Love, he has been forgetful or unlucky or both. Only two actors have been delivering at full throttle. One is Michael Bryant, who after jigging and jogging through an opening funeral cortege to eye-catching scene-stealing effect, proceeds to establish a punch-drunk, cauliflowereared Crookfingered Take. (That was the role the Bob Peck character was first offered in A Chorus of Disapproval; Mr Bryant suggests that he might have been a happier man had he stuck with it.) The other is Sara Kestelman, whose Mrs Peachum is unfussed and acidulously phrased in speech and song. Stephen Moore as her spouse shouts his laugh-lines which with him is always a sign of desperation; still they do make, with Sally Dexter's Polly, a credibly shabby-genteel menage, and Miss Dexter, who has a lot of singing to do, does it well.

Eve Adam's Jenny in her red dress looks like Georgia Brown in Oliver! and has the twang if not the dynamism. Niall Buggy's fruity Tiger Brown may be inexcusable but I enjoyed it. Barry James probably sings the Moritat very well (I'd certainly expect him to) but the funny funeral cancels him out. The other praised visual set-piece, Macheath's flight over the rooftops, merely holds up the action, and seems borrowed from Mr Wood's production of The Provok'd Wife. Robert David Macdonald's translation is lively, and seems to have inherited whole phrases from Eric Bentley and Marc Blitzstein. Either coincidence or Mr Macdonald has done wisely here for Blitzstein's versions, the abomination of professional Brechtians, are the only singable ones anybody has come up with.

All they need is to be de-bowdlerised, and that task has been thoroughly accomplished here.
