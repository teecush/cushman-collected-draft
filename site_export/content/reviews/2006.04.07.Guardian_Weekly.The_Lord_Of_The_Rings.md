The Lord of the Rings

Theatre

Princess of Wales, Toronto

The parade of images in Matthew Warchus's production of The Lord of the Rings is ceaseless and astonishing. The Black Riders are truly terrifying, especially the first one who appears out of nowhere as a spectral compound of man and horse. The senior Orcs travel on great curved snowshoes; other ranks push themselves into battle on sinister crutches. The Ents, on stilts and held up by wires, do a superb job of looking like trees. The stage itself is a spectacle, constantly moving up, down and around, splitting apart and coming together again. It even takes a bow at the end and nobody could begrudge it.

This show, reputedly the most expensive yet staged, has spent its millions wisely. Practically too, as devices work strictly to serve the story. Rob Howell's set, superlatively lit by Paul Pyant, is an arrangement of branches that makes an environmental statement as well as a narrative one; and unlike the film it includes the last episode, in which hobbits chase the polluters from the Shire.

The obvious problem remains: how, in one three-and-a-half-hour show, do you adapt a book that barely fitted into three long films? Warchus and his co-adaptor Shaun McKenna have done it cleverly, cutting out great patches of plot and sensitively binding the remains together. This show is intelligent, but it isn't satisfying. The sheer proliferating bulk of JRR Tolkien's trilogy is a major part of its attraction. Reduced, however cunningly, it loses weight and even urgency.

Most of the score, by AR Rahman and the Finnish group Varttina, is serviceable, underlining rather than advancing or creating the plot.

The British actors James Loye and Peter Howe play Frodo and Sam admirably. Most of the rest are Canadian. Gollum is superbly played by Michael Therriault, who a few years ago was a fine Ariel; now he's a Caliban, malevolent and tormented. Evan Buliung's Strider, glamorous even in tatters, is that rare commodity, a genuine hero. The others make less of a mark or have less of a chance.

In this show the human element fights hard and sometimes wins, but the mechanical element, which is after all a human invention, is wonderful. Robert Cushman
