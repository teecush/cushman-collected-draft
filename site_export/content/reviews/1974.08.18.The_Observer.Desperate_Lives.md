Desperate lives

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on "Stallerhof," "Bingo" and the Beatles

The event of the week should have been the arrival in London of Edward Bond's Bingo (Royal Court), first seen in Exeter last year, but it has been overshadowed for me by a German play, Stallerhof, which has been at the Bush, and reopens this week at the Pool in Edinburgh, as a lunchtime offering on the Festival fringe.

It is the work of a young writer, Franz Xaver Kroetz, and it recalls Mr Bond's Saved in the spareness, frequently contracting into silence, of its dialogue and the brute (which is not to say brutal) realism of its action; we are shown characters living at the extremest edge of social and emotional deprivation. An ageing farmworker couples with his employer's spastic daughter, and gets her pregnant. Her parents' reaction is disgust: but then that has been their reaction to her throughout her life. When her mother describes her as "backward," the word is at worst an accusation; at best, this being a religious family, the acceptance of a cross.

The dramatist's satiric emphasis on Bavarian Catholicism is perhaps the play's weakest point; it leads him to conventionalise the presentation of the parents. He has little to say about farmers other than that he is a tyrant embittered by puritanism and poverty. As facts these are not disputable, but there is no surprise in them, and the man behaves strictly according to plan when he sourly dismisses the employee who has made him a grandfather.

His wife fares more interestingly. Declaring her intention to wash her daughter's filthiness away the same way it went in, she prepares, with a hosepipe and a pail of soapy water, to administer an abortion. It seems that she will go through with it, and certainly in a play which has already presented us, graphically but quite calmly and unsensationally, with defecation, masturbation and intercourse, there seems no aesthetic reason why she should not. But she draws back, respecting the girl's humanity, for perhaps the first time in her life.

Starkness is not in itself a guarantee of quality; to strip a scene of obvious emotion can be as short a cut to impressing an audience as striking up "Hearts and Flowers." Occasionally I suspected that David Mouchtar-Samurai's extraordinarily measured production was attempting to hypnotise us into admiration. But behind the evenness lies an infinity of concentration, most of all in the scenes between Celia Quicke as the retarded Beppi and Bill Stewart as her seducer. Is it in fact seduction? Is it rape? Maybe it qualifies as love. The man proceeds on the apparent assumption that the girl will not understand what is happening to her; and initially perhaps that is the truth. There is certainly little physical pleasure for her; but if part of her is an automaton, dropping her knickers whenever her partner approaches, a fragment is an awakened human being. Miss Quicke's performance, filtering emotional half-tones through incoherent murmurs and earnest bespectacled blinks, is uncanny. She is used, abused and yet, at the core, untouchable. She put me in mind of the first great tragedy of the inarticulate (and, more than incidentally, another German work): Buchner's Woyzeck. Both, as it happens, contain crucial scenes in fairgrounds, a potent image of mechanical gaiety offsetting desperate lives.

There is a similar feeling to two speeches in Mr Bond's Bingo. A crazed old man shudders in memory through the merrymaking associated with a public hanging. Another man - who unfortunately for his own peace of mind is not crazed - recalls the ingenious horrors of bear-baiting. This second man is Shakespeare; and however marginal biography may have been to Mr Bond's conscious concerns, the play stands as the most convincing picture of a genius - certainly of this genius - achieved by a contemporary dramatist.

One reason for this is that he shows us genius, but not creativity. This is Shakespeare in retirement, the central figure, ironically enough, of another tragedy of inarticulacy. The irony is compounded by the casting of John Gielgud, the most eloquent of actors, who here achieves his finest effects in silence, as he tries to distance himself from the acquisitive cruelty which he once denounced from behind the mad mask of King Lear (the play depends a good deal on our awareness of this literary fact), and in which, as a Stratford burgher, he is now acquiescing. Allegorically this is a most moving performance, for who in our time has been closer to Shakespeare than Gielgud? Naturalistically, it fails to convince; it is Shakespeare taken at his own valuation - as a man superior to avarice - rather than at Mr Bond's. It is a static performance, as melancholic at the beginning as at the end, and it thereby underlines the main flaw in the play, an impressive still life rather than a developing drama.

Jane Howell's production, still excessively low-keyed, has gained some good performances since its premiere in Exeter; most notably there is Arthur Lowe's Ben Jonson. Mr Lowe, with his uniquely inconsequential timing, could get a laugh on almost any line; the house expects it of him and he obliges, then stills the tumult with the declaration: "I hate. A short, hard word. Begins with a hiss and ends with a spit."

The London theatre is getting very chummy with its songwriters. The Mermaid has "Cole." Now at the Lyric are John, Paul, George, Ringo and Bert, who stayed behind in Liverpool, a happily dedicated fan, while the fab four were making it all over the world, and was finally disillusioned by their disillusion. All that money and success and London (Bingo). What, one wonders, will happen to the creators of this show, which also comes to us from Liverpool? To be honest, Bert might well have been left on Merseyside a second time; he must have meant more there. George Costigan, who plays him, gets on excellent terms with the audience, but his story interrupts that of the Beatles. Or vice versa.
