A case of Nuts

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Glasgow.

I CAN'T think of much to say about Nuts (Whitehall). It is an American court-room drama, obeying most of the American court-room dramatic rules, though with a few twists. The setting is the Bellevue mental hospital. A call girl has been accused of the manslaughter of one of her clients; everybody except she and her attorney is trying to prove that she is insane.

A psychiatrist, as is customary (cf. The Caine Mutiny novel, play or film), has his testimony rubbished by counsel for the defence, who is young, aggressive and Jewish. (Only bland Gentile lawyers are ever allowed to appear for the State. This looks like discrimination and somebody should investigate it.) Usually in such circumstances defence wants to prove its client mad; here of course it's the opposite.

Later the heroine's stepfather is forced to admit that he molested her somewhat in her youth. This helps to explain why she is disturbed, though I thought we were trying to prove that she wasn't. Anyway, the matter--and the feminist point--once established, both are dropped.

She herself takes the stand in Act II and demonstrates that, lucid or not, she is one hell of an actress. She has given indications of this earlier on with well-timed dismissive interjections about everyone present. Anne Twomey takes full advantage. I did not believe in her--she is too like the sardonic sufferer of a dozen social-conscience movies--but I enjoyed her.

On its own terms, Tom Topor's play succeeds. Its jokes made me laugh. The tension, when the judge decides whether or not the lady is fit to stand criminal trial, is effective. But the form is restrictive, and the content largely unexplored.

* * *

The Young Vic is engaged in a season of plays about Venice. They have omitted Volpone (and, come to that, The Gondoliers), but the two obvious Shakespeares are there, and there has been a brave, and surprisingly successful, resurrection of Byron's Marino Faliero. Promised for last is Otway's Venice Preserv'd and I look forward to it, hoping it will atone for the current production at the Almeida.

Otway's play, like Byron's, concerns a plot against a corrupt Senate, undertaken by conspirators whose noble motives are tarnished by social or sexual pique. Otway has far less sense of place than Byron, for whom the glamorous, already decaying, Venice outweighed everything else. But he sees far more incisively into character and has the advantage, writing in 1682, of a blank-verse tradition not yet demoralised by Shakespearean blackmail. Byron's, like all the plays of the Romantics, is a book of quotations. Otway's protagonists are heavily indebted to Brutus and Cassius, but his language is darkly and unselfconsciously his own.

At the Almeida most of it is moaned or mumbled. Plainly Tim Albery intended a neurotic, nihilistic, battened-down production; the set suggests a ruined ballroom with clouded mirrors on the wall, chandeliers on the floor and pillars that come in for a lot of heavy petting from the actors. Costumes are early twentieth-century; the general atmosphere is rise-of-Mussolini.

Two actors alternate the star turns of Jaffeir and Pierre at successive performances; two actresses deal similarly with the leading ladies, and also share the subsidiary male roles. There are, as you might expect, some suggestive quick changes, as when Helen Cooper (Wednesday's casting) switches from Antonio, the masochistic senator, to Belvidera, who also enjoys suffering. But there should be more to her than that; more even to Antonio. He pays to be ill-treated; but he can pay, so he can threaten as well. As written, the scenes are scurrilously funny; here they are monochrome.

If Mr Albery had cut the play down to an hour, his treatment might work. To give the full text with so little variety is to induce screaming boredom. Andrew Wilde's Jaffeir is a sickly public schoolboy, Jonathan Moore's Pierre a tone-deaf lout. Johanna Kirby gets some flavour into the courtesan Aquilina.

Jaffeir threatens his wife with a dagger in one hand, and the other up her skirt; but even this blend of cruelty and eroticism has no shock value in a production that is down on the characters from the start. An interpretation for our time, perhaps, but even our time doesn't have to be that ungenerous.

* * *

There are so many hints here of the Glasgow Citizens' style that I flew to the fountainhead with foreboding. Goethe's Torquato Tasso was the play; the tribute of one (by us) neglected poet to another even more so. The decor is again 1920s: 150 years after Goethe, 350 after Tasso.

We start with two ladies taking champagne on top of a giant haystack; beneath them is a flock of plainly artificial sheep. A flunky presses a switch and a loudspeaker emits equally artificial bleats. Later the rural crops get swathed in some of the Citizens' regulation dust-sheets. Michael Levine's set, richly and satirically decorative, is well up to Glasgow scratch.

As usual, the initial effect is relentlessly jokey; Robert David Macdonald's translation is fuller than ever of tags from other writers. As usual, the jokeyness gradually becomes transparent, a way of getting us into the play.

The subject is the tragic vanity of princes and poets. Tasso presents his masterpiece to his patron, the Prince of Ferrara; he senses slights everywhere and is also hopelessly in love with the prince's sister. If the hero recalls Moliere's Misanthrope, the technique--suffering endured with decorum, exacerbated by it, and finally shattering it--recalls Racine. But the play is more powerful than any Racine I have seen. Mr Macdonald gives the play an English form that is probably inexact, but allows you to apprehend the original, without ever denying its tantalising remoteness. Self-consciousness is the name of the game, but it is also what the play is about.

As director, he gets some of the best acting I have seen at this theatre: Dikran Tulaine, brittle and explosive as Tasso; Peter Rumney, stiff and benignly blinkered as the Prince; Ciaran Hinds, smooth and unswerving as the politician who is the poet's chosen enemy. The women, as generally happens here, are closer to caricature, but vigorous: Kate Ingram a suffering over-bred princess; Jill Spurrier a jealous go-between.

The original Tasso wrote accomplished pastorals, and Goethe here reactivated the genre. This production reminds us what a powerful and attractive form it can be.

The Bouncing Czecks (King's Head) remind us of the enduring comicality of some oldish songs, and some older jokes. They also do some funny original numbers. Their assumed personae--an overweening act at a dreadful Hawaiian holiday resort--break down into Sharon Campbell, pretty and ingratiating; Brian Bowles, in a zoot suit until briefly dragged up as a hapless Forces Favourite with a Rose Murphy falsetto; and Richard Lee, the prime mover, who implants Frankie Howerd grimaces on a countenance that is definitely Albert Finney.
