I don't want to describe too much of the plot but I have to say that in the course of Andrew Sachs's wordless radio play, The Revenge (repeated 31 July Radio 3), somebody's motor-bike gets stolen. The owner is disturbed, as who would not be, and his feelings get the better of him. He breaks the rules and says something. He tries to disguise it as an angry mumble but the words get through. 'Hey,' he says, 'come back 'ere.'

Well, everyone is entitled to a single lapse, and four words hardly count as dialogue. The Revenge may be wordless but it is far from voiceless. It utilised 11 actors, chief among them the author himself. Good casting: Mr Sachs is best known for his tour-de-force performance as Manuel, the incoherent Spanish waiter of Fawlty Towers. He's used to doing without recognisable words. In The Revenge he plays a man on the run, and he does a lot of panting. Also, of course, a lot of running; and he did it all himself. For, though The Revenge is composed of sounds, it eschews pre-packaged effects. It was shot - I mean recorded - entirely on location. The recording took seven days, the editing another seven; and it still worked out cheaper than if they had done it in a studio. By 'they' I mean Sachs, his director Glyn Dearman, and Lloyd Silverthorne, who is credited with the 'technical realisation'. He could not, in the circumstances, be called a studio manager.

Sachs wrote the play, for fun really, in 1963. Dry your eyes; he has not spent the intervening 15 years campaigning bitterly for its production. He seems, in fact, to have forgotten about it. Its rediscovery has enabled Dearman to pursue a thesis and to ask a question. The thesis is that radio drama has come to rely too much on language and to neglect - or worse, stereotype - other forms of aural communication: too much chat and not enough noise, in other words. (I know I shouldn't be using other words; unfortunately I can't draw.) The question was whether, if you tried it on, audiences would understand.

The question was asked quite literally. Listeners were requested to write in with their reactions after the play was first broadcast (on 1 June); a gratifying 990 obliged, and an even more gratifying 985 of these had managed to follow the main drift of the story. Nobody, though, had managed to grasp every detail; but no two sets of lacunae were alike. Even more interestingly, there was no appreciable difference in response between those who heard the play in mono (on 'battered old trannies') and those who were able to receive it in its full binaural glory.

Reviewer's privileges (a pair of headphones and a darkened room in Broadcasting House) put me in the second category. Being a verbal animal, I was astonished, not to say complacent, at how much I took in. (My score, the production team said, after quizzing me, was strictly average.)

I can imagine - though I don't know that I would enjoy - a production conceived entirely in terms of mechanical sound; this one emphasised the human element, to the extent that it became a bit frustrating. I kept expecting speech to break out - it would have been natural - and there was strain as well as wit inherent in its banishment. But the maintenance of suspense was extraordinary; and so, when you come to think of it, was the justification of the title. For revenge, after all, entails motivation; we had to be assured that the violence in which the play ends was not the random act of a psychotic. The play achieves this by the use of a phone-call (not, perish the thought, a phone conversation) linking assailant and victim; we may not have known them but we knew they knew each other.

Listeners, of course, made a few connections that were not consciously intended. I assumed, for example, that children I heard at one point were in hot pursuit of the ice-cream van that had immediately preceded them. Not, apparently, so. Reactions like this, however, have suggested to Dearman a possible next step: give the audience some sounds and let them make up their own story.

Television directors proverbially wish that they were making films; radio directors seem to be tending more and more towards the same ambition. (I know from my own experience that there is a special kick - admittedly not always shared by the actors - in recording scenes out of sequence and putting them together in the editing channel.) Dearman's eyes gleamed as he spoke of the excitements of location: of waiting for the right silence - as elusive as the right light - and of recording scenes of underwater escape in a swimming-pool. Here, by the way, about one minute in, I got lost. I recognised the presence of water and assumed that someone was making his way through it but the quality of his gurgles was not enough to make me picture him as a frogman. Lloyd Silverthorne shook his head reproachfully when I confessed, but agreed to forgive me. The error didn't impair my understanding of the ensuing bits; future listeners take note, not to say heart. Who, I wonder, will be the first radio stuntman? Come to that, does Andrew Sachs count as the first radio mime?

Anyone who has listened to much radio drama, let alone worked in the medium, will share the impatience of Sachs and Dearman with the standard effects: the eternal 'pub background', for example, or the doors that open and close whenever someone comes into a room. Progressive radio production, ironically enough, is often a matter of eliminating noise.

All the same, there are ways in which I would like radio drama to be less Spartan from time to time; it could sometimes emulate light entertainment in using effects as aural slapstick. It could even exploit its own clichés. Nobody pretends that wordless radio drama is an end to be pursued very far; The Revenge was intended basically as a demonstration, and it's a brilliant one. But there may be room for a companion-piece: studio-bound and comic.

Robert Cushman is drama critic of The Observer.
