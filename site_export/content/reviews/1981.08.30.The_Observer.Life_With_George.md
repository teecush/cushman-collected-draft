Life with George

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

The week brought four American plays to London, all full of marriage. It climaxed fittingly with the legendary George and Martha of Edward Albee's Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? (Lyttelton).

As marriages go in the modern theatre they have a good one. They share a sense of humour, which we are always being told is the essential thing. If it is destructive of course; they pass from tearing the skin off each other to flaying the younger couple who are their guests for an extended late-night early-morning drinking session. (As a temperance tract this piece ranks second only to 'The Iceman Cometh.')

George is a history professor promoted far short of his wildest dreams, Martha is the daughter of the university professor and never lets George forget his failure. But the play ends with him comforting her, and this finale, which was condemned as sentimental when the play first appeared, now seems perfectly logical. This is not a Strindbergian couple tied together by their hatred of each other. They hate themselves, but that's different.

The reality of this hatred gives the play more depth than you find in the average American wisecracking comedy; but it has all the vivacity proper to the genre. It used to be said that the play was great fun in its first two acts, and fell apart in its third, where we are told the truth about George and Martha's son, who serves as pretext and hostage in their verbal battles. We find they've made him up; a revelation to which the only response was, and ever will be, 'big deal.' So they're not buccaneering truth-tellers; they're abject -- and improbably consistent -- fantasists. This makes them more pitiable, but much less interesting. Now the play is a classic we know what's coming, and the anticlimax casts its shadow from way ahead.

It's not helped by the self-consciousness of the construction. George and Martha are constantly threatening that now they will really take the gloves off. But all that happens, melodramatics apart, is that the two jokers go on joking.

Development in this play really rests with their two victimised guests, and the realisation of this is one of the strengths of Nancy Meckler's production. Mary Maddox as Honey, the perpetual child-bride, draws a straight line from ingenuousness through shock to hysteria. David Schofield as her scientist husband charts a square-cut progress from shocked upholder of good manners to willing sexual prey of his hostess, goaded en route by George's rhetorical elaborations on the conflict between their two disciplines, one of his best little jokes. (If taken seriously, some of the decline-of-civilisation stuff is an ominous forecast of Mr Albee's subsequent plays, all much shorter, but seeming much longer.)

Meanwhile two of Britain's best, and most seemingly indigenous, players magnificently extend themselves. I last saw Margaret Tyzack on stage at Stratford, Ontario, in a range of roles that no actress could beat; but her most characteristic vein is that of a good sport and it makes, however distended, a fine base for Martha. She has found an American range of gesture: arms raised in mock horror, a swinging walk for raids on her husband or the drinks cabinet. Paul Eddington is, with the possible exception of Donald Sinden, our most polished high comedian (and there's still time for him to fulfill his destiny in 'The Importance of Being Earnest'); his George is a lethal teddybear, attacking silkily or volcanically from all quarters.

It's obvious that, whatever their friends may say, George and Martha will never split up.

Paul and Susan, in Mike Weller's Loose Ends (Hampstead), are the opposite, the golden pair with everything going for them. Naturally they don't last; though equally naturally they will never quite let go of each other, and will probably spend regular periods for the rest of their lives kicking themselves. We follow them through the 1970s (about time, after all those plays about the Sixties, an overrated period in which a lot of pop singers got rich).

They meet on the beach at Bali; ten years later they are both New York successes, he having switched from teaching to film-editing, she after a slow start having made it as a photographer.

Their marriage splinters because she doesn't want children; but without devaluing this quarrel the author only conveys that if it hadn't been this it would have been something else. A happy relationship is by definition precarious, easily taken for granted, easily wrecked. Paul and Susan know their luck and -- as one of their most perceptive friends points out -- can't stop worrying about it; and unloading their worries on other people.

* * *

Neil Simon's Chapter Two (Lyric, Hammersmith) is about second marriage; between a widower and a divorcee, seen very much from the former's point of view. He is played, with a great fund of unforced emotion, by Gary Waldhorn; a long speech on the guilt he still feels for missing his first wife is the only moment of sustained reality in the play's second half. By this time Mr Simon is writing sob-stuff, working his way to an improbable reunion that comes over as the impurest Broadway sunshine.

The first half, which is courtship, is much better; the jokes are good and are quite believable as defence mechanisms. Mr Simon doesn't make concessions to audiences; only to himself. Maureen Lipman is a delightful heroine, but she is kept short of material and ends up effortful. I had forgotten the sub-plot about the affair between the protagonists' two married confidantes and wasn't too happy to be reminded of it, but it at least occasions a very funny performance of outsized neurosis from Lynda Bellingham.

* * *

The play stands up better to a second look than Mark Medoff's Children of a Lesser God (Mermaid). This makes a worthy point about the need to regard the deaf (and by implication other disabled) as people with their own rights, even their own culture, but once made, it's made. In New York I enjoyed it as a love story. There are still some sparks, though there are fewer sexual sparks between Elizabeth Quinn and Trevor Eve than between their American counterparts.
