No laughter in Court

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

AT THE ROYAL COURT:
25 Years of the English Stage Company
edited by Richard Findlater
Amber Lane Press £12.95

IN an age when theatrical history is forgotten as soon as made, this book is immensely welcome. Described as a "celebration," it divides the Royal Court's history from 1956 to 1980 into seven sections. Each begins with a brusque survey, by Richard Findlater, of the plays produced, and the writers, actors, directors and designers responsible. Three or four of these then offer their reminiscences.

There are 23 contributors, counting Mr Findlater himself and Samuel Beckett who, mindful of his reputation, has sent a two-sentence greeting telegram. Then there are appendices; most crucially and creditably there is a list of all plays presented at the Court, upstairs and down, weekdays and Sundays, complete with casts: the kind of vital material that is becoming increasingly hard to find in print.

Vying in importance are a glorious series of photographs, apparently chosen by one of the Court's major directors, Bill Gaskill. Some are creamy with nostalgia: the 1950s (that check shirt worn alternately by Jimmy and Alison in Look Back in Anger, all those ties sported by the prickliest rebels) seem incredibly distant. But the most evocative shot of all is of the empty stage stretching unexpectedly deep as seen from the dress circle. John Osborne's accompanying claim "I think you can do anything on that kind of stage" seems impossible to dispute.

It is the theatre itself that provokes loyalties. Nobody has heard of the ESC; everyone has heard of the Court. To abandon it for larger or more "democratic" premises elsewhere would, as is intimated at the end of the book, be criminal madness. Nowhere else could such creative eccentricity be expected to thrive.

Outsiders -- critics especially -- will be relieved to find that their own love-hate (or at any rate admiration-mistrust) for the set-up is fully shared by those within. Among the most engaging contributors are Jack Shepherd recalling "baffling" acting classes and baffled playwrights ("no one wants a drink with a playwright who has just learned something") and Christopher Hampton on the fate of his play The Philanthropist ("disgracefully successful, so much so that I've always felt I left under something of a cloud").

Mr Hampton also writes amusingly of the Court's attitude ("savagery and contempt") towards the work of other theatres. Tolerant himself, he was "shocked." He's not alone. Court directors, like anybody, have the right to hate or walk out of any shows they please to, but dismissal of everybody else's work may not be the best way of enlivening your own. Over the years I haven't had many laughs in Sloane Square, and I grew weary in this book of the adjective "serious": a questionable theatrical virtue anyway, and here used mainly as a synonym for "our (or, in extreme cases, my) work." The Court is not the only theatre that seeks to present writers' work as well as possible (most places try) and a monochrome production style is not always the best means to that end.

Critics, often berated by the Court for not taking it at its own valuation, may note that they have frequently been more enthusiastic about the plays than have the Courtiers themselves: from The Entertainer on, it seems that no play has been mounted without a fearful internal struggle.

The founding figure of George Devine emerges as in Irving Wardle's fine biography, as movingly impressive: a practical genius. There seems general agreement that after his death the place went into decline; you can hear, beneath the usually benevolent tone of this book, the older and younger generations blaming each other. Once again Mr Hampton has the most balanced comment: "it is no longer ... the only outlet for serious new work, and if the offspring are as healthy or even occasionally healthier than the parent, that is not necessarily to the Court's discredit."

I think myself that there has been an upswing since 1977, marked by the appointment of Stuart Burge, a director from outside the Court tradition but with something of Devine's breadth of background. His successor Max Stafford-Clark has promised a "totally traditional" programme of "idealism tempered by compromise." That's more than a good joke.
