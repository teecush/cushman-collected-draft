On the razor's edge

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Jonathan Pryce's Hamlet and The Shadow of a Gunman.

THE ovation which greeted Jonathan Pryce's Hamlet (Royal Court) announced that, for the first time since David Warner played the role 15 years ago, a generation has found and crowned its prince.

Mr Warner was acclaimed less as an actor than as an archetype; he was taken to be the essence of studenthood, 1965. The interest attaching to Mr Pryce is more strictly gladiatorial: that of seeing a prized young actor, not in himself particularly representative of anything, match his own talent to the role. Nobody expected heroic acting of Mr Warner; everybody expects it of Mr Pryce.

Heroic acting has nothing to do with nobility, or even with vocal resonance. It depends on authority, on variety, on the ability to play each moment to the full. More than anything, perhaps, it means the ability to remain attractive while behaving outrageously — in this play above all others.

It is odd, considering what they have to put up with from him, how much everyone in the play likes Hamlet. Nobody — except Claudius when lying and Laertes when duped — ever says a word against him. Mr Pryce's power and intelligence are amply backed up by sheer cheek — it may be his distinguishing mark as an actor — and few of Hamlet's attitudes give him visible trouble. No figure could be more palpably heartbroken than the one he presents in the first scene: with that cadaverous face, how could he wear anything but black? He seems totally open to grief; you see why Gertrude, Horatio and Ophelia all want to protect him.

When it comes to the great central encounters, nobody could balance more elegantly or provocatively on the razor's edge of pretended madness, whether baiting Rosencrantz and Guildenstern or tearfully abandoning Ophelia. (His quiet 'I did love you once,' when she produces his old letters, is inspired.) His technique is both formidable and individual: over a continuous ground-bass of hysteria, he executes the most telling and scrupulous individual cadenzas. Only in the graveyard struggle with Laertes — where rhetoric is for once undiluted by self-satire — do his guns fail him.

If you wish to see an actor wrestling vibrantly, wittily and honestly with a great role, then London offers nothing better. You will not, though, find an overall solution to the problems of the character. I wonder if Mr Pryce isn't a gentler actor than anyone (including himself) likes to believe; his description of the dead Polonius ('foolish, prating knave') comes out surprisingly affectionate, and though he seems to be aiming for a dark prince at the close he is frustrated by the text in which Hamlet acts with inescapable decency, never killing anyone unless seriously provoked.

Meanwhile Mr Pryce has presented us with a new interpretative conundrum, though it may have originated purely as the practical answer to a problem of production. The director Richard Eyre has decided against a visible Ghost, or even an independently audible one. The Ghost speaks through Hamlet, who struggles and retches as if in the grip of diabolic possession. This of course adds great point to the spirit that I have seen may be a devil, and to the later visitation in the closet-scene, but it seems to have little bearing on what the sane Mr Pryce does with the rest of the play. As it is, we admire his skill at switching between voices, but wonder whether the play doesn't present him with quite enough challenges as it is.

Mr Eyre's production has the same virtues and limitations as Mr Pryce's performance. It is thoroughly sensible and thoroughly alive, but offers no overall view. It does, however — much aided by William Dudley's burnished beehive of a set — offer atmosphere. This Elsinore, composed of an elegant series of adjoining timbered cubicles, is a paradise for eavesdroppers. It can be quickly, if noisily, rearranged while the air resounds with bangs and thumps, suggesting that the Danish royals are engaged in frenzied diplomatic activity or alternatively that they have the builders in. Ophelia's grave is not a trap, but a shelf in an upright mausoleum brimful of skulls. How the gravedigger can tell which is Yorick's is more of a mystery than ever.

Geoffrey Chater's brilliant Polonius is the scene made flesh: prying, self-satisfied, rabbiting both to and about his children, but showing no affection for them whatever. Michael Elphick's grossly practical Claudius is positively sympathetic by comparison, aptly conveying that he, like Hamlet, is in over his head, lumbered with a destiny he would far rather escape. Jill Bennett's Gertrude, like most others, is more impressive out of love with him than in; Harriet Walter's Ophelia, similarly, is a sweet blank at first, but improves with madness. Christopher Logue arrives as the First Player, bringing with him (for no very good reason) what I assume is his own rewriting of the Hecuba speech. A couple of small-part players I could cheerfully have strangled; otherwise it was a cheering and a cheered occasion.

* * *

Michael Pennington, who is to be the year's second Hamlet, is limbering up in the first of O'Casey's comedies of the Troubles, The Shadow of a Gunman (Other Place, Stratford-upon-Avon), as Donal Davoren, another poet posing as a killer. Mr Pennington has a particular talent for monitoring the movement and the meaning of any scene in which he is involved; sometimes this can be distracting, but the clarity with which he shows us Davoren's vanity, and his flirtations with the hero-worshipping Minnie, are immensely helpful. We get not a straight man and a clown, but two equally comic cowards, the other being the pedlar Seamus Shields, whom Norman Rodway — giving his best performance in ages — keeps rigorously clear of stage-Irish ingratiation.

I had always regarded the play as containing some good jokes, a burst of effective melodrama and a lot of winsome padding. Michael Bogdanov's production shows that, though the play is built like a vaudeville, there is muscle in every turn. All the intrusions build. Dearbhla Molloy is a merry, touching Minnie, and the play stands as one of the few in which the author can generalise about a country because he has been so particular about its inhabitants.

Another Irish actor, J. G. Devlin, can be seen at his best in the National Theatre's generally admirable production of The Iceman Cometh, which this week is visiting the Theatre Royal, Bath.
