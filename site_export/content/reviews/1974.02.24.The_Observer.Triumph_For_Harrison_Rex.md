Triumph for Harrison Rex

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a Pirandello revival.

NOT the least merit of Clifford Williams's production of Pirandello's Henry IV (Her Majesty's) is that it destroys the play's reputation as one of the clumsiest pieces of narrative ever to attain the status of a modern classic.

It really is quite easy to follow, even for those who arrive at the theatre sharing the delusion of the young man so fetchingly portrayed in this production by Robin Sachs; he imagines, until corrected, that the monarch of the title is Henry IV of England; whom Shakespeare so memorably bisected. In fact, as he rapidly learns, we are concerned with an earlier Henry, the twelfth-century German Emperor who opposed the Pope and was forced to expiate his defiance by kneeling in the snow at Canossa.

Not that the play is really about the historical Henry: its protagonist is a twentieth-century Italian who dressed up as the Emperor in a carnival procession, fell from his horse, was concussed and awoke in the belief that he was the man he had been impersonating. When the play begins he has been maintained in this illusion for 20 years, surrounded and humoured by servants in period costume. At this point he is visited by a group of people who believe that he may be restored to sanity, among them the woman whom he wooed in vain before his accident and his former rival, now her lover.

It is, though, too late for "Henry" to be cured; he has known the truth for eight years, during which he has enjoyed prolonging the masquerade. Rex Harrison projects this enjoyment quite magnificently. Always he preserves a careful distance from everyone else, alternately confounding them with his courtesy and his rudeness. At times it is difficult to distinguish the two; Mr Harrison is constantly cocking his head towards his interlocutors, a gesture which denotes simultaneously an earnest desire to hear what they have to say and an implicit dismissal of it. He knows who his visitors are, of course, and he derives a sharp-edged pleasure from their uneasiness in the disguises which they must adopt in order to be admitted to his presence; faced with his old rival Belcredi, unhappily attired as a Benedictine monk, he twits him with being one of the Emperor's historical enemies and cannot be persuaded otherwise. The feigning madman is traditionally the quickest-witted man on stage and Mr Harrison is the most mentally agile of actors.

He is also the most lucid; and here we are in trouble. We are not informed that Henry has recovered from his delusion until halfway through the play; up until then we should at least have been in doubt and the revelation should strike us like a thunderclap. Here there is no surprise; Mr Harrison's conscious manipulation of the situation has been too obvious, and despite his admirably uncompromising appearance, his rouged cheeks and greying hair, nothing in his personality suggests madness.

Shorn of its ambiguity, the play loses much else. Perhaps it is to the point that while Henry, by remaining for 20 years in the role of the young emperor abasing himself at Canossa, has made a futile attempt to conquer time (how futile those grey locks bear witness), Mr Harrison as an actor seems to have done precisely that; his perennial vigour is amazing. Pirandello's hero is an all-time loser; in the end, the play being in part an inverted revenge-tragedy, much indebted to Hamlet, Henry runs Belcredi through. The penalty here, though, is not death, but death-in-life; having apparently given proof of his relapse into madness he is cast back for all time into his fantasy, knowing it to be a fraud. It is an appalling prospect; but Mr Harrison looks as if he might squeeze some fun from it.

None of this criticism constitutes an excuse for missing the performance. Mr Harrison is almost a great actor--who fits, by a truly Pirandellian irony, into hardly any of the great parts--and his wit, authority and vocal resource are to be treasured. Besides, missing his Henry would entail missing the Donna Matilda of Yvonne Mitchell, an actress whose work has recently taken on a rare and rewarding boldness of outline without losing any of its accustomed truth; her portrayal of Henry's lost love is uncompromising in its vanity and self-pity, its uneasy regrets and final terror.

James Villiers's lounge-lizard Belcredi is expectedly expert, and Peter Cellier as the senior member of the imperial entourage has an unforced style and presence which recall standards that could once (though, alas, no longer) be taken for granted in the West End. This production, with its spacious sets by Farrah, comes nearer those standards than most: nearer, for example, than The Waltz of the Toreadors (and there is a part for Mr Harrison) across the road.

* * *

If I may just go back to where I came in, Jean Genet's The Maids makes very few concessions to those (me, for instance) who like a good story well told, and Minos Volonakis's production at Greenwich makes even fewer. Those who care may argue whether the play's psychological content has been sacrificed to its Black Mass aspect or vice versa (I have seen both views advanced); I would merely like to quote Pauline Kael's gloss on "ritual" as "a word that has come to stand for much of what I could happily do without in the theatre" and to endorse it, where Black Masses are concerned, in spades.

Yolanda Sonnabend's bedroom is, shell-like (it was only when the walls were referred to as "the ears of God" that I got the message); it is also geometrically floral, like the dance routines of Busby Berkeley. Indeed, when Vivien Merchant comes on as Madame, lamenting her jailed lover, it is briefly possible to see the whole thing as a transplanted Warner-Brothers musical; Miss Merchant actually looks very 1930s and might easily pass for the society dame in hopeless thrall to the cynical bootlegger. She sounds very sleepy throughout.

Nor is Glenda Jackson absolutely the life of the party as the more ferocious of the domestics, though she does her own intense thing to some effect towards the end. She is partnered, vibrantly if too relentlessly, by Susannah York who should appear on our stages more often, if only to learn how to husband her very impressive resources. Neither actress can escape from the stranglehold of their author, who is interested in them only as the vehicle of his own obsessions. (Madame, being satirised and therefore objectified, is a more rewarding role, and Miss Merchant does gather a few laughs.) Mr Volonakis's production is fittingly portentous (you might argue that the rhetoric should be played down, but I cannot see what would be left) and the run is already sold out.

* * *

That being so, hasten to the Hampstead Theatre Club which this week offers amazing reductions: prices slashed by half, since the play Bremen Coffee lasts only an hour (The Maids is 90 minutes), within which time its heroine plans and commits a dozen murders (the maids plan one, and bungle it). Though she flourished in Germany in the 1830s, her author (Rainer Werner Fassbinder) sees her as an extremist forerunner of Women's Lib, ready to poison anyone who denies her sexual satisfaction or the control of her own business. Linda Marlowe conveys the former obsession more powerfully than the latter, but the production, elegantly acted and set, testifies anew to the potential of multiple murder (especially when kept in the family, cf. Lizzie Borden) as a source of harmless theatrical fun.
