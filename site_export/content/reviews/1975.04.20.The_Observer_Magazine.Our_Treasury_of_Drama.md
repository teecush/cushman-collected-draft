Our Treasury of Drama

Robert Cushman on our treasury of drama

THE ENGLISH theatre is proverbially insular. We have—or so we are told—a glorious dramatic heritage, and we see no good reason to complicate it with the works of untranslatable Europeans. We do actually make a few exceptions; insularity is not, after all, the same thing as xenophobia. We have tolerated Ibsen, and we have virtually adopted Chekhov.

The situation may well change. The new National Theatre complex will gobble up quantities of plays and there are enough institutional or semi-institutional theatres to begrudge them every mouthful. We have half-a-dozen companies chasing the same plays (and to a large extent the same actors). They will have to discover some new ones; or, rather, some old ones. The golden and silver ages of English drama—the ‘Elizabethan’ and ‘Restoration’ periods—are likely to be extensively revisited. What follows is a sketch-map of the terrain, noting some recently discovered features, and others still neglected.

It should be noted that a single revival of a play proves nothing, except perhaps the intrepidity of the company responsible. A piece may really be considered to have entered the national repertory when a second revival is undertaken within, say, 15 years.

Some kind of relationship can usually be traced between a play’s academic prestige and its theatrical appearances. The school textbooks themselves remain in circulation. We see little medieval drama in our theatre, but when we do it is likely to be extracted from the biblical mystery cycles, originally performed by the craft-guilds. These are favoured less for their religious content than for their status as community drama; even their multiple authorship works in their favour in a culture where the individual is fashionably disprized.

The sheer size of the mystery cycles is another enticement; apart from Shakespeare’s histories, they are the nearest we have come to epic theatre. Of the four major cycles extant, those of York, Chester and Coventry have all in recent years been mounted in their cities of origin. The fourth cycle, that of Wakefield, has had to come south for recognition; it has been staged in London several times, by the Mermaid and the Young Vic, which have both cultivated (if that is the right word) an informal, broadly comic house-style ideal for the plays’ unsophisticated anachronistic humour, if not for their grandeur of scope.

The best of the Wakefield plays—those credited to an unknown ‘Wakefield master’—are, when read or seen in the context of the whole cycle, stunningly good. They detach themselves from their surroundings, rather as Shakespeare does from the work of his contemporaries. Suddenly the lines cease to be well-meaning doggerel and take on vigour and stage-presence; there is even a sense of dramatic (rather than just narrative) form. This is particularly evident in the celebrated Shepherds’ play, the first good play in the language.

The second is generally agreed to be the morality drama ‘Everyman’; it is probably the best known of all medieval plays. Best known, that is, by name (and possibly by association with Everyman’s Library, which derived from it both its name and its motto) and perhaps in its printed text. But it has had no recent stage history; it may take first place among the schoolbook classics on which our theatre has turned its back. a small candle for George Peele’s ‘The Old Wives’ Tale’, in which a bedtime story takes charm and spectacular shape before the astonished eye of its narrator.

And there remains one major dramatist whose development spanned the whole period and who has remained unstaged since the seventeenth century. George Chapman, whose translation of Homer inspired Keats’s sonnet, wrote some reflectively resonant tragedies, but it is his comedies that now suggest themselves for performance.

The major Restoration comedies have been fashionable since the 1920s. Critics who condemned them as immoral or (more astringently) as painfully frivolous, merely confirmed the theatrical view of the plays as a daring brand of escapism. Nowadays we are much soberer and have discovered social realism; the best productions in this vein have mercifully not neglected the acute and joyous sexual patternings that give the plays their life.

Modern criticism is at last getting to grips with the plays, and the theatre is helping, though it is still fighting shy of the most powerful writer of the period. William Wycherley’s ‘The Country Wife’ is theoretically a staple of the repertoire, and it may be only chance that has kept it out of London for nearly 20 years. But the savagery of ‘The Plain Dealer’ seems to frighten everybody off.

Of all our classic dramatists Wycherley is the one most urgently in need of reclamation. Unless you count Henry Fielding, the last writer of the great ages, who, at a time when Restoration comedy had long lost its celebrated raffishness and had given itself over to decent sentiment and indecent sentimentality, turned out topical squibs and burlesque tragedies, which are still funny to read, and which it must be possible to stage (though nobody has yet discovered how) so that we understand the jokes. There is one hilarious piece entitled ‘The Historical Register for the Year 1736’. The day some manager puts that in his bills, we shall really have a liberated repertoire.
