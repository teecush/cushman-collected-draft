The laughing Shylock

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Stratford and London.

I HAVE never seen a funnier Shylock than the one offered by David Suchet in John Barton's new Stratford production of The Merchant of Venice; or one more moving.

Obviously the two things go together, but getting the balance right is the problem that bedevils every production of this play. I happen to have seen three in the past four weeks. Shared Experience's Jew was a masked cringer in a convention that seems right for the play in theory, but breaks down in practice; the character undeniably expands as the play progresses, but this version refused to grow with it. Timothy West at the Old Vic was strong on pride, vindictiveness and professional concern: a well-rounded portrait in fact, but with no context to fit into.

Mr Barton is probably better on context -- comic context anyway -- than any other Shakespearean director. Ian McKellen once said of him approvingly that despite his reputation he wasn't an academic, he was a great vulgar showman; and he is also an insistent realist. More and more he has been setting plays in recognisable, if anachronistic, societies, with all rules observed; and more and more he probes for human details, asking obvious but generally neglected questions.

No other director has spotlit Portia's realisation that, with two suitors down, the leaden casket must be the one. Sinead Cusack's Portia indeed is understandably fixated on her father's trio of oversized fortune cookies; when we first meet her she is staring at them with fascinated loathing. She only learns to laugh when Bassanio gets them off her back.

Mr Suchet, by contrast, laughs from the start. Laughter -- mocking himself and everybody else -- is Shylock's line of defence. The amount that Mr Suchet conveys through the one word 'well' -- repeated at the end of each of his first three lines -- is extraordinary. 'You,' he says to Antonio and Bassanio, 'are coming to me': the ludicrousness of the situation gets to them as well. It embarrasses Bassanio, as it is meant to, but Tom Wilkinson's Antonio (who seems the most manic of depressives) can see the joke, especially the really hilarious bit about the pound of flesh.

There is of course another level of fun -- revenge-comedy -- that Shylock shares only with the audience. Mr Suchet's skill in drawing out and differentiating these strands is dazzling; but it does involve him in a continuous use of jovial falsetto and I began to wonder if he had anything else in store.

The aftermath of Jessica's elopement proves that he has. On 'to bait fish withal,' though it's still a savage joke, he strikes a table with his stick, and with a force that nearly jerked me out of my seat. The ensuing scene with Raymond Westwell's Tubal (all unctuous, sadistic concern) is a masterpiece of cat-and-mouse, with Shylock sometimes playing both roles himself; 'to curse never fell upon our nation' is undercut by a self-pitying 'I never felt it till now,' whose absurdity the speaker half-recognises as he speaks it.

Probably only a Jewish actor would take such risks in this role; Mr Suchet has no need to stand on his dignity, knowing that the play will take care of that for him. And it may be that only a Jewish actor -- an honest, uncompromising one -- can make sense of the play now. As written, The Merchant probably is thoughtlessly anti-Semitic; what redeems it, even makes it useful, is its insight into the effects of persecution.

* * *

The key line is not 'Hath not a Jew eyes,' but 'since I am a dog beware my fangs.' This is the passion that carries Mr Suchet through the trial scene, and on to disaster, by way of Portia's spurned appeal for charity: the moment at which the play's lines satisfyingly cross. She grows as he dwindles.

For all its charm and intelligence, Miss Cusack's Portia comes over as a directed performance, where Mr Suchet's is autonomous and unpredictable, and quite different in its sophistication from Patrick Stewart's greasy usurer in Mr Barton's very similar Other Place production. Terry Wood is physically and vocally a magnanimous Morocco, and Lorenzo -- not the most rewarding role in the play -- is given an intriguing gentleman-sportsman air by Michael Siberry.

Mr Barton goes in too much for the everybody-joining-in-the-joke joke, better handled at the Vic, where Jessica's chiming in with Shylock on his 'fast bind, fast find' proverb told us something about their relationship. Aided by Christopher Morley's twin set (gondola-poles for Venice, branches for Belmont), Mr Barton otherwise furnishes a musical, autumnal production, whose mercantile tone is set by constant recourse to brandy and cigars.

That makes it the fourth transported classic in succession. Like the Irish Seagull and the Caribbean Measure for Measure, it seems motivated by a mischievous desire to see what the play will look like in a new suit of clothes. The Knight of the Burning Pestle (reviewed too briefly last week), with a punk apprentice, is more concerned to make the play comprehensible to a modern audience. This, which appears the more respectable reason, is actually the more disreputable. Michael Bogdanov is being condescending both to his actors and his audience if he despairs of making the play funny as written.

And there are other problems. When Beaumont made his Jacobean Citizen's Wife claim that she'd never seen a play before, it was credible, and she had some excuse for mixing up life and drama. Margaret Courtenay's matron at the Aldwych has obviously spent a lifetime watching soap-opera on TV. She knows the score. Mr Bogdanov has had perforce to keep the play-within-a-play in doublet and hose; why should the Citizen's Wife now treat Mistress Merrythought as her neighbour when they are obviously separated by centuries? And isn't it rather a drag for Timothy Spall as Rafe to have to throw new grunts into old pentameters?

To overcome all these doubts, and still come up with a monstrously enjoyable show, Mr Bogdanov and troupe have to work very hard. Sometimes their endless inventiveness seems endless, but mostly it is extraordinarily apposite. There is a pedantic streak in Mr Bogdanov that compels him to find an equivalent for everything of the original he has jettisoned. Beaumont satirised his own theatre; so does he.

I doubt if there is any company as confident in its own identity and its audience's to spoof itself as successfully as the RSC does here. It produces a very warm feeling. The production shares the repertoire with the revived Nicholas Nickleby (all seats sold, eat your hearts out), so the Aldwych will probably find themselves faced with yet another housing problem.

* * *

The La Claca company from Catalonia apparently felt, after their successful collaboration with Joan Miro on Mori el Merma, that they were in danger of losing their own identity, becoming Miro images. On the basis of their Antologia (Riverside Studios), their fears were justified, but to act on them ill-advised. The performers (only two this time) are at their best when they re-envelop themselves in Miro's creations for an extract from their greatest hit. In their other work, earlier and later in date, they function mainly as puppeteers, and both their bite and their charm are smothered in whimsy.

I can't quite read my notes, but I think the best line in the feminist revue Female Trouble (Arts) was 'female trouble -- you can give it or receive it -- it's better to give.' Unfortunately the rest of Bryony Lavery's script fails to match it, either in wit or in spirit. These performers too are better at satirising themselves than anyone else. They make good jokes about women; when it comes to men, they simply state grievances. The music is variable, the performance level -- Anne-Marie Davies, Caroline Noh, Lou Wakefield -- very high, and the set, mostly composed of cutout zebras, mysteriously attractive.
