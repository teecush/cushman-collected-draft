Fighting it out in the suburbs

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a new play at the National.

THERE is a curious innocence about John Hopkins's plays; at times they bleed like open wounds.

Their vulnerability can turn them into actors' feasts. From the television quartet Talking to a Stranger everyone remembers Judi Dench's exhilarating tirades. In New York last week I saw a remarkable young actor, Michael Moriarty, rising effortlessly, with no apparent build-up, to climax after climax as a volatile desperate homosexual in Mr Hopkins's Find Your Way Home, which flopped in London a couple of years ago.

His later plays are more secretive. It took us a long time to find out what was bugging the unfortunate husband in Economic Necessity. We learn even less about a similar figure in his new play at the National, Next of Kin. As James Laurenson plays him (eye-catchingly well), Brian Lloyd, suburban husband in his thirties, is hesitant, choked, withdrawn, and happiest when putting a toy tank together. He likes using his hands, is not at all happy in his job as a car salesman. So he walks out, in the first act, and to my regret is never seen again.

It could be that he leaves merely to avoid meeting his relatives. The occasion of the play is one of Mr Hopkins's Sunday afternoon family reunions. On their way to Brian's home are his two sisters (with their husbands); his brother, without his wife; and their widowed mother. All arrived, they settle down to a round of recrimination and intrigue as bloody-minded and protracted as you could find in several months of other dramatists' Sundays.

The innocence remains; Mr Hopkins approaches the horrors of family life as if nobody had ever dramatised them before. The naivete can be excruciating: the play yields a choice collection of lines you have wished never to hear again. "You want to hurt him," says A, to which B replies, "Naturally, I love him." A mother is informed that she resents her children "because you made them and they aren't everlastingly grateful." The nastiest of the women declares herself with the words, "I'm not responsible; I won't be made responsible." Occasionally the dialogue goes antiphonal. "He was himself," remarks somebody of the dead father; "a man to be remembered," caps somebody else. At moments like these Harold Pinter's production becomes suitably, if half-heartedly, hieratic.

There are compensations. Mr Hopkins may have overloaded his battlefield (all these crises in one afternoon?), but he knows his way round it, and he draws a subtle distinction between the ties of family and those of marriage. His siblings may hate one another, but they can still constitute a united force. Indeed, their hatred may be their strength; against it Brian's wife, Susan--their reluctant hostess--can muster only a cold dislike, related as she is by marriage, but not by blood. (The title really proclaims the play.) When the adult pressure falters, a suitably horrible brood of children can take over, confirming incidentally that the standard of juvenile acting in this country is improving out of recognition. Of the grown-ups Gemma Jones as Susan establishes herself slowly but irresistibly at the centre of the play; and, though I felt dubious about their claims to consanguinity, the others mostly justify their presence.

* * *

Having seen two first-rate musicals in New York (Candide and A Little Night Music), I approached London's two new offerings rather warily. It is probably kinder not to consider the Israeli Dominoes (Shaw) as a musical at all. Its tunes display an occasional piping charm; its lyrics may be assumed to have lost in translation. This charity might be extended to the play itself; its three characters converse in the kind of simple sentences to be found in a foreign phrase-book's early chapters. For the first act I was just about able to believe that this artlessness might conceal art; it was agreeable to watch John Bluthal, a doormat offering himself to every passing pair of feet, or to hear Richard Hampton, an apparently lucky lover, so exultant at having found someone who will weep at his funeral that he can hardly wait for that happy day to dawn.

The second act, in which these two are corralled with the lady of their choice, applies itself to larger themes, but with the same limited resources, and the result is grisly. The author, Khanoch Levin, is presented in the programme as among Israel's newest and finest; it is all the more depressing to find him offering us, in all seriousness, the stock Jewish figure of the castrating wife and mother. At least he has Dorothy Vernon, a splendid singing actress, to embody his harpy for him; were there a show she might have stopped it.

* * *

At Drury Lane there is unquestionably a show, as enormous as Dominoes is tiny. I trembled at it in prospect; it has become all too easy (all too common, anyway) to take a successful play and market it as a musical by chopping down its title to a single key word; this last season on Broadway A Raisin in the Sun and Two for the Seesaw have resurfaced as Raisin and Seesaw respectively. By American standards, Billy (ne Liar) probably rates as a formula musical; by ours it is something of a phenomenon.

It represents a successful compromise with the British theatrical genius in that its virtues belong as much to dramaturgy as to showbiz. Onna White's choreography is more notable for vigour than invention (and much of the dancing is leaden); the songs by Don Black and John Barry are all right, sometimes displaying a humour hardly to be expected from the men who once won an Oscar for Born Free, a terribly solemn ballad about (one had to assume) a lion. But the book (by Dick Clement and Ian La Frenais out of Keith Waterhouse and Willis Hall) and its direction (by Patrick Garland) are something else: funny, humane, and neatly structured. The production numbers, already an excellent device for representing Billy's fantasy life, gain from the finesse with which they are introduced. The opening routine, Ambrosia, introduces us to the hero's dream-world, and its brief reprise at the end is an apt and touching way of signifying his final retreat from reality.

Bryan Pringle and Avis Bunnage are shunted on and off stage to offer some vinegary vignettes of Billy's home life; Barry James, a short and pungent comedian, peers darkly out of dance halls and funeral parlours; and Lockwood West contributes some sterling memories of the North in the days before shopping precincts. Michael Crawford seems rather a winsome plant to have flowered in this rocky soil; having to do most of his acting through a throat-mike, on a vast and comfortless set, he is inhibited from making much contact with the life around him. He is happiest in his moments of solitary clowning, and as an appealing and energetic song-and-dance-man. A nimble mover he is, but no Fred Astaire. You would not expect him to be? Well, somebody does, because the first-half finale involves him in a top-hat routine which is not witty enough to pass as parody. Obviously we are meant to take it straight and Mr Crawford ("Ooh, isn't he clever") to our hearts. Yet again amateurism strikes the British musical, but the blow, for once, is not fatal.
