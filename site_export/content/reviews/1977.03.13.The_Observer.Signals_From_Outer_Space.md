Signals from outer space

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Illuminatus!'

THE Cottesloe Theatre, a three-tiered black box with flexible seating arrangements -- and by that token flexible acting arrangements -- opened to the public on the Friday before last. It may be described as the National Theatre's experimental space, or perhaps as its studio, or anyway as considerably smaller than the Olivier or the Lyttelton.

It is not approached through the main National foyer, but through its own tradesman's entrance at the side of the building, on your left as you clamber out of the river. The reason for this segregation, it has been said, is to spare the purchasers of the cheap seats in the Cottesloe the sight of the buyers of expensive seats in the other houses. Or possibly vice versa. Whichever way the prejudice runs, the National really has no business to pander to it; but I appreciate that this is hardly the time to start arranging the concrete blocks all over again.

The Cottesloe can seat 400; today and for the next two week-ends, as for those just past, it probably will. At least it will at 2 p.m., which is when performances of the initial offering commence. By 11 or so, which is when they tend to finish, some gaps may have become apparent in the ranks: not many though. Having stayed the course myself, and gladly, I urge you to reject all reviewers who departed early.

The show is the product of the Science Fiction Theatre of Liverpool, a new group, and it is called Illuminatus!, that being the singular of Illuminati, an old group. The Illuminati date back, apparently, to the eighteenth century (I think; you will appreciate that in a nine-hour play many statements are made, and some of them conflict) and now they control everything, rather like the Mafia, except that the Mafia is part of what they control. They are responsible for wars and political assassinations and forbidding notices hung up in supermarkets. Their agents range from Texan red-neck sheriffs to festival-circuit rock-groups. They are under the control of, or influenced by, or at least going along with, some malevolent forces from outer space.

Opposed to them are a hard-working New York cop named Saul, a gangling radical journalist called George, and -- most formidably -- Hagbard Celine, described as Last of the Freebooters. He is served by numerous beautiful and provocatively clad girls, a singing dolphin, and a computer which, for the best of reasons, talks like John Gielgud; and they all live in a yellow submarine. Except the dolphin, of course; he lives outside.

Celine, a dedicated free-market man, is an unlikely hero to find on the fringe right now, but he certainly strikes a chord. The conflict between him and the Illuminati is that between individualism (motto: "We Discordians must stick apart") and regimentation. Thus far have we come from the simple conflict between good and evil in Lord of the Rings.

I make that comparison because Illuminatus!, too, started out as a triple-decker cult-novel, the work of two Playboy scribes, Robert Shea and Robert Anton Wilson. It comes equipped with off-putting credentials (like a recommendation from Timothy Leary) and it has been compared to the works of Kurt Vonnegut, which I find impassable; it is nevertheless an immensely engaging read -- or, in my case this week, skim. It is much funnier than it is portentous.

In their stage version Ken Campbell and Chris Langham weave in and out less than their source, keep individual strands of narrative moving for longer. They have regularised the structure, so that it seems to be moving towards a definite climax: the salvation of the world, or some such trifle. When that actually happens, and turns out not to be a climax at all, one feels a bit cheated.

The show is best when it takes the material as it comes (nearly all the dialogue is straight from source) and here its greatest asset is its sheer length. It proves most of the usual quibbles about turning novels into plays to be purely academic; all you need is time, so that you are not pressured into squeezing all the juice out of the original.

The production, on a modest end stage, pursues a simple course until interrupted by some startling or hilarious image; Mr Langham's totally engaging George wedged unaccountably into a bed one-fifth his own size; or a dying gangster, raving through a damning but incomprehensible confession in a bed perched on an alarming diagonal tilt. Or perhaps Howard the Dolphin will swim by, melodiously imparting some fragment of his race's wholly oral culture. As Hagbard says, "how could they handle pens with their fins, you know?"

Neil Cunningham is a beautifully deft and uncaring Hagbard, James Broadbent and Chris Taynton play excellently any number of roles, and David Rappaport, a midget, has a long and masterly monologue which perfectly exemplifies the fusion of English music-hall's earthy charm with the airy wisecracking flights of the Americans -- a blend that I take to be Mr Campbell's peculiar contribution. The day is well sprinkled with intervals -- so many that one loses track of the five plays into which the event is supposedly divided. You simply swallow it whole, joining towards the end in a rousing chorus which goes "Situation Normal -- All Fucked Up," as wise a comment on practically everything as the theatre currently has to offer.

* * *

In Back Street Romeo (Half Moon Theatre) Steve Gooch wrecks what might have been a lively, even penetrating study of factional strife in the East End (trade unionists versus petty thugs) by pinning it on to the familiar titular love-plot in which I doubt he has much real interest. The effort to keep the two elements together makes the second half of his story impossible to follow.
