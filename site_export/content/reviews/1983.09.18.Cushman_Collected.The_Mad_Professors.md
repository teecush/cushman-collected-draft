The mad professors

Theatre

Robert Cushman on a new play by Howard Brenton.

PLAYWRIGHTS must get the horrors as they sit trying to devise a plot and characters to fit their chosen theme. A story can twist away from the point, apparently from the mere necessity of filling out an evening, and people be reduced to interchangeable ciphers, making it spin. The problem, rampant this year, is vividly illustrated in Howard Brenton's new one, The Genius (Royal Court).

Mr Brenton writes in a hard, plain style that turns out to be full of ambiguities. Start with the title, which is probably a joke on sexual conditioning. We assume it to refer to Leo Lehrer, an American mathematician washed up at a university near Birmingham. Not Alabama: Warwickshire. But the genius could also be Gilly, an undergraduate who has unknowingly worked out an equation that has already driven Leo into appalled silence. It is apparently the key to the invention of a new bomb. How Mutually Assured Destruction could be made any madder is not explained (the question is, so to speak, academic), but we swallow it anyway.

Mr Brenton is said to have got the idea from translating Brecht's Life of Galileo for the National Theatre. The scene in which Leo opens Gilly's eyes to the consequences of her own brilliance parallels Galileo's seduction with science of the Little Monk. At one moment Leo quotes Einstein's famous declaration that, had he foreseen the results of relativity, he would have settled for watchmaking. "Didn't though, did he?" snaps Gilly, bringing the play up against a point that Brecht evaded.

He treated the scientific appetite as ungovernable until his last scene, when he primly suggested that a scientist ought to confine himself to humanely (i.e. politically) sanctioned areas: an intellectual and theatrical sell-out. Friedrich Dürrenmatt was more realistic in The Physicists, the best and toughest play yet written on the subject: "What has been thought cannot be unthought."

Gilly has her own paraphrase of that: "A thought is a thought. You can't not have it." Leo shouts her down, but he never really contradicts her, and neither does the play. So, there they are, possessed through their own literally undeniable talents of intolerable knowledge. What are they to do with it?

What they should probably do is shut up, but that would mean an early end to the play. So they stage a campus happening, to demonstrate the terrors (I apologise for the inadequacy of the word) of nuclear war. The play has, you see, a secondary theme: the idea of a university. Here is an allegedly humane institution, run by artsmen seeking a rapprochement with science: Leo is actually seen as a kind of artist. (Mr Brenton's chosen parallel is music.)

And, of course, since this is a Brentonian Play for England, the university betrays itself. It is financed by government, for government. It is crawling with spies, of both factions. It shops Leo; and his friend the Bursar (who is also his cuckold: there is a lot of plot) is given a very nasty time by the police. With the secret known to the West, Gilly drops it through the letter-box of the Russian Embassy as well. Having cheerfully maintained the balance of terror, the play proceeds to a peace demo. Well, all right, I sympathise; what else is there to do?

The trouble is that all this--the play's second half--depends on rapid changes of heart that are just not dramatised. There is a full bag of clichés available to carry the narrative. The wine-sipping, cancer-ridden vice-chancellor, squatting on his shooting-stick, is one of them. So are the liberal-ineffectual don, and the spouting student activist (though for him, it turns out, Mr Brenton has an alibi).

If Leo's rebirth has to be taken for granted, his earlier cynicism is extensively documented. He even gets to tumble a lady in the snow: a scene actually written with considerable panache and strikingly intersected with the discovery of Gilly's genius sums. "Equations in the snow" is the scene-heading; and that is the kind of image for which one prizes Mr Brenton. Belief may give out, but not attention.

The play has been excellently staged by Danny Boyle, in economical designs by Peter Hartwell largely composed of evocative, adaptable streamers. There are good performances by Hugh Fraser, Clive Swift and Paul McGann, though Trevor Eve has let Leo's aggression go to his head and Anna Nygh, beginning as the bored and sexy lady (God, aren't the middle classes rotten?) standard in this kind of play, is later defeated by a staggering number of changes of front.

* * *

I always thought that I preferred oblique oddball satire to head-on political statement; so imagine my confusion at liking Mr Brenton's play more than Hanif Kureishi's Birds of Paradise (Hampstead). Mr Kureishi is clearly working out for himself a valuable comic style, but there is little substance in this account of a South London family, upper-working class, bought out by a Pakistani student. (Echoes of The Cherry Orchard only become deafening in a last scene full of dust-sheets and suitcases.)

There are good satiric touches about England now, a timeless tea of lettuce, tomato and salad-cream, and the memorable inquiry "Do you think the taxpayer educated you just to enjoy films with sub-titles?" But the dialogue is rarefied to the point of other-worldliness, especially when delivered by Joe Melia, whose acting is too informal even to be conversational.

Jean Boht, an undervalued actress, functions as housewife and much-needed anchor. Howard Davies directs, on what must be the busiest set he has ever countenanced. (I am about to say something unfair and untrue: they don't design at Hampstead, they merely shift the furniture around.)

* * *

Back to the mad-scientist theme in, of course, The Tempest. This has good comedy from Christopher Benjamin and Ian Talbot, too little of anything else from Bob Peck as Caliban, and unusually lively court scenes, with especial credit to Jeffery Dench's cynical weakling of a Sebastian.

The main merit of Ron Daniels's production, though, is the frame it provides for Derek Jacobi. Flames fly up at the end of Prospero's renunciation speech, and very impressive they are, but they are only appropriate because they correspond to the sparks that have flown from Mr Jacobi. Giving up his magic costs him, but then so, earlier, has the exercise of it.

Mr Jacobi is now a superb verse-actor on four counts: tonal colour, lucidity, architecture, and characterisation. He builds a pleasing relationship with Alice Krige's Miranda (faint echoes of early Judi Dench), an angry and passionate one with his enemies; while with Mark Rylance's Ariel he forges a master-servant bond, funny at times, but woundingly painful when broken.

* * *

Medea (Riverside Studios) is a South African adaptation of a play by Grillparzer, Austrian, nineteenth-century. What can be discerned of the original is interesting, especially the detailed spitefulness with which Jason abandons Medea and she retaliates. Barney Simon's production, which must be aiming at simple stateliness, is however horribly dull, Yvonne Bryceland's Medea rarely more than competent, and the others rather less.
