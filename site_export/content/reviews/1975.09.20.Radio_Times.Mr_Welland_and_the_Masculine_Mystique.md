Mr Welland and the masculine mystique

It is a masculine world: beer, sweat and coachloads of rugger hearties. Its women live on the sidelines. But rugby makes separate demands on both players and their women, as a new drama series starting this week shows. Here Robert Cushman talks to playwright Colin Welland about the divided loyalties around the game

The Wild West Show
Friday 10.15 pm BBC2

'AS YOU KNOW we're a pretty motley crew. One of the great strengths of our game is that up here in the North, at least, it's not identified with any social stratum or class. If you want to play rugby, you're welcome.'

The speaker is the secretary of Cogdon rugby union club. Cogdon is a small town in the North, and imaginary. It has been created, though, as much through memory and observation as imagination. Television playwright Colin Welland played for just such a club in his home town of Newton-le-Willows in Lancashire -- played for it for 20 years from the time he was 16 until he was 36. He would even slip back for the odd game during the three years he was PC Graham in Z Cars, a formative experience.

Z Cars was a masculine world; women were on the sidelines, suffering and criticising. And this may also be the impression created initially by Welland's cycle of six plays about the Cogdon rugby players. It's even called The Wild West Show -- after the rugby song about being off to see the Wild West show.

Fear had been expressed that women may find the series unpalatably male -- or at any rate the first play, Good Lad Terry, which is concerned with the election of a new captain and focuses almost exclusively on the practice-field, the changing-room, the communal bath, the club bar. Welland himself is unworried. 'Let's woo 'em that way, let's get a bit of rile going.'

The clue to his approach is to be found in that speech of the secretary's and in his own reminiscences of playing for Newton. 'I can go back to that club now, I can meet fellers who I've known since I was eight.

'It's a complete cross-section. In our pack we had a schoolteacher hooking, two face-workers at the pit propping, in the second row would be a doctor and a corset-salesman.'

But all this camaraderie is based on a strictly limited view of the world, one which Welland himself has been compelled to discard. He got out of Newton when he was 26, probably just in time. (He went to Manchester to act in rep, played the lead in a Pinter play straight off, and discovered colloquial modern dialogue for the first time. The discovery was inspiring, and he began writing almost as soon as he had begun acting.) There are plenty of others who should have got out but didn't; and they appear in the plays. This image of a closed society which is part haven, part trap recurs in Welland's plays.

The role of the women in the plays steadily expands. In the electioneering of the first play they are, according to one of the stage-directions 'the female -- shut out from it all -- all the big decision-making -- handy for having it off with and helping serve food on special occasions'. But as the series proceeds they assume central roles in more complex situations. Mad Mick's wife has to choose between her distaste for rugby and the disintegration of her husband to whom the club means so much. In Oh Oh Antonio the captain's marriage reaches a crisis during an old friend's anniversary party. And in Melvyn's Marauders, a wife is faced with her portly husband's obsession to get back into trim for one more match.

While the men can escape reality at the rugby club, the women are left to cope at home. Welland is insistent that his rugby plays, unlike David Storey's The Changing Room (which he thinks belongs spiritually to the 50s), are happening now. 'The place isn't a sort of picturesque Lancashire pitch with a viaduct behind it and terraced houses -- it's got motorways round it and factories beside it. And all the attitudes and the problems that the lads are facing are today.'

He thinks in fact that both sexes are trapped by the system. But while the men can respond by trying to perpetuate their youth, the women have to retrace their steps because nothing individual is left for them to do. 'You go to Leeds on a Wednesday night, you go into a pub and it's packed with married women who have this night out. The system's easier on the men but it still sells them short.'

Welland has written about rugby before; in fact it was the subject of his first produced play Bangelstein's Boys which was such a success that London Weekend television entered for an Italia Prize. (It didn't win; 'they thought it was too disgusting.') The Wild West Show attempts to do justice to the material which was left unexplored in that early one-shot effort; it has the same producer, Kenith Trodd, and three of the six instalments have the same director, John Mackenzie. His career between these two works on rugby has been almost entirely, and very successfully, on television. There has been one stage play, Say Goodnight to Grandma, and as a theatre critic I would like more. He would, too (the theatre pays better for one thing), but he likes to keep his stories on the move ('I'm good at action and rows, I'm bad at philosophising') and he feels hampered by the restrictions of set and characters imposed on most stage playwrights. Certainly it's as a playwright that he wants to be remembered.

The Wild West Show is likely to exercise rather than exorcise his mixed feelings about his early years and the game that seems to symbolise them. 'The one thing you can't show is the thrill of actually playing. It's very much a communal game; it's probably very damaging to the maturing process but I've got great affection for it. I'd hate to see Newton Rugby Club go. But my wife, whenever she's really disgusted with me, will always say "Back we go to Newton rugby club." She sees me back again as one of those boozing chauvinist pigs.'
