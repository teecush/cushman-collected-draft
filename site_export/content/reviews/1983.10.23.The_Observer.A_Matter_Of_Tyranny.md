A matter of tyranny

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a new play by David Edgar.

ONE SCENE in David Edgar's Maydays (Barbican) really takes off. An exiled Russian dissident, named Lermontov, is being feted in London by an organisation supposedly dedicated to Liberty: actually a right-wing front.

As he listens to the speeches in his honour, he realises that he is being used. When he says his own piece, he abandons his notes, and haltingly informs his hosts that they are the mirror-image of the tyranny from which he has escaped. They may not, he says, be jailers, but they look like the people who employ jailers.

The force of the writing is here matched by that of the acting. Bob Peck, the preternaturally honest actor, plays Mr Edgar's honest man and does so, abandoning his recent steamrolling tactics, with superb delicacy. You can see his confusion, his shock, his passion; and I have never known the plight of a man fumbling for words in a foreign language to be presented with less fuss or less trickery.

This scene comes near the end of a long play. We have seen Lermontov doing time in a camp, having first met him in 1956, a Russian officer allowing an Hungarian rebel to escape. That rebel, now a sleek professional empire, appears as one of his sponsors in London.

So does an American, who makes a stomach-turning speech appropriating Russian sufferings as an argument for cracking down on protest in the West; the more disquieting since some 10 years earlier, Vietnam time, we have heard this man making a sorrowful appraisal of his own radical past and his children's radical present.

Also on hand, and I am sorry this has taken so long, are two disillusioned British intellectuals. One, Jeremy Crowther, was a post-war socialist, turned sour by Hungary and bilious by student sit-ins a decade later. The other, Martin Glass, was a child of the Sixties who went through all the protests, the parties, the splinter-groups, and came out the other side. He is the central character.

The play, like Mr Edgar's Nicholas Nickleby adaptation, is composed of huge building-blocks, artfully arranged. There is rarely any confusion of time, place or character. The sections balance; they support and illuminate one another, with the broadest contrast drawn between political struggle in Russia and political struggle here. ('We are both defectors,' says Lermontov to Glass in a line that Mr Peck makes the weightiest of the evening.) And yet the play is a crushing disappointment; the bitterest in ages. Within those blocks, there is nothing going on.

In Mary Barnes, which I now think his best play, Mr Edgar inserted a portrait of a generation into an exciting human story. In Destiny he hit on a wonderful untried subject - British Fascism - and surprised us with the sympathetic rigour of his approach; you could feel his mind expanding as he wrote it.

Maydays, being about his own side, should catch him on the raw, but it all seems very distant. He has written a play about people changing their minds, and - except in that one fine banquet scene - failed to show us the changes happening. Crowther's disaffection is taken for granted; Glass is given a good speech repudiating his comrades (a memorable line about Communism destroying the feelings that made people Communists in the first place), but he has made up his mind before he comes on stage.

Besides, Mr Edgar lets these comrades off rather easily; he has some good laughs at their infighting and their intransigence, but he spares them the hard questions. (What do they think about the IRA? Or the Baader-Meinhofs?) And though he mocks their reliance on slogan and cliché, he often resorts to instant metaphor himself. A scene between Glass and his mother is especially flat.

Behind it all - not just the play, but the advance publicity - is a large misconception: that what the world needs now is ipso facto a monster play about politics, on a subsidised stage. And even that is a red herring because much of Maydays is not about politics at all, but merely about politicking.

Politicking is a legitimate human activity. So is stamp-collecting, but I think that if the philately lobby wanted to get a play on at the Barbican, we would demand either that it was hilariously funny or that it offered some really searing insights into the human condition.

The irony is that Mr Edgar knows all this. He knows that most of his characters are playing private games, and he attacks them for it. A phrase of Trotsky's runs through the play: 'human dust,' the people who have to be sacrificed for the revolution. Which apparently includes those it is supposed to liberate. But he never brings any of them on stage. That confrontation, honestly handled, might be dynamite.

* * *

Nicholas Wright's The Custom of the Country (Pit) takes the title and situation of a Jacobean rarity (one by Fletcher and Massinger, thoughtfully revived by the Glasgow Citizens' earlier this year). Setting the intrigue up is a laborious job, and the wit rarely fizzes enough to justify it, but in David Jones's production a couple of scenes work. The best - the pitiful plight of a stud in a brothel for ladies - is the truest to the original.

Sara Kestelman does some bravura bulldozing as a gold magnate, and Josette Simon takes magical advantage of the author's trite invention: an African girl who, when words fail her, expresses herself in hypnotic snatches of native dance.

* * *

I have little space for The Cherry Orchard (Haymarket), but that may be as well. Lindsay Anderson's production passes swiftly but limply from one bit of acting to another. The best is Bill Fraser's Pishchik; he got a laugh with his first line, and at the end a restive audience was palpably held when he made his embarrassed farewell to the family.

Another set-piece - Lopahin's drunk speech - is finely done by Frank Finlay; indeed he is the best I have seen in this role, especially in his silent establishment of his crippling love for Ranevskaya. Frank Grimes also does Trofimov's long speech well. Some of the lower orders are played very crude, but, confusingly, not all. Joan Plowright, with a constant sob in her throat, made Madame seem so sorry for herself that my own sympathy froze.
