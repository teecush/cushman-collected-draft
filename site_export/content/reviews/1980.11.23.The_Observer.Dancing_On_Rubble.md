Dancing on rubble

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

AN-HISTORICAL play is being mounted, amid threats of censorship and closure. The objections centre around scenes of rape and mutilation; this is an invasion drama and the Celts are getting it.

We are not talking about The Romans in Britain. All this happens in The Irish Play, whose author, Ron Hutchinson, can hardly have foreseen how topical his piece would turn out to be. It has some resemblance to an earlier Howard Brenton work, The Churchill Play, with which it shares a director, Barry Kyle, and a theatre, the Warehouse. It is the most joyous new work I remember seeing at this address, and for its author — previously associated with the kind of verbal booze-ups he himself describes as "Irishry" — a great jump forward.

We are in an Irish club in Coventry where, for one night only, there is to be a break in the usual round of bingo and squawky country-and-western bands composed of the entertainment secretary's relatives. The members, or some of them, are presenting a home-brewed play about the twelfth-century Norman incursions which, due to betrayals and rivalries among the natives, set the tone for the ensuing 800 years of Irish history.

The young author's length of hair and the lady director's lack of bra arouse suspicion in the predominantly middle-aged breasts of the club committee, as does the fact that the club president — shortly up for re-election — favours the play and is actually appearing in it as Henry II and most of his barons.

Personal ambitions criss-cross with political protestations, patriotism, as Johnson should have said, being the first refuge of the expatriate. The compulsion to talk about Ireland while living in England is one that Mr Hutchinson shares with his characters. We are always being told (much to our surprise) that not enough plays are being written about Ireland, meaning Ulster and meaning, as a rule, a prescribed political viewpoint. The undying fatuity that a good play can be written about an Issue (and had damn well better be, or our authors will have to stay in and write it on the black-board) must bear hard upon an honest playwright; Mr Hutchinson dramatises the pressures in the committee scene that is the heart of the evening.

At one end of the table is the Socialist demanding that the oppressed peasants of the twelfth century be brought to recognise the advantages of the nationalisation of the means of production, distribution and exchange; at the other (slightly caricatured) the representative of the women's section declaring for the proprieties: "the women will have rape at a pinch but not language"; between them ("I won't take second place to anyone where republican fervour's involved — historically speaking of course") various shades of hypocrisy, sanctimony and self-interest.

Mr Hutchinson's structure is probably more cumbrous than he needs to make his points, and the motives of the increasingly fanatical president remain obscure. Perhaps he's just stage-struck. But the irruption into the clubmen's quarrels of rioting actors in medieval costume brilliantly dramatises the persistence of the past in the present; and when at the end the play-within-a-play takes over the stage-upon-a-stage (as in The Churchill Play and on much the same set), becomes as sombre as farcical.

Factions divide, the costume-trunk is impounded, bully-boys are brought in, the show goes on and the house comes down. There is a clutch of good performances from John Cowley's resident president to the little girl who, after asking, "Is it my turn now?", ends the play by dancing among the rubble, while accompanying herself on the pipe: a superb final image. Mr Hutchinson has written, literally, a play-and-a-half.

* * *

Seamus Finnegan's Act of Union (playing lunchtimes at the Soho Poly and transferring shortly to Action Space, Chenies Street) is the more approved kind of Irish play, set in Belfast. Its decent aim, well achieved, is to tell us what life is like there, emphasising the contradictions while acknowledging (in the words of Valerie Lilley's tart raconteuse housewife) "youse have a hard time understanding the easy bits."

* * *

Lunchtimes at the King's Head is a Schnitzler double-bill: a typical piece of bittersweet badinage called New Year's Eve, and a short story rendered by Stephen Oliver into a mini-opera, A Man of Feeling, with a lovely ironic curl (rather than twist) in its tail. Mr Oliver distinguishes himself as translator, librettist and composer (funny and astringent), Nichola McAuliffe and Oz Clarke as actors and singers. The opera, though not the play, is to go into the evening bill with Coward's Shadow Play next month.

* * *

The Biograph Girl (Phoenix) is Mary Pickford, though, she being dead and unavailable for interview, the publicity has focused on Lillian Gish. If this is confusing so is the show. Miss Pickford (who spends most of the time working for Paramount, not Biograph) shares stage-time with Miss Gish and the implacably visionary D. W. Griffith. Indeed they get more time than she does; or maybe it seems more. Sheila White's Mary is the best thing of the evening; while everyone else is stuffy and noble, she alone has bubble and edge. She gets one of those "Hello Dolly" numbers (which A Chorus Line should have killed) in which she smiles stage-centre while the male cast walk round her telling the world how wonderful she is.

In this context she deserves it, but even she gets murdered by the show. End of Act One she does a satirical number about her wholesome image, which she hates. Then she strips off curls and white frock, smiles wickedly, says "right, it's time to start living that dream" and struts into the wings as preparation for a stunning bad-girl number. Only there isn't one; she doesn't come back. Showbiz logic is denied, and dramatic logic never has a chance. All the show knows is that silents sure were a glamorous era and, hell, there must have been some tragedy, because the talkies came in and ended a lot of careers.

This last thought prompts a pleasantly plaintive trio for three fading stars. (One of them, Mack Sennett, was actually a director by this time, but the show, which models him on Donald O'Connor role in Singin' in the Rain, isn't bothered.) Earlier the composer, David Heneker, has furnished some sprightly enough tunes. But he has also thrown in a tawdry rock (or perhaps just rocky) golddiggers number, a ludicrously earnest chorale sung on the set of Intolerance and called Beyond Babel (given the straight-chinned, eyes-front treatment of Griffith and Company) and a Thirties torcher, school of These Foolish Things, in which D. W. unwisely attempts an exhaustive analysis of his decline in technical terms ("as we call it in the trade, we've reached the gentle fade").

Quality apart, these musical anachronisms have no place in a show which elsewhere plugs its period in a Charlestoning routine called Nineteen Twenty Five. Choreographer Irving Davies meanwhile knows two ways of staging a number. In one the whole company (12) comes downstage, stands sideways on, and does the train sequence, which involves gyrating while clutching the bottom of the person ahead. Soloists come downstage, face full front, sing and never move. After the British musicals that aimed insultingly low (Barnardo) or misguidedly high (Colette), we have — less awful but more painful — the one that sits and decomposes in the middle.
