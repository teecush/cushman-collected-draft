Perils of Eng. Lit?

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Educating Rita, A Quick Deco and Rutherford and Son.

TREADING eagerly in the upwardly mobile footsteps of Shaw's Eliza Doolittle and Arnold Wesker's Beatie Bryant comes the heroine of Willy Russell's Educating Rita (Warehouse), whose real name is Susan.

She calls herself Rita after the author, or is it the heroine, of a novel she has been impressed by: a circumstance which greatly irritates her English teacher. What turns him off is not her taste in literature, but the affectation of such an action and the suggestibility it implies. He is a weary don, supervising an Open University course to earn some extra gelt to get drunk on. She is a hairdresser's assistant in her mid-twenties, married without children, who left school soon bored and now doubts the existence of the working-class culture she keeps hearing about, and which seems to her to consist of endless drinking sessions and soulless mating sessions of the approved variety. Her enthusiasm for literature astounds her instructor (he doesn't come across much of that in his walk of life) and he tries to get rid of her. Stuck with her, he tries to teach her how to read books in the manner approved by examiners. He counsels her against answering questions according to the dictates of simple enthusiasm. 'Is your opinion worth the radio?' Give reasons, he tells her, and quote relevant authorities.

The play stays, ably and, on the principle of leaving the best till last, accurately with Rita. Miss Walters is unmatched among young actresses at suggesting a mind chafing against restraints, and she has been developing, in addition to her native canniness, a joyously exuberant comic style. (Her performance in Good Fun at Sheffield was a marvellous piece of tight-rope-walking.) In this play, with due help from her author, she gathers more laughs in an evening than have been heard in the entire previous history of the Warehouse. But she is also moving, and--again with Mr Russell's assistance--she brings alive, through narrative, the whole history of her disintegrating marriage to a resentful husband: something not necessarily to be regretted, but still painful to go through. She might develop in a lower register, though.

Her partner in this duet is another matter. The beginning of one scene catches him reading Jake's Thing and that probably places him. Once a poet, but all ambition and even interest long gone--and Mr Russell has added no new ingredients. Mark Kingston's performance is what I would call fluffy: pleasant, never actually wrong, but lacking in bite.

The play may lack the breadth of Roots, but in one respect it goes beyond it. It shows us the learning process, and makes us both anxious and involved. In Mr Wesker's play it had all happened, apart from the final traumatic piece of self-education, before the curtain went up. So Mr Russell burrows and questions. We were left in no doubt that Beatie Bryant was pursuing the right values; it was merely her mentor who let her down. Mr Russell doesn't fall for trendy philistinism; as a writer he values writing. But he assaults the ends for which it is taught; when Rita learns to give the right answers something of her individuality has been lost.

Actually I think the emphasis on exam-technique is exaggerated, but much of it is still alarmingly true. In literary criticism, Rita is informed, is an objective science, and she should not muddy it with such irrelevant considerations as whether or not she actually enjoys a book--all of which chills the blood considerably. Sent off to summer school, she returns able to make all the right noises about Blake, though she makes them, admittedly, with some dash.

Mr Russell hasn't shaped this material very decisively, but he has at least recognised that it exists. He has structured the play as a series of sketches with blackout endings: not very neatly handled in Mike Ockrent's production, which leaves scenes and actors hanging, but building up into a shaggy-teacher story, at the end. By prevailing standards, this is an exceptionally lively evening.

* * *

Of all the authors who most puzzles Rita is Forster; she has problems, as who wouldn't, with souls, and one admonitory lament begins, 'My son, I gave you the whole of myself jack'; it seemed to sum up the Genius of A Quick Deco (Chalk Farm). Chalk-striped suits and rimless glasses for the men, mandatory black dress for the lady, potted greenery for the set: that's the ambience. Nothing brassy; John Golder and John Harle, the two actor-musicians who devised it, are as discreet in song, speech and movement as a pair of Edwardian shadows.

When Duke Ellington's Mood Indigo, usually rendered as a lush lullaby, was rendered on a small set of flutes, with spare, court-romanticism, its despairing sweetness seemed to say--you could call the other side of it--Brecht and Weill. Later might be, Brecht and Eisler.

In fact this selection of the words and music of Noel Coward seldom strays far from fragmented revue form, is the perfect heir for actors who enjoy lecturing audiences. If I am to be bullied I think I would rather have it done by a live author.

* * *

Coward brings me to Mrs Worthington's Daughters about whom, on the evidence available at the Theatre Upstairs, he had the right idea. The touring ladies--and gentlemen--who make up the company travelling under that name will doubtless improve with age, but at present they are lamentably under-powered for Rutherford and Son, the harmless Edwardian drama of domestic exploitation (by Githa Sowerby, a woman; and so what?) they are supposed to be resuscitating. They sit around looking tired, and seldom raise their voices above a moan. The oppressed--on this showing as on many others--are not the kind of people you want to spend your time with. The oppressors aren't much fun either.
