Committing high treason

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Stratford.

WALLACE SHAWN is a stocky dishevelled mole of a New York playwright best known here for the erotic farce A Thought in Three Parts. He also acts with - to judge from a performance in Machiavelli's Mandrake a couple of seasons ago - more enthusiasm than technique.

By his own account Mr Shawn took to acting to pay his bills. His own account is being rendered (at the Theatre Upstairs, until Wednesday) in an entertainment entitled My Dinner with Andre. Andre is Andre Gregory, once a famous off-Broadway director, but absent from the scene since the early 1970s. Like Mr Shawn he appears here as himself. The action, after some scene-setting remarks by Mr Shawn, consists of a restaurant conversation between the two.

Mr Shawn disarmingly admits that he was dreading the encounter, but planned to survive it by pumping Mr Gregory with questions and listening to the answers. As a performer, Mr Gregory combines maximum relaxation with minimum projection and the first half of the evening, before one adjusts to his level, is a bit of a trial. Mr Gregory tells of working on large-scale improvisations with Jerzy Grotowski in the Polish forests, of travelling to Tibet: experiences that have focused and confirmed his disillusion with America and with the theatre. He would like to submit audiences to experiences of similar intensity in the theatre, but he sees no way.

And here the evening becomes unusually exciting. Mr Shawn is provoked into reply, and comes through with shy, spluttering eloquence. Mr Gregory has apparently been preaching a sort of spiritual quiescence, somewhat intimidating Mr Shawn who says, "I'm not adequate just to be a human being"; he needs, he says, goals and actions. And small ones will do; as he says, it isn't practical to take everyone to Everest. "Why not enjoy the details?" he asks; getting up in the morning, being with your wife, having a cup of coffee.

He goes on to preach the highest theatrical treason and to reveal that he enjoys not only writing plays, but getting the reviews and reading about plays "and things about things about plays." He celebrates in fact all the trivia that theatrical gurus of every stamp have instructed us to despise. Generations of guilt fall away. I felt like cheering.

If we can stop worrying about the grandiose things power-hungry theatricals would like their shows to do to people, we can maybe get down to appreciating the smaller things it can do for them. (Audiences, you may notice, are the factor left out of nearly every theatrical manifesto.) I don't want to imply that Mr Gregory, especially when he starts speaking up to reply to Mr Shawn, is a mere bully - he's actually very charming - or that there is no common ground between the two of them. Mr Shawn has his full measure of unease, and Mr Gregory his of compassion. And he makes a mark; Mr Shawn scampers home to tell his wife every detail of their conversation. His mind has not been changed, but it has been coloured. Which is all that a play can do; and what this one does far better than most.

* * *

Edward Bond in The Guardian last week was attacking James Fenton of The Sunday Times for not liking The Romans in Britain. He obviously felt that more than Howard Brenton's play was at stake; he wanted reviewers to pick up sides, preferably his own. We are asked to supply blanket approval of one stream of work (Mr Bond differs from previous scourges of the Press in wanting rave reviews for his friends as well as himself) and blanket disapproval of another.

Academic critics may be able to oblige with this kind of partisanship, but regular reviewers cannot. We see too much, and are confounded by finding merits and faults in unsuspected quarters. We may start radical or reactionary, but we invariably end up liberal. I liked The Romans in Britain and I have liked most of Mr Bond's plays, but I have probably damned myself by liking Stoppard, Ayckbourn, Gray and Frayn as well.

Audiences tend to be as disobligingly catholic as reviewers; the all-or-nothing response craved by theatre people is rarely an honest possibility. A lady with a personal stake in the new Stratford productions of Richard II and Richard III told me during an interval that she wanted the shows to be either loved or hated. She's an old and dear friend and I hate to disoblige her, but I have to confess that though temperamentally out of tune with Terry Hands's hectically operatic approach, I thought these examples of it pretty good.

Mr Hands is an avowed enemy of realism and he can justifiably assert that these two early Shakespeare histories are stylised pieces, full of emblematic characters and elaborate verbal tropes. But I suspect that the war between realism and stylisation is a phony one. There never was a wholly realistic play or production, never will or can be. Complete stylisation is a possibility, but is unlikely.

In practice the two modes always co-exist, and if a text tilts in one direction it is the director's job to restore the balance. (I expect to contradict myself on this point, and rapidly.) For example: in the Flint Castle scene of Richard II, after Alan Howard as Richard has expressed his willingness to come down and surrender to Bolingbroke in the base court, he executes a highly unrealistic and acrobatically impressive walk down the wall of Farrah's burnished-copper set. This should be a stunning image, but though arresting it doesn't work because Richard has already made the point verbally and at formal length. What we need here is a nifty prosaic exit on one level and entrance on another.

Similarly Richard III's hoodwinking of the Lord Mayor of London is as written a pretty crude affair: it may be that instead of staging it as an outsize Punch and Judy show it might be better to opt for a sober treatment that might add a sense of Realpolitik.

Both productions are at their best in the scenes that follow these central episodes. In Richard II Mr Howard abandons the stinging and/or mournful approach most actors bring to the deposition. He is almost playful, even in comparing himself to Christ, and by so much the more disconcerting; he almost embarrasses Bolingbroke on to the throne, and it is plain that David Suchet's usurper (hitherto a plain and rather likeable opportunist) will never get over it. Mr Howard is almost coy when he looks at himself in the glass: over-powering when he breaks it to pieces. This may be the motif that links the two plays: R2 loves his own image, R3 hates it.

Mr Howard's Crookback goes heavily for deformity, taking the first soliloquy rewardingly at face value. His prize scene is the coronation, when he must heave himself painfully up the steps, and sits on them to take counsel from a diminutive page on the Princes' murder. He makes more than any actor I have seen of Richard's nightmare, prompted by a posse of ghosts who mark the climax of Mr Hands's boldly helter-skelter production. (The spirits of Rivers, Vaughan and Grey even get away with playing ring-a-ring-a-wars-of-the-roses.) The play also has the best of Stratford's support with Derek Godfrey a ruefully witty Buckingham, with an excellently charted relationship to Richard, Mr Suchet a helplessly sick king, Richard Pasco a resounding Clarence, Domini Blythe, Barbara Leigh-Hunt and Sinead Cusack a powerful pack of queens and Joe Melia, no less, turning unexpectedly up as Second Murderer.

* * *

Peter Buckman's All Together Now (Greenwich) finds a new source of drama: the tribulations and evolution of a local brass band. It is their actual music that provides the excitement, comic and dramatic as well as musical. The talk bits are routinely competent group-portraiture; none of the actors puts down roots in Peter Dews's production.
