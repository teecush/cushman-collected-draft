Can't Help Loving You

I don't know if I could cope with a masterpiece at lunchtime. Try me. In the meantime I have descended into Inter-Action's Queensway basement for a forgettable hour of harmless fun. Two workshop improvisations have been knocked up by author-director Neil Hornick into a pair of one-act plays.

*Henry* introduces us to a quintet of conscientious fun-lovers who discover that one of their number is missing and spend the bulk of the play reacting to the fact. A predictable gamut is run, developing from, roughly, “Henry was the life and soul of the party, how are we going to get along without him?” to “Henry was always the one who spoiled everything, we'd never take him back.” A superficial view might find this obvious and heavy-handed. A superficial view would, on the whole, be correct, but there should be some acknowledgement of the mechanical skill involved. The route is quite persuasively charted, and an impressive number of changes are rung on the simple phrase “I am having a good time”. I didn't have a bad one myself; but I was glad when it stopped. Most of the cast acted loyally. Sandy Macdonald acted well.

I can't be the only person to whom computer dating has seemed for some time a likely subject for a comedy. I know I can't because of Mr Hornick's *Can't Help Loving You*. In approved comic fashion this takes a potentially funny situation just one step beyond reality. The computer-matched couple arrive for their first date armed with tape-recorders which assume the burden of conversation for them, and are able to sustain it long after the humans have fled the scene and each other. Of course it's amusing to hear people talking in the style of questionnaires (“I am a white male Caucasian between twenty-five and thirty years old”) but it's only one joke. It is also tough on the actors; deprived of dialogue by the march of science they could only hop around trying vainly to achieve a convincing variety of reactions to a single situation. (The first play proved how much easier this is when you have something to say.) What we have here (why should I knock myself out trying to be original?) is an over-extended revue sketch. The comedy remains to be written.
