Austrian attitudes

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

THIS week has brought us two German, or at any rate Germanic, works, sponsored in missionary mood by the Goethe Institute. I wish the Institute would get its facts right: Magic Afternoon (Bush) is not the second but the third of Wolfgang Bauer's plays to be seen over here, and Schnitzler's Anatol (Open Space) has certainly not had to wait till now for its British premiere. Both no doubt are enterprising choices but the area is not quite as unknown as press releases and programmes would have us believe.

Magic Afternoon will actually seem comfortingly familiar to anyone who attended the same author's Ghosts at Hampstead last year. It is both tighter and shorter than its predecessor (two blessings) but it occupies the same territory, and is similarly concerned with violent delights which have, but naturally, violent ends. Once again the characters are young layabouts; I am sorry to sound censorious but I could hardly disapprove of them more than their author does and laying about is undeniably what, most of the time, they do.

In Herb Greer's translation and Barbara Markham's production they have been pretty well acclimatised to London. In a dank bed-sit, in the low days of 1967, a would-be writer keeps a discreet distance from his typewriter ("everything," he admits, "is one big lazy excuse") while his Swedish girl-friend rows with him, jeers at him, and asks to be taken out. Since the evening paper advertises no suitable movie, she seems doomed to stay in. From time to time they put on a record ("Concrete and Clay," "In the Midnight Hour") and make love. These are not exactly joyful encounters; either the partners are mouthing the lyrics, eyes closed and more or less unconscious, or they are pummelling each other, escalating rapidly from playfulness to brutality. She gets hurt. Another couple take possession of the room and there is a repeat performance, ending in a broken nose. The play finishes in outright mayhem.

The build is logical, the rhythms fine, the ambience recognisable and the characters--as performed by Gregory Floy, Katja Kersten, Jack Galloway and Holly Wilson--broadly but firmly drawn. They seem out of key only when the text gets them playing word-games and piling on literary references and hinting that they are aware of their own status as dramatic fictions. Here it seems that a portentous schematic original has been--in two senses--naturalised, and the joins show. Beyond this there is a grinding quality about the writing: evidence goes on accumulating long after the verdict has been reached. Perhaps the author is exercising his own past, but why, at this date and length, pick on 1967? The characters are notably apolitical, and perhaps the implication is that we are in at the death of an unthinking Bohemianism which the events of the following year were to smash and awaken forever. I only said perhaps, and having offered the diagnosis I have to dissociate myself from it; Bauer's people are still around, no more lovable even when politicised.

The Goethe Institutionals are no narrow chauvinists; Wolfgang Bauer is Austrian while Schnitzler, as is well known, invented Vienna. His silvery reputation abroad has suffered from an act of self-censorship. He wrote a masterpiece--the parade of couplings generally known as La Ronde--which earned him the unwanted reputation of a purveyor of erotica; he therefore forbade the play to be performed while its copyright endured.

Anatol was his first play; it too is an assembly of sketches and it shows why the comparative explicitness of the later piece became necessary. Anatol is a roue whose prowess, from what we see and what we hear discussed, seems to consist more in luring women to the supper-table than into bed. Central European theatre is the only theatre that knows how to eat, and this play duly presents a set-piece in a restaurant, where Anatol's love (who is leaving him) tucks gluttonously into her last meal at his expense.

All his roses exhibit some variety of thorn; they are too insensitive, or too predatory, or too forgetful, or too protective of their reputations. There are six of them in the present version (a lively one, by Frank Marcus) and all are played by Prunella Scales. She distinguishes them magnificently, the brisk ingenue from the fastidious bourgeoise, the masterful, breathily enchanting circus-rider from the delicately emotional wife, trying to focus and hold on to some finer feeling. Only once does she resort to caricature in an effort to find a new character; but the variables in the performance are ultimately less important than its common factor, which is vitality. The actress is continuously alight.

Versatility might, paradoxically, benefit Anatol himself. There is one scene whose disillusion recalls that of The Misanthrope but Anatol is no Alceste; he reacts not with anger but with wry acceptance. Derek Godfrey does this beautifully but he meets his other rebuffs in much the same spirit. He is adept at exposing the hopeful vanity of youth peering through the mask of worldly middle age. But Anatol, according to Schnitzler's stage directions, was a young man; the melancholy is that for all his proclaimed ideals, he is an ageing lecher in spirit.
