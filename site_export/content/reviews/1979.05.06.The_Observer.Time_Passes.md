Time passes

SOMERSET MAUGHAM'S penultimate play begins with tea on the lawn and ends with tea in the drawing-room. During the first repast tennis is transpiring off stage; during the second madness is happening on it.

For Services Rendered (Lyttelton) is an unmasking play, in the grand tradition of English bourgeois comedy. The Ardsleys are a country solicitor's family who discover, during the autumn of 1932, that the world is slipping away from them. It is Mrs Ardsley who epitomises their plight; she finds she is dying. She doesn't seem to mind very much. "I'm not at home in this world of today. I'm pre-war."

All the characters are inclined to blame the war (now 14 years in the past) for their sufferings, and Maugham seems to agree with them. At all events he gives a key denunciatory speech to Sydney, the son of the house, blinded in action. A friend of the family, Collie, is an ex-officer trying and failing to make a civilian living as a garage owner; financial ruin leads him to suicide.

The Ardsleys have three daughters. Eva, the eldest, has been cast as nurse and martyr, looking after Sydney, and is sick of the role; she sees Collie (who gives her no encouragement) as her last chance of marriage and his death sends her round the bend. The second, Ethel, has made a miserable marriage to a drunken farmer who had the bad taste to do well out of the war and is now engaged in trying to seduce the youngest girl, Lois. He only succeeds in scaring her--temporarily at least--off an equally married but slightly less disreputable suitor whom she doesn't love. What he did in and since the great war is not made clear but--since he refuses to bale Collie out but can squander money on a necklace for Lois--it was presumably something shameful.

It is difficult to trace all the characters' troubles to the battlefield; where the three sisters are concerned you have to resort to that old standby, the shortage of available men. Maugham's polemical bursts are powerful enough, but they obscure the play's real perspective. Its villain is not war but time; the pivotal roles are the mother embracing death and Lois grabbing for life. (She fears the subtle burden of looking less than her age; nobody can believe that she has cause to worry.)

These shifts of focus create a jolting effect in the narrative which is curiously at odds with the lapidary ease of the style; but if Maugham can sometimes be confused over cause and effect, he has a lethal eye and ear for actual process. There is little communication between his characters and less consideration; they are a set of gleaming monsters.

The cankered comedy of manners is a genre at which the National Theatre has always excelled; and Michael Rudman's production is the most satisfying seen on the South Bank since The Madras House. Witty and moving, it is also rock-hard, its spirit enshrined in the dining-room of the play's central act which Carl Toms (aided by Gerry Jenkinson's ice-cold lighting) has rendered as a cross between barrack and counting-house.

Here, in Leslie Sands's iron-clad performance, Ardsley pere confronts the erring Collie and effectively condemns him. It is notable by the way that this paterfamilias hardly addresses his wife throughout the play and that she ignores him. Jean Anderson establishes a woman who finds her comfort in rigidity and cannot survive its passing as brilliantly as Elizabeth Romilly shows spontaneity and calculation at war in Lois. Robin Bailey reaffirms the pleasures of elegance; Harold Innocent, happily released from his usual joke-roles, is a piercingly mordant Sydney; and Peter Jeffrey and Phyllida Law make a funny, pathetic and dangerous pair of intruders.

The toughest role is Alison Fiske's as Eva; she is disconcertingly strident, presumably in preparation for her mad-scene which--it must be admitted--is crudely written stuff. Mr Ardsley's complaint-making, too, now raises horse-laughs rather than the intended shudders. It is in brisk meanness and casual lacerations that the play excels.

* * *

Bent (Royal Court)--a new play by an American author, Martin Sherman--is set only a couple of years later. We are in Germany in 1934; homosexuals, in the wake of the Night of the Long Knives, are being sent to concentration camps. The play centres on one who has a talent for surviving at the expense of friends and lovers. In Dachau he manages to exchange the homosexual's pink triangle for the yellow star of the Jew, which, in those early days, is slightly preferable. (There's a sick joke for you.) Also in Dachau he finds the love of his life and, true to form, is the indirect cause of his death. Finally donning his triangle he impales himself on the electric fence. This is the "coming out" play to end them all.

It's also a (strictly verbal) celebration of homosexual love; and I suppose there's a point in setting it in the extremest circumstances. With both fascism and sexual bigotry looming again, the play may even have value as a cautionary tale. But, inevitably, the setting overwhelms the play. Mr Sherman is reasonably sparing with the horrors, but he can of course unleash them at any time and produce a guaranteed (and therefore devalued) effect. And, by the opposite token, any play in which two camp inmates are left alone long enough to form a relationship, provokes incredulity: concentration-camp drama may be a contradiction in terms.

The second half consists almost entirely of a duologue while carrying stones, and Mr Sherman shows considerable skill in keeping it going. But in relation to what is supposed to be happening around, it seems mawkish--as, by any standards, does the end. If the hero had affirmed his identity, and gone on living, that would have been defiance. Ian McKellen sparkles in the character's early insolence and frightens in his rodent tenacity. His admirable partner, Tom Bell, I trust found the play less exploitative than Holocaust. I didn't.

* * *

The French Have A Song For It (Piccadilly) also takes on great events and falls short of them. In this compilation of chansons, the revolutionary section seems self-conscious; and Peter Reeves's singing of Let It Rock, Coco has lost the blazing ferocity of its simpler staging at the King's Head. But the evening is for the most part a joyful one. There are several funny songs delightfully hiccupped by Amanda Barrie; and above all there is the revelation of Helen Shapiro, who at 15 was the best pop singer this country has ever produced and is now nearly great. Her tone is as warm and deep as ever, her demeanour magnetically relaxed and her phrasing superb, whether singing a shrugging song of loneliness or, sable-clad, sending up My Man as rotten as it has long deserved. Most songs are sung in Mr Reeves's conscientious translations, but we also get to hear the Gallic original of My Way. It doesn't scan in French either.
