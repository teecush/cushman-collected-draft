A cumquat from Tokyo

EDINBURGH FESTIVAL

ROBERT CUSHMAN on companies from Japan, America and Italy.

FOR the first time in over 30 years there is no play at the Assembly Hall. The drama section of the Edinburgh Festival has contracted in size, and what remains is interesting and -- what should please the internationalists -- every last bit of it is foreign.

More foreign than Sankai Juku they do not come. People may carry on like this all the time in Tokyo, but the charge this Japanese dance group brought to Edinburgh was extraordinary. Aghast city fathers glimpsed them from their windows, falling to the ground upside down in very slow motion. At a new official venue in the Assembly Rooms (you lose a hall, you gain a room) they presented Kinkan Shonen, translated as 'The Cumquat Seed.'

I could make nothing of title or translation, but I was entranced -- literally -- by much of the performance, due at Sadler's Wells in London in October. The director-star, Ushio Amagatsu, began by executing a backward fall that looked very much like death; like most of the evening's movement, it supported the programme's claims for the unique subtleness of Japanese hips. The body fell on to a dust-strewn stage.

I thought, as Westerners are prone to do, of Hiroshima; and I thought of it again, much later, when a dwarf appeared: a smiling, bald, swaying midget in a smock. The feet were visible, but no hands and indeed no arms; they might only have been stumps hidden in the folds. The smile looked beneficent, but seemed menacing; the figure made a long progress towards the audience, who sat terrified at what might be revealed. There was bagpipe-music playing -- which I took as an ironic tribute to the host-city -- and the actor seemed to be sobbing. Then the figure retreated to a small ledge, which it tried, unavailingly, to scale; the performer was demonstrating horrifying agility. Then, at last, unbelievably, the figure straightened up and was our star again, full size.

If the programme was to be believed, I had got it all wrong; we were being offered 'the dream of a young boy wondering about the origin of life' and I can just about see how that fits. There is a supporting cast of four, whose heads are shaven and whose faces are often obscured by putty masks; they are much given to undulation. I found their effectiveness varied with the music. Sometimes it was pounding, characterless rock: the kind of music you can't listen to -- I can't anyway -- but which gets you focusing on the actors in sheer self-defence. When it changed to jazz, and to a recognisable tune (Miles Davis's 'All Blues') the spell was broken; one saluted an amazing versatility of limb, but we were in the self-regarding world of modern sculpture: very arty. The last theme was the 'Going Home' bit from the New World Symphony; whether the homewardness or the new-worldliness was the point, I couldn't tell; certainly the peace was shattering.

Co-starring is a peacock, which gets fondled as if about to be made love to or to get its neck broken. But it survives, and is granted its own pool of light, in which it bathes for a while before -- at my visit -- walking off. I have irreverent memories of an off-Broadway revue called 'Scrambled Feet,' whose title-number was about the inadvisability of ever sharing a stage with a duck. For the most part Kinkan Shonen is traumatising theatre; by far the finest performance art -- if that's what it is -- that I have seen.

* * *

From the Drama Centre at Harvard (headmaster, Robert Brustein) came the American Repertory Theatre in Sganarelle (Lyceum): a programme of four Moliere farces, originally staged at Yale when the director, Andrei Serban, took against Bulgakov's Moliere play, currently visible at Stratford.

Sganarelle was the name Moliere always bestowed on his leading clown-characters. They are not the same character, and were played here by four separate actors. The best was the first, a young harlequin with an air of cheeky innocence, named Thomas Darrah, who in 'The Flying Doctor' appeared as his own imaginary twin, delabouring himself like a one-man Punch and Judy. He was the centrepiece of a fast, airy production that raised spirits and hopes. Slowly they were dashed, as the same slapstick techniques were applied to dissimilar plays. In 'The Forced Marriage' a series of intellectual quacks were lamely parodied as American gurus of 15 years back. In 'The Imaginary Cuckold' the zippy couplets of Albert Bermel's translation were gulped into obscurity, while the actors rushed around disguising the plot. Charm returned with 'A Dumb Show' (based on 'The Doctor In Spite of Himself'), in which the actors spoke jibberish, whose meaning was revealed to us in sub-titles. This was cute; as, potentially, was much of the evening. Maybe I would have surrendered to it more easily if the programme had not described the show's use of traditional shtick as an 'attempt to rediscover the wellsprings of farce itself.' Maybe an academic theatre has to go on like this, but, as a professional company, the ART should just shut up and get on with it.

There was more manifesto theatre from the Cooperativa Teatromusica of Rome, which presented L'Olympiade (Lyceum), described as an opera without music. A 1733 libretto by Metastasio was performed without a note of music (there were, apparently, some 30 settings for the production to reject). We began with silence; this, presumably, was the overture. Then on came Irene Worth to tell us the story. A complicated tangle of lost children and courtly love, like 'Oedipus' crossed with 'The Two Gentlemen of Verona.' Even Italians find it hard to follow, said Miss Worth, who seemed to think the whole thing a bit of a giggle. The audience tended to agree (opera without music, just listen to those words) was, after all, a standby of intimate revue, and the rhythmic repetition of key-phrases is reliably risible. Sandro Sequi's production had some impressive painterly groupings, but it all made for a faint experience.

* * *

Critics are easily jaded by the Festival fringe; we forget how exciting an experience it is for everyone else. The multiplicity that defeats us turns them on. The most substantial show I saw was Men Should Weep (Moray House), a revival by the Scottish 7:84 Company of a Glasgow Unity Theatre play written just after the war, but set in the Thirties. It is directed by Giles Havergal, and is a fascinating application of the Glasgow Citizens' style to mainstream naturalism.

The set is done in grey sheets; supporting characters become a chorus; Ena Lamont Stewart's Gorbals drama becomes a tenement symphony. The treatment does some harm to the play's flow, but enormously enhances its peak moments, of which there are hundreds. The substance -- resilient suffering women, sexist men -- recalls Juno and the Paycock, and, though that play is funnier, this one is more emotionally truthful. Elizabeth MacLennan heads an enormously good cast.

A Traverse late-night show, Woza Albert!, begins with a really swinging band: trombone, trumpet, guitar and drums are prominent. But there are only two musicians, and they have no instruments. They are two black South African actors, Percy Mtwa and Mbongeni Ngema, who deploy a great range of vocal and physical effects with terrific energy and precision. Their main business is a parable. The Messiah arrives in South Africa and finds himself en route to a white supremacist Calvary. The performers exude all our sympathies, much of our horror, a lot of our admiration; but the script's exploration of its basic idea is dogged rather than inspired.
