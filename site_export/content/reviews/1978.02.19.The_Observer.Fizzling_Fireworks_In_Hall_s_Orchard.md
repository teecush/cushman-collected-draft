Fizzling fireworks in Hall's Orchard

WHATEVER strictures may be made on the lack of company continuity at the National Theatre, I have to report that certain traditions are becoming established.

Take last week's openings. The Cherry Orchard (Olivier) is the second play in which Albert Finney (Lopakhin) has told Susan Fleetwood (Varya) to get her to a nunnery; the difference is that this time she looks like complying. Arnold Wesker's Love Letters on Blue Paper (Cottesloe) gives us Michael Gough in bed; what with Watch It Come Down and Bedroom Farce, he must have played more scenes in pyjamas than any living actor not employed by Paul Raymond.

The programme to The Cherry Orchard quotes some of Chekhov's objections to the realistic production methods favoured by his original Russian interpreters. In the author's teeth I would maintain that much of the appeal of his plays, as of the greatest Russian novels, is in their inclusiveness. Before it, as Eric Bentley once noted, all conventional ideas of genre simply break down; they are too small. When the Moscow Arts Theatre first came to London, Kenneth Tynan, trying excitedly to define its quality, fell back on what I would call a brilliant surrender: 'How these actors exist -- roundly and egocentrically exist.'

A production that cannot summon this force of personality may well achieve success by going to the other extreme, as Peter Gill did last month at Riverside Studios: cultivating extreme lucidity in spartan surroundings. Peter Hall's version at the National occupies a halfway house. John Bury's sets, on a squared-off Olivier platform, are almost realistic; the performances almost fill out the play. But that 'almost' gives a great deal away.

It starts splendidly, governed by Mr Finney, bigger-chested and bigger-bellied than ever before, and making credible both Lopakhin's peasant origins and present prosperity. But like Michael Elphick at Riverside, though for opposite reasons, he muffles the buying of the estate; he goes into overdrive, and the play's rhythm, already impaired by a curiously half-baked party, is dislocated. It never picks up, though Mr Finney's abortive proposal to Varya is touching, and crowns a finely detailed performance by Miss Fleetwood of self-perpetuating frustration: the only Varya I have seen whose religious sense is both emphasised and explained.

Ben Kingsley's Trofimov is as good, and should wrap up the question of the play's politics forever. Sidling into his prophetic speech with no audible change of gear (a unique achievement) he leaves no doubt of its rightness, and none either of its irrelevance to his own frailness, which are mercilessly but not cruelly emphasised. Ranyevskaya is right about him, in both her affection and her rebukes. Dorothy Tutin plays her throughout as a woman of quick perceptions: too quick to be acted on. If it seems a miniaturist performance, this may well be a miniaturist role; if we are to be moved at the final departure, it must be by her brother, who unlike her has no lover and no Paris to run to.

Robert Stephens, unfortunately, proves too urbane an actor to turn Gayev's easy tears to account. Other proven players (Terence Rigby, Helen Ryan) make equally slight impressions. A star-cast production cannot afford to have its fireworks fizzle like this; and the deployment of Ralph Richardson as Firs (for a few more performances only) seems peculiarly frivolous. It shifts the play's weight in odd directions (there is no attempt to turn Firs into the protagonist, though the Richardson presence and the occasional grouping seem to demand it) and adds to a sense of disarray below stairs.

He has nothing in common with Susan Littler's flighty chambermaid or Derek Thompson's flash footman and they, good enough in themselves, are pretty disparate too. (They may have grown apart, but it's important that they started out together.) Nicky Henson is completely in command of Yepikhodov's physical and verbal accidents (Michael Frayn's translation generously lets him announce that 'you have reduced me to a complete and utter state of mind'), but neither the character nor the actor are much extended. That really is the trouble throughout; Mr Kingsley apart, the only performances with counterpoint are in the meaning of the act, beautiful performances but they too lack surprise; their delicate sensibilities seem obvious, there is no paradox in their late-flowering creativities. Mr Gough actually seems likelier to be an art historian than a trade unionist. Miss Spriggs's voice oozes affection.

Rattigan's In Praise of Love made out a better case for the emotions, using a similar situation: a partner kept unaware of impending bereavement, a confidant agonising between them. Kenneth Cranham is in good high-tension form in the latter role, and Timothy Block impresses as a moderately brash intruder.

Death also crowns Lenka Janurek's In the Blood (Theatre Upstairs). The circumstances are unlikely, but the author makes us believe in them and in her own right to invoke the final dramatic solution: both marks of a major talent. Up till then she has been leisurely in her evocation of a plush mental home where tomorrow's men can become the most engrossing topic of discussion in the world.

All this detail can be -- in fact is -- self-indulgent, but out of it spring some disturbing as well as disturbed people. They are well realised in Tim Fywell's production; at least the patients are. Jean Boht, an underrated actress, weeps unnervingly; Anthony Douze, a silent patient, Grey by name and nature, hovers uncannily; and Carol Frazer, who seems to have her author very much with her, is the spirit of self-destruction: a former anorexic, eating herself over the edge.

At the Soho Poly these lunchtimes Steve Grant, the estimable theatre editor of Time Out, has turned playwright with a piece entitled Media Hack. (Do I sniff self-deprecation? Which of us then shall escape whipping?) Turning on the attempts of a man dressed up for culinary purposes as Henry VIII to dispose of a mere one wife and his encounters with a couple of encouragingly inefficient terrorists, the piece is compact of pleasing jokes if uncertain how to deploy them. The victim-lady (Georgina Melville) is a coolly recognisable kind of virago.
