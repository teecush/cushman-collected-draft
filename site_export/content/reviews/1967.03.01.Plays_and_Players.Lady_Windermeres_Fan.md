Douglas Byng has now taken over Lord Augustus and, on his second night gave an amiable, uncertain performance. He is no real replacement for Wilfrid Hyde White, who effortlessly got all the character’s laughs, and made his disillusion with Mrs Erlynne extremely and economically touching. I’ve heard his performance dismissed as a speciality turn, but it’s not pleasant to envisage the play without speciality turns.

It’s easy to divide it into melodrama (bad) and epigram (good), but glib to assume that melodrama automatically damns. This one fails because its author doesn’t seem fundamentally interested in his own contrivances. If the play has a main action, it’s the progression of Lady Windermere from idealistic intolerance to humanity, but who cares what goes on in the mind of this priggish snow-princess, or even believes she has a mind in the first place? The intrigues in which she is involved are perfunctory and on the author’s part joyless, but they take up a lot of the play. The rest is comment, and the joke lines are not that good when heard the second time. (The best of them are printed in the programme, anyway.) Those in *The Importance of Being Earnest* are not intrinsically superior but they’re allied to a superb sense of comic situation. The funny characters here are immune from situations which are reserved for the unfortunate leads. Wit is, or should be, more than a string of disembodied quotations directed down centre, even with the dazzling flair of Isabel Jeans.

And, to be fair, there is slightly more to it here. When the stage is cleared in Scene III for a leisurely discussion of social morality—the themes that the main action ineptly dramatises—the effect is pleasant and rewarding. The characters are not falling over themselves to be outrageous; the epigrams are nicely spaced, within an intelligible framework. The production, despite a couple of weak performances, realises this scene extremely well, and one’s faith in Wilde’s sophistication, his ability to see an issue clearly from any number of angles without imbalance, is temporarily restored.

Otherwise, with Hyde White gone, the evening is indisputably Coral Browne’s: her sheen, her taste, her timing are impeccable. Why isn’t she playing Cleopatra somewhere? Somebody should also rescue Ronald Lewis, who is caught in a trap, playing Lord Darlington for real and losing gamely but inevitably in the process.
