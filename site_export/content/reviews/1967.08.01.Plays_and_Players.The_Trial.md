The Trial

Maybe we're going soft. Long before the days of World Theatre Seasons foreign companies visited us regularly, without benefit of simultaneous translation and we did our best to cope. The language barrier seldom deterred critics from writing detailed reviews, compounded in about equal proportions of faith, hope and charity. And yet, the burden of the notices received by the Czech Theatre on the Balustrade for their production of *The Trial* has been baffled indignation. Judgment has been self-righteously withheld (in a context of Kafka as an earlier Aldwych programme once had it, this must be catching) pending either a crash-course in Czech or the immediate employment of a bisexual pair of bi-linguists to synchronise as best they can with the dialogue on stage.

My Czech is no better than the next critic's but I thought I recognised in Jan Grossman's production the flawlessly efficient workings of a powerful theatrical imagination. It didn't quite tally with what I take to have been Kafka's intentions, or anyway with his ambience. Kafka's world was an ordinary one in which extraordinary things happened and were thereby rendered more terrifying; in Grossman's version much of the same extraordinary things happen (and then some—Josef K. does an unbelievable back-somersault in the brief interval between strangulation and demise) but in a context in which nothing else can be expected. It shrieks nightmare from the start; the murderers look like Laurel and Hardy but I don't see Stan and Ollie surviving ten minutes in this atmosphere; even Goldberg and McCann might have found it menacing beyond the call of comedy. There's a lot of organ music, which is very impressive as organ music invariably is. There's a set composed of tubular steel scaffolding which revolves smartly and reminded me strongly of at least one other set composed of tubular steel scaffolding; it's backed by a dazzling cyc and the overall effect bears out all the good things you ever heard about Czech designers except that, doubtless by some oversight, it's not by Svoboda. There's an impressive collection of nymphets, nymphomaniacs and just plain nymphs, by whom K was not, as far as I could tell, universally frustrated. Above all, there's speed and precision of movement; I've never seen a production as uncannily choreographed. The Piccolo Teatro seem, in comparison, showily virtuoso. These Czechs have other things on their mind beside their feet, but their springy swiftness, just one and a half times as fast as normal pace, was totally expressive; it concretely evoked a firmly believable dream-world (compare, unfavourably, Jonathan Miller's *Alice in Wonderland*, as slow as which no mortal man has surely ever dreamed).

The net result was sheer terror. With this I was well satisfied but I doubt if the director intended me to be, since he framed the play in what is, at first blush, a classic alienation device; a narrative voice to apprise us at intervals of what was going to happen, or had just happened, or was happening at this moment if we would kindly concentrate; for the Czechless, it was often anybody's guess which. This, of course, just made the whole thing even more terrifying, and less susceptible to the rational analysis which I take to be the goal of alienation. I got the picture of K versus the rest, but I found it difficult to tell the rest apart.

So, finally, I guess I join hands with everyone else. I wanted a translation. To say that everyone likely to see the production will have read the original anyway is no real answer; the last thing anyone ever bore away from *The Trial* is a clear plot-outline. The programme synopsis, unless you can memorise it before the lights go down, is no help either; though, by virtue of its being merely the baldest string of apparently unrelated incidents, it unnerved me more than either seeing the play or (yes, madam, my credentials are impeccable) reading the book.

Jan Preucil is a brilliant K, a good deal smoother than you'd expect and more interestingly doomed on that account; and, apart from a slight lack of weight, I couldn't fault the ensemble. The whole is a coolly thrilling experience and takes second place only to the Comédie Française in Marivaux in the Non-Tourist Class of this year's festival.
