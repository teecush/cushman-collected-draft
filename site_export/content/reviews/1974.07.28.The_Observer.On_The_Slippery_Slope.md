On the slippery slope

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

I IMAGINE that the Mayfair is even more dependent than most West End theatres on the patronage of tourists. I wonder what the visitors, when they roll down from the hotel upstairs, will make of The Golden Pathway Annual.

For here, imported from the studio of Sheffield's Crucible Theatre, is the authentically English entertainment in whose possibility I had ceased to believe. On the first night a house filled with natives resounded to the laughter of recognition. The show-stopping moment arrived when Michael Peters, schoolboy of the 1950s, brandished a textbook at us, and announced: "We not only have to read these books; we have to put brown paper covers on them as well." Michael is in the first form of his grammar school: "Class," as he emphasises, "1A."

He has, perhaps, some reason to be proud; though he admits that his mother is quite capable of being proud enough for them both. She can introduce her son's scholastic achievements into any conversation, 45 seconds after the off. And behind this there lingers the memory of the morning on which his parents had crept respectfully into his bedroom ("quite a den you've got up here") to acquaint him with the awful significance of the approaching 11-plus. If he fails it, he will miss out on life's opportunities, just as his father always has. Not, as his mother hastily adds, that there's anything wrong with his father's life: it's just that...

Fill in the rest for yourself.

The complexities of the situation are endless and familiar, though in the theatre they have suffered from their association with turgid melodramas about declasse students. The virtue of this show is that it catches the process of alienation on the wing.

It is written by John Harding and John Burrows, two young actors who also comprise half the cast. The hybrid form they have adopted--half play, half revue--is both a strength and a weakness. The scenes are mainly self-contained sketches; some of them are funny and pointful, but when they fail to be either they can be partially excused as temporary lapses in the execution of a master-plan. When the plan itself falters we can concentrate on the individual jokes.

Two main images are employed to chart Michael's progress from conception (he is an immediate result of post-war euphoria) to post-graduation (brandishing an Honours Degree in English, no less, in a vain attempt to break into Fleet Street). First, awaiting us as we enter the theatre, is that huge immortal advertisement for children's shoes, in which two determined tots, pathetically isolated but immaculately shod, march away up a steeply dwindling perspective.

When I was their size this advertisement used to terrify me; its threat ("Children's shoes have far to go") seemed far more potent than its promise ("Startrite and they'll walk happily ever after"). I now know, thanks to Messrs Harding and Burrows, that my fears were fully justified and that the Golden Pathway they travelled would lead only to disillusion.

Which brings us to the second image. The Golden Pathway Annual is a 10-volume compilation, delivered to Michael in birthday instalments, and designed to foster general knowledge, intellectual curiosity, a sense of adventure, pride in our island story, and snobbery. It is the Children's Encyclopaedia, as it might have been edited by Enid Blyton.

The authors have a fine instinct for the potent nostalgic symbol. It was apparent in their previous collaboration, For Sylvia, which was hailed by some as a satiric view of World War II, and which I thought lovely and about as malevolent as The Boy Friend. On the simplest level here they can put on a record of "The Laughing Policeman" and flood the house with memories of "Children's Favourites," and Sunday mornings with Uncle Mac. The Golden Pathway Annual is one of their aptest creations, but having created it they seem none too certain of what to do with it. It does not control the action as it should. There are a couple of good scenes early on where its stories merge in Michael's mind with his real-life circumstances; he imagines himself caught up in a middle-class children's adventure story, where he is cast as the faithful dog and his father--a laundry-roundsman of whom, as his education progresses, he grows increasingly ashamed--as the villain.

As the show proceeds, however, the framework diminishes, occasionally vanishing altogether. When Michael, as a bob-a-jobbing Scout, retreats from the advances of a mature prospective mistress, his punch-line--"Can I come back when I'm a Rover?"--is transparent gag-writing, the product of the author's sensibility rather than the character's. So we stop believing in the play, settle for it as a revue, and have no response when the end arrives and the screws are tightened. Michael begins ineffectually to revolt against his conditioning, but by now the evening is out of control; even Volume 10 of the annual lies far in the past and we have forgotten it. A last-second attempt to remind us of it is devoid of resonance.

Mr Burrows plays Michael, and Mr Harding assumes a variety of roles, most of them figures of authority. Their relationship (which could trigger off a television series) is seen at its purest when Mr Harding briefly impersonates a chicken; oblivious of incipient incineration he stares haughtily down his beak, while Mr Burrows (who cannot be much more than three at the time) gazes back, bright-eyed and fascinated. Mark Wing Davey plays Dad, and Maggie McCarthy is all womankind; the director is Ed Thomason. Good people all.

* * *

The Sack Race (Ambassadors) is a business thriller about a firm which appears to be in a bad way; its walls shudder when touched, and it cannot afford three-dimensional box-files for its shelves, which are filled instead with painted replicas. I failed to follow the intrigues, but the minutiae of office life have a soap-opera fascination, and Michael Denison plays an excellent and conscientious managing clerk in a manner befitting.

* * *

Sherlock's Last Case (Open Space) is a graceless pastiche, whose author believes that a four-line doggerel may be described as a limerick. I do not think that a man possessed of such convictions should be trusted to create a text composed of epigrams, and the actors--to judge from the reluctance with which they let his phrases escape their lips--would agree with me.
