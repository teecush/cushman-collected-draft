Shakespeare in Britain is like the weather. One does not complain about either, since both are inescapable. The Merchant of Venice is produced at least every other year, and nobody gets too exercised over Shylock as a figure of anti-Semitism.

If anybody does, there are two ready answers: Shakespeare was an Elizabethan and could hardly be expected to transcend the prejudices of his time; and the play is actually subversive, with Shylock its most sympathetic character. As a British Jew, product of two pragmatic cultures, I enjoy reconciling these two contradictory positions.

In Britain, Shakespeare is simply there. In North America he's often nowhere, so when he does appear he gets a reaction. Joe Papp, the impresario most responsible for keeping him on display in Manhattan, was recently informed in the correspondence columns of New York magazine that, having once produced The Merchant, he had forfeited his right to take any moral stand whatever on questions of racial prejudice.

American Jews are perhaps more sensitive, certainly more vocal, than British (also, of course, more numerous) and many of them, when they come across The Merchant, cannot believe their ears. Even the Canadian Shakespeare Festival at Stratford, Ontario, has fought shy of the play. In 37 years this British byword for theatrical popularity has surfaced there only five times. When the festival first attempted it, in 1955, the Canadian Jewish News denounced the play as "pure, unadulterated anti-Semitism" with "no place in modern society".

Since then Shakespeare has become enough of an institution in Canada for outrage to have modulated into polite concern. When the play was announced for this year's season, the Canadian Jewish Congress expressed the reasonable hope that visiting school parties would be confined to children old enough to understand the play and its implications, and that their teachers would tell them all about its historical context. (They certainly should; that's their job.)

For a modern audience the play's main stumbling block is Shylock's enforced conversion, as a condition of his pardon, to Christianity. This is a happy ending? Michael Langham, the director at the Canadian festival, then surprised everybody by announcing that he was going to cut it. He believed in the play's final harmony, the victory of mercy over justice, and he did not want it compromised by what a modern audience would take as an act of gratuitous cruelty.

This decision, undoubtedly well meant, pleased nobody. The Jewish community said it certainly was not being done at their instigation. Censorship is as live an issue in conservative Canada as it is in Britain, and teachers and libraries were aghast to see Stratford apparently censoring itself.

Newspaper editorials, calling for the sacking of just about the entire festival, defended the play's integrity as shrilly as if it had once been attacked. Real anti-Semites strutted out of the woodwork proclaiming, usually on the telephone, that Jews ought to be forcibly converted, or that it was too good for them, or both.

At the theatre, Brian Bedford, a brilliantly contained actor, delivered the speech "hath not a Jew eyes" with furious effect into the face of a small boy who had been taunting him a second earlier. I thought it a wonderful image of the persecuted turning on his tormentors, but a Jewish professor of English condemned it in a Montreal newspaper as a stereotype of the Jew as bogeyman. Both of us, I suppose, saw what we looked for. Art may have the power to change our opinions, but it is far likelier to confirm them, whatever they are.

No critic noticed, but it was not only Jewish sensibilities that were spared in this production; Portia's parting shot to the Prince of Morocco ("let all of his complexion choose me so") went as well. But the better Langham's production got - and it got very good indeed - the more one regretted its compromises. He was obviously capable of giving us the play in its full complexity.

I wondered what would happen if he went to the logical extreme and cut all references to Jewishness. You would be left with the comedy of a villainous usurer who lost his daughter and his ducats, and deserved to. Maybe that's all that Shakespeare, who probably never met a Jew, really intended. But one clings to the belief that he intended and what he wrote were two different things.

Peter Hall, interviewed about his London production with Dustin Hoffman, was quoted as saying that The Merchant is "the most pro-Semitic play ever written". I'd like to know how he works that one out.

The play's superficial assumptions are certainly anti-Semitic; the Jew is assumed to be a villain, and proves one. But it's also his villainy - his flesh-and-blood unwhining villainy, more interesting than any sanctity - that saves him and saves the play.

If it were deeply anti-Semitic then its vileness would, in some form or other, infect all its author's other works and we should expunge them from the centre of our culture. But if some remain they all must: including those, like The Merchant and The Taming of the Shrew and Henry V, that offend against fashion. A theatre explicitly devoted to Shakespeare - in Stratford, Canada or Stratford, England - has to keep showing us all the play in all their facets.
