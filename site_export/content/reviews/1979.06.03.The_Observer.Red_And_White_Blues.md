Red and White blues

Theatre
by ROBERT CUSHMAN

Mikhail Bulgakov's The White Guard (Aldwych) is celebrated as the play to which Stalin accorded his personal protection when the Soviet censors got at it. This may prove that Stalin was a subtler critic than might be expected, or merely that he was in certain moods an old sentimentalist.

The principal characters are recognisable human beings, and as such are by their rarity value alone to tap the tear-ducts of a post-revolutionary Russian audience. But they are also rarities in another sense. The White Guard are the military remnants of Tsarism, surviving in a Ukraine of which the Bolsheviks have yet to take possession when the play begins. Their survival depends however on the support of the German Army--who have set up a puppet regime. We see both Army and regime melt away, leaving the country to a triangular struggle.

Bulgakov's play is in three acts, and each party holds sway over the situation for one of them. Act I shows the Whites in their last days of command, Act II is a bloody interlude of victory for anarchic Ukrainian nationalists, and in Act III we hear, though we do not see, the arrival of the Reds.

If we assume for the moment that Bulgakov was writing official propaganda, then he was going about it in quite the most tactful and sensible way. He was keeping the approved side offstage, thus avoiding both caricature and hagiography.

Actually you can take the last scene any way you want, and this is for once a sign of strength rather than weakness. The struggle is obviously unresolved in the author's own mind, and you can feel the stresses at work in the action. Sympathy is tugged in all directions. It is notable, for example, that the last line of the play is one of regret for the old days, but notable too that the speaker is a Polish officer in a play where non-Russians are treated with suspicion. One Russian escapes to Berlin. He happens to be the husband of the play's heroine, and by that action he forfeits all rights to her. The only question is which of her native suitors she will take in his place. Yelena, the lady in question, seems to symbolise Russia itself. Patriotism is the play's moral absolute.

The domestic action only rarely takes on this kind of significance. For much of the time it is pursued for its own sake, and charmingly. But the Turbins are only the focus for the first and last sections of the play. If Bulgakov's aim was to dramatise the pressure of history through its effects on a family (as Chekhov did, without even mentioning specific historic events) then he failed; and in fact admitted as much by dramatising the German evacuation and brief nationalist triumph head on.

This results in one scene of cardboard chronicling, and one of arbitrary sensationalism. Barry Kyle's production finds no style for either of them. But the central flaw--that the play changes direction without any apparent awareness of the fact--is probably ineradicable.

The hearth-side scenes are well done in their own right, with Juliet Stevenson ending up as a vibrant Yelena after a shaky beginning in which her powers of responding to bad news seem overstretched. Patrick Stewart plays the Russian raisonneur, better, and more frequently, than any other British actor, and except when he shouts, his performance here is well up to standard. (To him falls the final task of welcoming the revolution, though since his aspect makes it difficult to forget Mr Stewart's television Lenin in The Fall of Eagles, the enemy seems within the gates from the start.) Richard Griffiths as a bumbling cousin confirms his status as this company's prime putter-in of feet and is still managing, somehow, to avoid stereotype. The other leading men are efficient, but there is a discernible gap between their native selves and their characters; and there is some awful acting further down the line. This is one of the RSC's middle-range productions: honourable but incomplete.

* * *

The theme of Brian Clark's Can You Hear Me at the Back? (Piccadilly) is presumably the gap between public speech and private action. There is a civic architect and his wife who is a doctor and their son who is taking his A-levels; and there are also a headmaster and his wife, a fashion designer. The latter lady is the only one who never gets to address an audience in the course of her work; and, lacking this outlet, she goes bananas.

At all events, she strips off in a middle-class home that is not her own. The immediate cause is that she is in love with the architect and that he is too good a talker to take her emotion, or anyone else's, seriously; he can always escape through words. Much the same accusation was levelled (by critics, chiefly) at the hero of Mr Clark's Whose Life Is It Anyway?; and he seems to have taken it to heart. At all events, glibness is here constantly berated, the result of course being more words. I think he should just have gone on giving his leading men good lines, and stayed unrepentant.

He remains an able writer, and one of his hero's set pieces--repenting of the monstrosities into which he has turned town centres in the name of progress--is not stuff, delivered with expected understated magnificence by Peter Barkworth. It does seem a cheat, though, to have put the Telford's Change ménage on stage in its entirety, with the relationships unchanged, impeccable though they all are at it: Mr Barkworth at conveying the doubts and assurances of a man wedded to his job but aware of its more dubious aspects, Hannah Gordon at being simultaneously loyal and impatient, and Michael Maloney--who comes off best--at bursting rebelliously out of his school uniform.

* * *

The Chichester production of The Eagle Has Two Heads has been attacked as too melodramatic; it is, in fact, not melodramatic enough. This is Cocteau once more in love with death, and to considerably more purpose than in Orpheus. A queen deprived of her consort on the marriage day finds his mirror image, years later, in a revolutionary poet who was attempting to assassinate her. They fall in love, and so can only be each other's death, as in fact they are.

Meanwhile there is some Ruritanian intrigue which can hold our attention only if it is pursued with gusto and if the protagonists are authentically doom-drenched. Jill Bennett suggests gnawing discontent but on too small a scale, while Christopher Neame scampers weightlessly through his lines. Heather Chasen and Michael Malnick make a sharp pair of lookers-on; Brian Blessed as the heavy is trying, I can only think, for shrill menace.

* * *

The American Repertory Theatre is composed of young transatlantic actors, and is situated on Tottenham Court Road, opposite Heal's. Their production of Archy and Mehitabel, based on the Don Marquis stories in which the cockroach plays Isherwood to an alley-cat's Sally Bowles, is enough to make me repent of any harsh things I have said of British attempts at American musicals. Truly, they ain't all born with it.
