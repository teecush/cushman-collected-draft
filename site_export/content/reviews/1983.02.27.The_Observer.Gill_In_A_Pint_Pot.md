Gill in a pint-pot

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

FORGIVE me for flogging an ailing horse; but I continue to wonder why the term "literary theatre" is applied to teemingly theatrical events like Nicholas Nickleby and A Handful of Dust that happen to be based on novels, while denied to the skeletal mood-and-memory pieces that have returned to fashion. The pieces whose dialogue - or monologue - so often harks back to the Georgian poets, and whose staging is the last refinement of the stripped, mannered naturalism pioneered at the Royal Court. The kind whose sets, production, acting and lighting shriek sensitivity at you. The kind currently represented at the Cottesloe by Harold Pinter's Other Places, by Peter Gill's Kick for Touch, and now by Mr Gill's revived Small Change, first seen at the Court in 1976.

There is an unspoken, maybe unconscious, assumption in these presentations (Fen, as produced by Joint Stock, is another one) that the audience should consider itself pretty privileged to be there. Since the audience pays to get in (I can just about remember what that feels like), this seems to me unwarranted, and on the verge of insulting. (I know that there are playwrights who believe in insulting the audience, but I think they are in the wrong profession. I do not, incidentally, count Messrs Pinter and Gill among them.) As I have said before, I resent it.

But I confess that these productions do create a kind of tension, and that it gets to me as well. Sometimes I fall asleep in my seat, but more often I sit on the edge of it, straining to catch something. (That's what I really resent. You can't even dismiss them with a good conscience.) Kick for Touch, though too small even for the Cottesloe - a Gill poured into a pint-pot - still kept me listening, and Small Change actually offers something.

Chiefly this is the relationship between two Welshwomen, living in neighbouring back-to-backs, resentful or afraid of their unseen husbands, bearing with their growing sons. (We watch the growing.) They console each other, even breaking at one point into a charming impromptu song-and-dance. One endures, the other cracks. One lives in unacknowledged bonds of passion with her son, the other can make no contact with hers. Maggie Steed is affectingly gentle, June Watson affectingly rugged. We see them in short scenes, ranging forwards and backwards in time. The duration is exactly matched to the dimensions of the subject.

The boys - James Hazeldine and Phillip Joseph - we get at length. They engage in lyrical retrospectives; indeed Mr Hazeldine seems to be the play's voice, which only confirms that autobiographers are their own least satisfactory characters. At the end, grown, they are given a sustained scene to themselves, making emotive accusations of betrayal on evidence that has never been presented.

The construction goes lumpy, and the lines collapse. The English will accept a lot of verbiage from the Welsh, as from the Irish but such phrases as "the hysterical flash of the present" would be unbelievable from anybody. The play concludes with a reprise of significant lines from its beginning: pure kitsch.

I dunno. When I saw Small Change on its last night at the Court, I was bored to death, but thought that it might be me, and that I was missing something marvellous. This time I wasn't bored, but I didn't marvel either.

* * *

Berlin, Berlin (Actors Touring Company, Warehouse) is an adaptation of a novel, and it is literary, by which I mean that it never takes on dramatic life. You don't actually feel that life slipping away between the adaptor's fingers, as in the bad old days of filleted schoolroom classics, but you come to the end of the evening realising that nothing has happened, though much has occurred, and that you haven't actually met anybody.

John Retallack, directing his own version of Robert Musil's book, delivers an intelligent, good-looking production, subverted by its need to make a statement. The more it goes on about Berlin, the less I felt like believing it. We are shown a collection of alienated people living in, or visiting, the Western sector. There are also songs, redolent of Brecht/Weill or Dietrich.

These, of course, are the ultimate cliches about the city. I imagine the intention is ironic, but they play as if there for their own sake. Why have all that music anyway? Isn't there enough to get through without it? That's another mark of literary theatre: excessive theatricalism. What we learn about Berlin is that it has a red-light district, paranoia, and a lot of anxiety about the bomb. It could be anywhere.

* * *

On the evidence of The Two Ronnies (Palladium) the West End's idea of showbiz luxury is, or remains, a flight of gold-painted steps adorned with light-bulbs. Aslant this, a line of dancing-girls do high kicks. Later on, and on, the same girls do the same kicks (though, to be fair, the sets change), while a quartet of haughty, glaring chorus-boys swivel their hips. British show-dancers might be great, they might be terrible; British choreographers aren't going to give us a chance to find out. Also on the bill are some good acrobats (French and Polish) and Strutz Bear Cats, a vocal group who represent Britain's latest ingenuous answer to Manhattan Transfer.

And there are Ronnie Barker and Ronnie Corbett, doing an anthology of their television material. They have not made the best possible choice: I remember their early shows, before their humour came to depend entirely on the great British euphemism, i.e., longish words taken to refer to parts of women's bodies.

I don't much mind this being sexist, but I do object to the monotony. Also to the waste of talent, especially of Mr Barker, a superb character actor with a rare blend of precision and effervescence, plus a virtuoso line in double-talk, fortuitously but pleasantly teamed with Mr Corbett, a stand-up comedian who does his stuff sitting down.

So what would I actually spend money on, if forced? A handful of classics; a couple of old musicals, Guys and Dolls and Mr Cinders; an extravaganza, Top Girls; a mod comedy, Hard Feelings; maybe a traditional piece of West Endery, Another Country. And of course a farce.

* * *

A new team of actors, mainly from what I think of as the Rudman era at the National Theatre, have taken over in Michael Frayn's Noises Off (Savoy); and they are mostly splendid, especially Benjamin Whitrow's desiccated charmer of a director. They all of course have great parts to play: Mr Frayn sets his characters up before he brings them down; and his farce-within-a-farce promises to be first-rate. The third act is still incredible (plays don't go to pieces in that way), but nobody minds. Michael Blakemore's direction remains exemplary.

None of my approved shows is what is known as "demanding." It's not the function of the theatre to make demands; that's for the audience. Nor is its function to "make you think"; you already know how to do that, and the chances of a playwright telling you something you don't already know - except on a purely factual level - are remote. Make them entertain you.
