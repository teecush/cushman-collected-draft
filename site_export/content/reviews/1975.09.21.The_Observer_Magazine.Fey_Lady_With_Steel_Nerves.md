Fey lady with steel nerves

THE first time many people took Mia Farrow seriously was when she appeared in Rosemary's Baby. Before then she might have been seen in the TV serial Peyton Place or read of as Frank Sinatra's third wife: a short-lived marriage that seems more unlikely the further away it recedes.

Rosemary fixed a screen-image for her, one that has been difficult to shake off. In Roman Polanski's neo-Gothic melodrama she played a young wife impregnated by witchcraft, and she brought to the role a haunting mixture of fright and determination. As her knowledge of her plight grew, her nerves began almost to protrude through her skin, but hysteria never quite took over.

Miss Farrow, now playing the title role in the Royal Shakespeare Theatre production of Granville-Barker's The Marrying of Ann Leete in the Aldwych, recalls Rosemary's Baby now as "the most fun I ever had making a film." She speaks of the demands it made on her but these turn out, surprisingly, to have been more physical than psychological: dangerous things like walking across a real crowded New York street while cars screeched to a halt around her. "But Roman said it was all right, nobody would hit a pregnant woman."

After Rosemary she was, inevitably, offered a whole string of "hounded, neurotic, ailing women" in films. She only played one, in Joseph Losey's Secret Ceremony, giving an odd, haunting performance.

Her films since then have been undistinguished, culminating in the thumpingly bathetic Great Gatsby. Meanwhile she resumed her stage career.

In 1963, at the age of 18, she had made her Broadway debut as Cecily in The Importance of Being Earnest, a role in which she received influential applause but no Press reviews (there was a newspaper strike). Nine years later, in Manchester and later in London, she played the title role in Barrie's Mary Rose. Barrie would seem to be the ideal dramatist for her - all feyness without, all steel within - but the performance proved a little beyond the theatrical effectiveness of her bright, piercing Anglo-American voice.

It tended to isolate her on stage, and made her next stage appearances memorable. She went to Greenwich for a season of plays under the direction of Robin Phillips: Chekhov's Three Sisters and Lorca's House of Bernarda Alba. In both she was the youngest sister and she looked a frail, gawky adolescent harbouring private traumas, private joys and private rages, hugging them to her so intensely that the privacy itself became public. Now she comes back to the stage again in Granville-Barker's play, eighteenth-century in setting and allusive in dialogue to an extent that has daunted producers and kept it off the stage during Edwardian days. She describes her character as "the only member of a wealthy political family trying to create an honest life for herself."

Meanwhile she tries to create her own life as the wife of the most popular (at a guess) musician in the country. Since Andre Previn's chief job is conducting the London Symphony Orchestra, the life centres just outside London: in a handsome low-slung oak-beamed farmhouse in Surrey, called The Haven and standing in 20 acres. When he travels she goes with him whenever she can. The main restriction on her mobility now is obvious: the heroine of Rosemary's Baby having become one of the most publicised of mothers: three sons, plus two adopted daughters from Vietnam. "Whatever is small is ours," remarked Previn.

With the children surrounding her, climbing all over her, asking her when they'll see the musical Peter Pan which they've watched her shooting for television (Christmas, of course) it seems pointless to ask what occupies her time when she's not working. But I do ask and she says of course that the children do.

Otherwise: "I always aim for the sea when I want a frolic. As a child I grew up by the sea in Malibu, and I miss being near it and consulting it daily." Consulting it? Well, "our brain is over 90 per cent water so we ought to feel an affinity with it."

Her father is film director John Farrow and her mother the actress Maureen O'Sullivan (Johnny Weissmuller Tarzan, she Jane) but she doesn't cite any family influence on her choice of career. She makes it sound the most comprehensible of desires: "To be anything you wanted - it's an irresponsible way of living a lot of lives. It's a safe way to let things out emotionally - there's no real risk because you know how it's going to end."

Robert Cushman
