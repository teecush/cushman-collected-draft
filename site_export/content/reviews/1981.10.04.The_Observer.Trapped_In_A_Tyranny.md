Trapped in a tyranny

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Nottingham.

ONE scene in Mephisto (Roundhouse) depicts a rehearsal in a Hamburg theatre of a scene from Goethe's Faust. The year is 1930, and the mainly Leftish company are still reeling from the news of Hitler's first substantial election victories. One of the actors is a Nazi, and the theatre's star, who is both directing and playing Mephistopheles, makes life hell for him, finally demanding his dismissal.

This superb scene, confounding expectation and alive with political and emotional cross-currents, has several pay-offs. The young Nazi, when his party comes to power, revolts at the idea of becoming an informer, denounces the regime, and becomes the evening's first martyr. (This play is nothing if not magnanimous.) Meanwhile his former tormentor, Hendrick Hofgen, has become locked into the role of Mephisto, on and off stage, going to Berlin and accepting the status of honoured artist of the National Socialist theatre. He was the one who had yapped loudest, and his politics are now shown to be at the service of his career.

His comrades--more deeply committed, though all to different factions--had stood by embarrassed or openly offended during that rehearsal scene. They all now meet predictable ends. A Communist dies after 40 days' interrogation in Dachau. A Social Democrat refuses to divorce his Jewish wife; they throw themselves together off a railway bridge.

The play was adapted by the French director Ariane Mnouchkine from a novel by Klaus--son of Thomas--Mann. This English production was first seen last year at the Oxford Playhouse. Klaus Mann is presented as prologue and an occasional chorus. The play presents his world: his father and family appear in transparent disguise; as their society crumbles, they quote from memory the last section of The Cherry Orchard, first playfully, then elegiacally. It's a reasonable device for a play that questions the relationship of the theatre to politics, and comes up with bleak conclusions.

To Hendrick's self-serving must be added the spirited but ultimately useless agitprop cabarets with which he and his friends occupy their spare time, mocking Hitler not because they think he's dangerous, but because they think he's ludicrous. The play mordantly satirises, for example, the Communist reaction to the first Nazi victories: welcomed as a blow against the Social Democrats. Attitudinising is no good; all that can be done is to tell the truth, and this Mephisto does, re-charging our cherished images of Weimar decadence and evoking, like no other work I know, the sense of being trapped in engulfing tyranny.

It has the sprawling handicaps of most dramatised novels: too many characters are given too little time, resulting in baldly emotional speeches that need careful colouring, and a succession of short scenes that ask for careful orchestration plus a firm decision on such matters as the function of a blackout. Apart from lively handling of the cabaret scenes, Gordon McDougall's production muffs everything. I have rarely seen so many actors left with egg on their faces, or indeed so many who seemed to deserve it. Neil Phillips and Tim Hardy are exceptions; and Ian McDiarmid gives the devil his usual reptilian vivacity, though he is perhaps too obviously corruptible from the start.

But the material triumphs; I have not been so moved in the theatre for a very long time. The play ends with a display of the names of actual theatre workers killed by the Nazis. Among them is Klaus Mann himself, who committed suicide after the war with his novel still unpublished. It was feared that it might offend Hendrick Hofgen's original, still successful and still powerful. His portrait--in the role of Mephisto--adorns many theatre text-books.

* * *

Blindly exclusive ideologies are similarly arraigned in Stephen Lowe's Tibetan Inroads (Royal Court). The subject is the Chinese invasion of Tibet. A blacksmith who has been castrated by the Buddhist authorities for adultery with a landowner's wife at first welcomes the Communists; indeed, as far as one can make out, he is single-handedly responsible for letting them in. They feed both his appetite for revenge and his appetite for self-improvement; then their rule too turns sour and he ends up doing forced labour in a road-gang.

He answers the ritual call to self-criticism with a double-edged attack on himself for refusing to question. ("I do not ask myself all the time, who is this road for?") The play's thrust is towards a synthesis of revolutionary practicality and the Buddhist faith in reincarnation. "I want that world you talked about," says the blacksmith's former mistress, "and I'll work for it as many lifetimes as it takes."

Such moments outweigh the play's considerable weaknesses. Mr Lowe falls into all the traps awaiting playwrights who attempt Oriental themes. The peasants and monks speak in curt mystic aphorisms, the Maoists in slogans which the actors try horribly to colloquialise; you never heard so many upward inflections ascending hopefully into the flies. There are also some appalling comic brigands. William Gaskill's production is in fact as devoutly flabby as that of Mephisto and as drably acted. Exceptions: Paul Brooke, though disappointingly obvious casting for a fat oppressor, compensates with stylish work as a magician; Kenneth Cranham keeps the show together in the lead, which affords him scope for his two finest moods--blunt fury and bewildered goodwill--and for much in between.

* * *

The Beastly Beatitudes of Balthazar B (Duke of York's) is another novel adaptation--by J. P. Donleavy after self--kept afloat by this author's fancifully boisterous dialogue, neatly flicked off by Patrick Ryecart and infectiously tromboned by Simon Callow, lungs and nostrils flaring. They keep us disarmed and entertained in the first act, where they are Trinity (Dublin) undergraduates, and even in the second, where they are respectively rich and broke in London, but here the script begins to go to set-pieces, undermined in Ron Daniels's production by yet more maladroit playing (what a week). The saving remnant this time consists of Sylvia Coleridge and Lizzie Romilly. But if you don't demand a plot it's a jolly evening.

* * *

Nottingham Playhouse has enterprisingly staged Mack and Mabel, a 10-year-old American musical about silent movies. (He Sennett; she Normand.) It contains Jerry Herman's best score: two good ballads, a rouser titled "Movies Were Movies" and something called "When Mabel Walks In The Room," which sounds like an attempt to endow Hello Dolly with motivation. (This time the chorus has some reason to welcome her back where she belongs.)

The main weakness is that though Mack--Denis Quilley in robust form--gets full treatment, Mabel is sketchily and conventionally written, though a miniature dynamo called Imelda Staunton lets her stomp a good way into the audience's hearts. The original New York production apparently sank the show with too much bazazz; this one is in no such danger.
