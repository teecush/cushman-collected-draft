Fun in Saratoga

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the Royal Shakespeare Company's seasonal treat

LET ME clue you in on Saratoga (Aldwych). A battle was fought there, but that is of no account. It boasts one of America's most celebrated race-courses, and that begins to matter. It is, or was, a great spa, and that is of crucial importance. Bronson Howard's comedy was premiered in New York in 1870 and reached the English stage four years later re-titled Brighton.

Howard, of whom apart from this production and its programme I know nothing, has apparently been called the Dean of American Drama. At the time he was writing, American Drama was still a small faculty, under heavy transatlantic influence. This, his second play, is a routine period piece, a comedy of manners ageing into heavily plotted comedy of intrigue. The characters are types, and frankly engagingly acknowledged as such. They have come to Saratoga to take the waters and each other. The younger ones enjoy themselves; their elders look on with a kind of tolerant disgust. "Call this travelling for pleasure?" asks one heavy father repeatedly. On the whole I would.

This show is another in the Royal Shakespeare Company's line of seasonal treats - London Assurance, Sherlock Holmes, Wild Oats. Like its predecessors, it celebrates the glories of stock theatre. You take a piece just slightly above the rut of its time and treat it, not disrespectfully, certainly not mockingly, but playfully. In fact such teasing as goes on is not so much of the work in hand as of the actors and the audience for enjoying it. Something atavistic in both factions responds to the material with huge enthusiasm; there are no depths to be explored, but there is a story to be told and followed and fun to be had - the kind of fun that probably drew us to the theatre in the first place.

Charmingly coloured by Jocelyn Herbert, Ronald Eyre's production is delectable, full of comic ripples. Some of these exist outside the action proper; Mr Eyre has taken a trio of waiters - "coloured gentlemen" they call themselves - and let them sing and strut between the acts, to tunes more or less of the period. Bert Williams's gloriously misanthropic Nobody ("Tain't never got nothin' from nobody") becomes the evening's theme song. (It is currently the most exposed of songs in town, since it figures also in Bubbling Brown Sugar.) Just before the last scene, when he is in serious trouble - I shan't of course give away the plot, but the play's sub-title is Pistols for Seven - Dennis Waterman as the multiply susceptible hero joins the ensemble to terrific effect. Aggrieved desperation has seldom been so funnily or rhythmically conveyed. This is actually the high-point of Mr Waterman's performance, which elsewhere tends to timidity. He does not so much offset the gaucherie of James Laurenson (playing his earnest best friend) as reproach it.

The ladies are the best of the evening. Mr Waterman is in love with two of them; only Maxine Audley as somebody's mother escapes his attentions and she is such a glamorous, stylish matriarch that the omission is difficult to understand. The amorous complications, by the way, can generally be seen coming half an act away; that is possibly the sign of a well-made play. Among the girls Joanna McCallum, who is very tall, pairs pleasantly with Polly James, who is the opposite and extremely determined: she gets her man with the aid of a late Victorian contact ad. There is also Cherie Lunghi as a beauteous young wife; she and her spouse (Jeffery Dench) are played - impeccably - as Southerners, conceivably because their name is Carter.

In the cameo reaches of the cast, which are extensive, I noted Billie Brown as a hobbledehoy (a word for which there is sadly little call nowadays) and David Shaw-Parker as an exhibiting artist. The first act is set in a gallery and promises sharper satire than accrues in the later stretches, which are merely comfortable. But there is bubble in the play presaging the next 50 years in which American comedy frothed and steamed ahead while the English variety died on its feet. But we are better curators than they are.

In New York the previous week I found that few had heard of the play or its author; one exception was Richmond Crinkley, the director-designate of the hitherto bedevilled repertory at Lincoln Centre, and one of his aims is to explore the American repertoire for himself without waiting to be scooped by London.

Mr Crinkley is an impresario, not a director, but he has assembled a formidable artistic board, ranging from Woody Allen to Robin Phillips. One hopes he can keep them together (or, if it would be more tactful, apart), since he faces a city where straight drama is in a horrendous way - though not quite as awful as a telephonic misunderstanding over my copy last week may have led you to believe: Neil Simon's Chapter Two has not been hailed as a bad play but as his best. I disagree, but not that emphatically.

For all his capitulations, Mr Simon writes with as much psychological seriousness as any of his compatriots; social seriousness is hardly to be found. The glibness of much British political drama is repellent, but it is preferable to the prevailing Broadway. This can be amusing, and sometimes it is enlarged by a few frames and is dubbed "myth-making," which makes it sound headier than it is. Hence the continued ascendancy of Sam Shepard - regarded by a promising writer like Thomas Babe as a continuing fount of inspiration - and the quick decline of David Mamet from genuinely theatrical plays to plays about the theatre.

This last tendency has been noted, with some disquiet, by Edgar, author of Destiny, currently on a year's sabbatical over there and, as far as I could tell, half-confused, half-delighted. The tendency is particularly noticeable in the musicals, to which one continually recurs. This isn't just my frivolity; a conversation with the sternest American playwright is apt to terminate in the revelation that he was first turned on by My Fair Lady or Guys and Dolls. But these days they keep cannibalising themselves.

This is true even of Michael Bennett's Ballroom, which, though it has a dowdy ordinary man-and-woman story, is really a celebration of dance. Mr Bennett, of course, handles this aspect superbly, drowning out the story and even the song in the process. I think he would like to eliminate song in fact, but the Broadway audience might not buy it. More interesting to me was A Broadway Musical (terrible title - tell people you've been to see it and they say "Which one?"), which is a sustained joke about a bad play being turned into a worse show.

The weakness is that the authors never quite admit to themselves or to us just how bad either is; there ought to be a revelatory last scene. But Charles Strouse has written a better score than for his blockbusters Applause and Annie, and Lee Adams's lyrics generally begin with funny ideas, even though they don't sustain them. It's good-humoured and I hope it gets past the critics; I saw it in preview, amid awful rumours, which gave the backstage calamity scenes an engagingly macabre quality.

Back in London, the King's Head has the best Canadian play I have seen. George F. Walker's Zastrozzi, the Master of Discipline is about a self-proclaimed sinner and saint who, philosophically speaking, need each other. All shades in between are, in a gory last scene, eliminated. I can imagine a production more attuned to sex 'n' violence than Kenneth Chubb's, but - in the performances of John Castle and especially Antony Milner - his is witty. Toronto here beats New York at its own bizarre game.
