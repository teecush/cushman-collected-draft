The kiss in the cafe

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

THE way in which boy gets girl in Stephen Poliakoff's Hitting Town (Bush) is distinctly unusual.

Ralph and Claire sit in a Wimpy bar in Leicester - a doubly depressing experience - and service is not forthcoming. It is in fact non-existent and Ralph, a nervy exhibitionist, fills his plate with the contents of up-ended mustard-jars and bisected plastic tomatoes. Still he gets no attention - the one visible waitress is actually off-duty and merely sits at an adjacent table, blankly sipping a drink - but at least Claire takes notice. Perhaps his whimsical vandalism turns her on, perhaps it embarrasses her and she wants to deflect him from it; either way, she kisses him, forcefully and repeatedly on the mouth.

Ralph is excited; he feels like telling the world. At least he calls up the local radio station and informs the DJ on the phone-in programme that he and Claire are lovers. He adds a few circumstantial details: that they are both 11 years old and that they are brother and sister. Most of this is untrue; Ralph and Claire are both in their twenties and they are not yet lovers. But, yes, they are siblings; and by the end of the play they have indeed gone to bed together.

The play presents incest as a by-product of urban blight; what else, it seems to be asking, is there to do in Leicester? Mr Poliakoff's psychological build-up is cunning, but with hardly a hint of contrivance. Ralph is a student at Birmingham, and Claire awaits his trips home with a mixture of eagerness, trepidation and proprietorial concern; he is rootless and seems to need an older woman. Their kinship apart, they might very easily drift into a desultory spasmodic affair. So we have a play about incest which avoids both sensationalism and studied camp casualness. Indeed, I regret having to use the word "incest" at all; it has a flavour of melodrama, and this play is an honest comedy.

Ralph's shapeless, defiant hysteria is well conveyed by James Aubrey, but is less interesting than Claire's control. Everything in Judy Monahan's performance - compassion, humour, exasperation, fear - is contained; she is not hard, nor preternaturally cool, but she will survive. Tim Fywell's production is tightly atmospheric; multiple scene-changes paradoxically work far better in this tiny space than in a large one. The play is slightly too long; Ralph's memories of the Birmingham bombing seem like an artificial attempt to add resonance to a text that breeds its own. It is preceded by This Property is Condemned, an early Tennessee Williams, done slowly.

What can I say about the first night of The Exorcism (Comedy)? After a dilatory start, depicting a quartet of wealthy lapsed idealists settling down to Christmas dinner in a richly-appointed country cottage, it gathered momentum when the author Don Taylor turned on the horrors and the four found themselves trapped in space and time (doors and windows, clocks and watches, alike immovable) and rose to a chilling climax when the spirit of the house took possession of one of the four and spoke through her.

The chosen medium was played by Mary Ure and the scene has since proved - in the most literal, ghastly sense - to have been the climax of her career as well. She died on the following day and I can only record that her last speech was frighteningly well done, while earlier in the evening she had meshed excellently with Honor Blackman, Brian Blessed and Ronald Hines as Peter Coe's production steadily raised the temperature.

It was not admittedly until past the interval that their efforts were rewarded. The play, though short, appears padded and the early airing of Socialist guilt (though vigorously done in particular by Mr Blessed, the best-heeled and worst-conscienced of them all) is hardly anything new. But it proves in the end unexpectedly valid; the ghost that haunts the house died cursing the unfeeling rich, and it is this curse that Ure pronounced and that Mr Taylor's characters expiate. It may not be the subtlest of propaganda, but it is more powerful than you would expect in the West End and has greater emotional force than much that is preached on the fringe; it is the theatrical equivalent of an Oxfam ad as opposed to a Workers' Revolutionary pamphlet. It will do nothing to change society, but it could inspire the penning of a few well-aimed cheques.

* * *

The World Theatre season is upon us again, reinforcing Peter Brook's contention that drama in an alien tongue can be more impressive than drama understood. It can also be more boring, but such are the breaks. The Cracow Stary Theatre's production of Wyspianski's November Night (Aldwych) was in fact less tedious than frustrating; it dramatises the Polish uprising of 1830 against Tsarist rule in a style which it would be tempting, but over-indulgent, to describe as epic.

It gives us not episodes but large fragments - chunks, really. Some show the insurrectionaries (who, like all good conspirators, wear their collars turned up); others, the Tsar's deputy, single in will but divided in conscience (and smoulderingly played by Jan Nowicki, who has an individual - perhaps unique - line in stocky neurotics); others still, supernatural powers, alternately encouraging strife and symbolising it, and here given stunningly operatic treatment. Andrzej Wajda's direction has visual grandeur to match. If the imagery seems obvious (withered branches and decapitated statues in the field, huge isolated double doors revealing brilliant vistas at court) it is also rewardingly bold. But if there is any unity in this dislocated text it would have to be apprehended through verbal imagery, and while this may abound in the original little of it survived the customary sepulchral litany available on the simultaneous translation system.

There is the kind of play (such as Philadelphia Here I Come!) in which two actors embody a single divided psyche; and there is the kind of play (most recently Only Make Believe) in which a writer writes on one side of the stage while the results of his labours are acted out on the other. I don't like either kind, and Stanley Price's The Two of Me (Jeannetta Cochrane) is both kinds at once. Its hero is played by the brothers Clive and David Swift, a wheeze to be remarked more for its cuteness than its cohesion. You get a better class of incest at the Bush.
