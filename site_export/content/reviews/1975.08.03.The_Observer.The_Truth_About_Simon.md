The truth about Simon

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

I DOUBT if there is a shapelier play on the London stage than Simon Gray's Otherwise Engaged (Queen's). Its two acts admirably rhyme, so that a mock suicide in the first is balanced by a genuine suicide in the second. This underlines the fact that in the first act we are concerned with characters to whom life and death are a sour joke, and in the second with people to whom they are a sour reality.

Between these two groups stands the play's central figure, so central that he never leaves the stage. His name, like his author's, is Simon, and he is a publisher, successful and extremely comfortable. The play shows him to us in two lights: our angle of vision changes abruptly at the interval. To begin with, he seems a good-natured, apparently passionless chap doing his best to accommodate everyone. He is scrupulously interested in the correct use of words. His friends, as friends will, use them sloppily, filling their conversation with unintentional ambiguities. Most of us would let these pass, but not Simon. His candour sweetly laced with irony, he tries to get the record straight by asking the questions we would all like to ask.

So we sympathise with him; we also laugh with him, his questions are so elegantly poised. And he seems so assured and with it all so kind; it seems very good of him to bother with people as grey as his brother, desperate for the assistant headmastership of a very minor public school, or as plain objectionable as his best friend, a drunken literary journalist who is having an affair with his own ex-wife and moans about having to write articles for the Radio Times. (It seems that all other publications are monopolised by versatile, hard-working Australians who don't care how little they're paid).

There are also the upstairs lodger, a sociology student who sponges on Simon without shame and is indulged without stint, and a girl who strips to the waist in an effort to get herself laid and published. Simon turns down her body (though not her manuscript) on the grounds that, though desirable, it cannot outweigh her appalling behaviour. Besides, he has a famously happy marriage. However, the implication that he therefore never plays around is dispelled at the first-act curtain when he receives a visit from an old schoolmate whose adored girlfriend, it turns out, Simon has seduced.

From then on it is all downhill for Simon. All the qualities for which we have esteemed him are turned against him. The man he has cuckolded recalls their schooldays; Simon was the golden boy, the beautiful bedmate of the sixth, while he himself was a plop; a quaint synonym for a drip, defined in later life as one who progresses from masturbation to marriage to monogamy. Simon's urbanity towards him, as towards his own brother, stands revealed as heartlessness. The lodger realises that his landlord's generosity barely conceals a lazy contempt.

Finally his marriage, the biggest prize of all, is shown to have its basis not in trust but in a kind of rootless tolerance; it goads his wife to take a lover (Mary Miller) which troubles his detachment hardly at all. The curtain falls on Simon, in the company of his writer chum, listening to a recording of Parsifal, for which he has been trying to find the time all day: portrait of a couple of creeps at rest.

I am not sure, though, that the play ever faces up to quite how creepy Simon is. According to his wife he should be envied for his serenity but pitied for the emptiness that makes it possible. But it is the serenity that makes the greater mark, since it finds its expression in wit. Alan Bates (who looks in this role appropriately like Cliff Richard, prototype of bland agelessness) corners virtually all of the evening's abundant laughs; one or two are left over for Julian Glover, in a good coarse performance of the journalist. But the other characters, Simon's victims, are allowed no spark; not once does a joke go against him. The play's structure and its style tell us different things; we only get round to feeling pity for Simon at the final curtain, by which time it seems disproportionate and sentimental.

Still, Otherwise Engaged, following the soupy Ardele into the Queen's, is, in Harold Pinter's icy production, a superior piece of comedie grincante. The actors, apart from one player whose words would sound as expressive delivered by a computer, are good; Mr Glover and Benjamin Whitrow match each other, soak for plop, and Mr Bates radiates glamorous unconcern. Whatever the author's intentions, audiences will go for Mr Bates for his insouciant put-downs and for the splendour of his living-room.

* * *

Topol's Othello at Chichester turns out surprisingly well. He can make little of his earlier scenes; what hampers him is not his voice, which is slow and painstaking but gets there in the end, but his appearance. His build seems unexpectedly light, he rocks on his feet, describes strange circles with one arm. The play goes to sleep. But the onset of jealousy transforms this Moor; an animal passion seizes him, at one point propelling him bodily down the steps of the platform stage and out through the auditorium. I have never seen an Othello throw a more convincing epileptic fit; even the arm gestures expand with his madness till the final scene finds him executing some spectacular underarm bowling. By this time his interpretation has worn thin again (unless you prefer an Othello permanently denuded of nobility), but he vitalises the middle of the play.

Keith Michell's Iago has an excellent moment as he gazes open-mouthed at the first eruption, wondering what he has let loose and whether he can control it. Iagos come in two categories; surly and glamorous. Mr Michell, naturally equipped for the latter, tries for the former. A sternly non-commissioned moustache vainly competes with a ringing Sandhurst voice. He carries no outer marks of villainy, which makes his dealings with the other characters brilliantly convincing, and none inner, which ruins the soliloquies. Peter Dews's production, accorded a nineteenth-century setting, is solidly attuned to plot and character. I doubt if Mr Dews would recognise an RSC Guiding Concept if he found one lurking in one of his own programme notes.

* * *

Agatha Christie's Murder at the Vicarage (Savoy) is about a Colonel who is found with a red rosette pinned to the back of his head. But the people on stage call it blood, and are distressed. They include an Artist, a Policeman, a Young Girl (who flounces; no other word for it, in token that she will come to no good), a Doctor, a Curate and, of course, a Vicar. Also a sweet old lady detective who solves the mystery by studying human nature, though where she finds it is unclear. Barbara Mullen plays her with much winsomeness, rotating both her arms even more ferociously than Topol. I thought Derek Bond as the vicar was the best, though I caught him gazing stolidly at the audience when he should have been humouring Miss Mullen. The younger members of the cast are unable to bring any conviction at all to their roles; which doubtless reflects great credit on them. Has not this play the cosiest of titles?
