All the world's a stage

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

THE ORDER of the week, as ever, is role-playing. We have two plays set in the theatre, one of them apparently written in the conviction that actors are wittier and more fascinating than anybody else, the other that they represent, because of the conditions of their craft, all humanity.

Both these contentions are fallacious, and I do not know of any professionals who share them. (Have you noticed that when actors write plays, they are generally not about actors?) But it is difficult for the rest of us not to swallow them at moments; particularly perhaps if we are critics.

To prove the point, one of our number turns up as a character in Molnar's The Guardsman (Lyttelton) which, being devoted to the second or 'all the world's a stage' proposition, is the more pretentious play, but is also the more talented. It was written, and is set, in pre-1914 Hungary and our colleague's name is Bela.

Those were the days, it seems, when a critic was less of a drudge, more of a confidant. Bela never wastes his time going to plays; he pays social calls in opera-boxes, sits in sumptuous boudoirs (yes, those were the days) and advises his friends, the stars on how to run their private lives. Since these are extensions of their professional existence, having a critic on the premises may conceivably be helpful. Actually Bela spends most of his time saying 'I told you so,' so his private role is an extension of his public one. His clients are Nandor and Ilona, toasts of Budapest, married six months: just about time, on previous evidence, for Ilona to seek a new lover. To save her the trouble, and himself the pain, Nandor dresses up as a Guards officer and in this guise attempts to seduce her. Does she recognise him?

At the end of the play she says that she did, immediately; and in giving this away I reckon I am doing a service to all concerned. I saw the play in Stratford, Ontario, last year and I found that I relished the middle more for knowing the ending. (The beginning, which plods unmercifully, bored me on both occasions.) For Ilona may be lying; and it is the peculiar virtue of the play that every word of their central encounter will support either contingency. Scarcely less piquant is the dilemma of Nandor himself, not knowing whether success or failure in his project will harm his amatory ego the more. There is also the matter of his actor's ego; can he stand to have his deception detected?

But this is where the play, and the claims that have been made for it, become irritating. Acting has never been taken to mean persuading your beloved at point-blank range that you are somebody else; and it makes an extremely clumsy metaphor for the subtler deceptions of everybody's emotional life. Molnar's The Wolf, which we saw in London a few years ago, is a far more affecting study of marital distrust; not being concerned with actors it could not take short cuts to artifice or to glamour.

Nandor could of course be schizophrenic; Richard Johnson's jolly performance flirts with this idea. (If you really want to know, the situation was more wittily and penetratingly dramatised in Irma la Douce.) Peter Wood's production meanwhile goes very hard for theatre-as-image; we first see Nandor and Ilona from behind, taking curtain calls in Othello (what else?) and are later privileged to watch all the changes of Ralph Koltai's stridently artificial sets. None of this is necessary but reason not the need; it is very pleasurable. So is the fine balance between reality and artifice that Diana Rigg maintains as Ilona, and so is the performance of Brenda Blethyn as the maid, besotted and adenoidal.

Miss Blethyn, I feel, deserves a paragraph. She has been at the National three years, impressing immediately as the wife in Bloody Neighbours, a play hardly anyone saw. Since then she has mainly been employed in domestic service of some kind. She got a break, and took it, in The Force of Habit but that was such a flop that nobody noticed. She has also been a replacement for better-known actresses; she took over the heroine in Tales from the Vienna Woods, for which she wasn't suited, and is now more delightfully employed in Bedroom Farce. There, as in The Guardsman, she is quietly explosive.

* * *

The things actors get up to. In Spine Chiller (Duke of York's), they are assembled in a New York theatre to rehearse a play (called Spine Chiller) that 10 years earlier had been called off in the same theatre owing to the unexplained murder of three of the cast. Some of the survivors have actually turned up for the revival; remember that actors need to eat and producers love a gimmick.

Needless to say history repeats itself; the mock-deaths of the play within the play turn out for real. The story rapidly slid away from me (the killing at the end of Act One surprised me most since I had never registered the victim being alive) but the sheer cheek of author George Baxt is engaging. For once, though, a show suffers from not being camp enough; since the accents are wrong, the spirit is, too. 'Are you leaving your body to medical science?' 'No, to Paul Newman.' Some actresses could get away with that but not Sian Phillips who, for all her authority, has never struck me as the upstaging type.

* * *

Real people are no consolation: not in Laurence Dobie's Penny Whistle (Hampstead) which offers six of them, teenaged, unemployed or otherwise malcontent, drifting into an anonymously smart hotel for coffee, going in for confessionals and for some very rowdy games -- during which, surprisingly, they remain undisturbed. (Service sure is bad.) They are real, especially in their confusions, but unfortunately, in drama, as in life, they are going nowhere. Substance failing, we look for style; and find it in Nancy Meckler's direction, Grant Hicks's set, and the performance of Peter-Hugo Daly.
