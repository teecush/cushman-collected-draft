In black and white

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the National's 'Measure for Measure.'

MICHAEL RUDMAN'S production of Measure for Measure (Lyttelton) has enormous carnival vitality. It reminds us that Shakespeare's plays are constructed like variety bills, each incident designed to be bashed over to the audience with maximum intensity.

There were more individual rounds of applause on the first night of this production than I have ever heard in the classical theatre. Some of this, I imagine, had to do with the nature of the occasion; Mr Rudman has re-set the play on an imaginary Caribbean island, and cast it for the most part with West Indians. The actors' friends were in, and they reacted with volatile partisanship. It was after all an historic production; black actors have never been so abundantly employed on a central stage.

By Mr Rudman's lights the audience counted almost as an extension of the play; in a programme note (a welcome return to an old tradition whereby the director generously furnishes the critics with rope for use at his own execution) he writes that Measure is a public play, whose major characters live or die by the reactions of the crowd. Lucio's jokes, for example, are cracked for the delectation of an enthusiastic bunch of low-lifes, equally ready to lynch him when he falls foul of the Duke.

I think Mr Rudman is essentially right, but he does run into problems. Shakespeare in this play often mentions the people, but when he gets down to presenting them he seems only interested in what, even by Elizabethan standards, must have been an unrepresentative sub-section. Life is centred first on the brothel, all the characters being either inmates or customers, and then, after the vice-laws have bitten, in the prison. So Mr Rudman's basic set, right down to the last scene, has to be the red-light district.

It seems odd that the Duke should choose this area for his ceremonial re-entry; odder that none of the bourgeoisie should be on hand to welcome him; and oddest that he should be crammed, together with his henchmen Angelo and Escalus, on to a balcony at the side of the stage. A battery of microphones does something for their official image, but the stage-picture is lopsided and the action slips away.

Two major characters are white: Leslie Sands's magnificently worldly Escalus (who seems, confusingly, to be in on the Duke's masquerade) and Antony Brown's Provost, a good chap but hidebound. They presumably are the relics of some former regime. Now the island is in the hands of Stefan Kalipha's playboy Duke, who goes into priestly disguise because ... here I have to stop since Mr Kalipha's rather nervous performance offers no solution to this, the play's central problem.

Yvette Harris's Isabella states emotions without exploring or elaborating them. Both these actors have trouble with the verse: Mr Kalipha tries to bury it, Miss Harris lets it bury her. Only Norman Beaton is on easy terms with it; his Angelo is an uncompromising Puritan--a cleric, in fact--who deliberately retreats behind his desk and into officialdom when Isabella begins to get to him. An actor who once managed, quite superbly, to play Ariel as a civil servant, obviously has no trouble with Angelo; he is complete master of old words and new modes. Maybe his best moment is his icy retreat when the comic relief arrives; under the leadership of Oscar James's Pompey, which has real swagger, they pose an unaccustomed threat.

Bertice Reading plays Mistress Overdone, temporarily reprieved from jail so that, disguised as a night-club pianist, she may sing 'Take, O take those lips away.' That song of course is in the text; not so the number that Peter Straker's Lucio, promoted to super-pimp, sings about the beneficent effect of sexual prohibition on the flesh-market. He performs it wonderfully, polishing it off with a stunning falsetto, and Tammy Eytle's lyric beats the hell out of every new musical in London. But it puts us in another convention, and we spend the rest of the evening waiting frustratedly for it to be followed up. In sum Mr Rudman makes mistakes, but he makes them in the right spirit: joyous not reductive.

* * *

By contrast the gags in The Merchant of Venice (Old Vic) are aimless, means of somehow getting through the play, or up and down the steps of an archaic set, which makes the Queen of the Adriatic look as if she's being held up by scaffolding. Timothy West, redirecting a former production, is his own best actor and such subtleties as exist--apart from the engaging portrayal of Salerio and Solanio as a right pair of Job's comforters--are to be found in his performance; Shylock's realisation in the midst of mourning for Jessica that Antonio is now in his power is a sharp corner negotiated with a quiet businessman's relish that carries through into the trial. Stephen Jenn is a golden boy Bassanio who actually makes you listen to his casket speech, but Prunella Scales's Portia really needs a script by Congreve.

* * *

In Beaumont's The Knight of the Burning Pestle (Aldwych) an Elizabethan grocer and his wife invade the performance of a romantic comedy and demand a part in it for their apprentice. They remain on stage, treating the characters in the drama as real while egging their boy, who is quixotically infatuated with stories of knight-errantry, on to greater deeds. Michael Bogdanov's production puts the intruders into modern dress, thereby adding another convention to a play in which there are already about two-and-a-half operative at any given moment.

The couple's naivety becomes unlikely, and their dialogue, partly rewritten, an uneasy compromise between two centuries. But there is a tremendous swing to the show, with an uproarious multiple duel, a barbering operation that makes Sweeney Todd look humanitarian and a Morris-dance of surreal extravagance. Timothy Spall makes a prodigious hero; Margaret Courtenay and Timothy Knightley do the city proud, and John Woodvine sings a lot, which can't be bad.

* * *

The Accrington Pals (Warehouse) of Peter Whelan's play are a Lancashire regiment who marched off to be annihilated in the First World War, but the title also refers to the women they left behind who draw ever closer together in the face of deprivation: social, sexual and--in the sense that they are denied accurate news from the front--intellectual. One (sensitively played by Janet Dale) refuses to make common cause with the others; this, I think, was meant to be the fulcrum of the play, but it is late in developing and doesn't mean much when it does.

The writing is sympathetic but slack (there is a ghost-scene which is as puzzling as those in Mr Whelan's Captain Swing and a lot less powerful) and there is some greyish acting. Bill Alexander's supposedly three-sided production is something of a sham; I got irritated by the constant trundling round of a single set-unit, since its face was pretty well invisible from my flank anyway, and the performances were all delivered firmly out front.

Mr Whelan's basic situation was anticipated 50 years ago in Coward's Cavalcade; so was much else, including the whole of Oh What a Lovely War. The scene in which a bereaved mother forces herself to celebrate on victory night is as powerful a wordless episode as any playwright has contrived. (She's upper-class, of course, but as misinformed about events as any of Mr Whelan's workers.) For all that, Cavalcade isn't much of a play; the pageantry gets in the way of the people who are paper-thin, mere sounding-boards for offstage disasters. (We don't lose anyone we know in the Boer War, but the Titanic and 1914-18 make up for it.)

What everybody knows about Cavalcade is that it ends with a patriotic toast and a despairing song, Twentieth Century Blues; in that order. David Horlock's revival (the first ever) at the Redgrave, Farnham, reverses the sequence, but if he's trying to soothe us, he doesn't succeed. The song is electrifying, sung by Christina Matthews in a voice to match her gleaming white dress; the toast is tame. So the impact is confused; it could even, who knows, be Brechtian. Mr Horlock has staged the play with magisterial efficiency, and the crowd scenes--with local amateurs swelling the numbers to 300--are like nothing you have ever seen.
