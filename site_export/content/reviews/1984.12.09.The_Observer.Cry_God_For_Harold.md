Cry God for Harold

ROBERT CUSHMAN on theatre books

HAROLD HOBSON still writes an elegant, teasing prose unmatched by any practising theatre critic. He can be roguishly apt. In Theatre in Britain: A Personal View (Phaidon £19.50), his new survey of the theatre since 1920, he observes that many social plays seem to have been written not out of liking for the human race, but out of a curious detestation of everyone who does not agree with them. 'Right, as we used to say, on.'

Hobson piles on the paradox, which can be provocative but also provoking. Faced with such bland, unremitting irony you begin to wonder if he actually means any of it. His gift to criticism is the principle of the still, small voice. Where others single out ringing lines and thunderous effects he dwells on quiet, hidden moments; murmured declarations of marital love are favourites. He is quite justified in picking these out, but whether he should cite them so remorselessly is another matter.

Taking his cue from Matthew Arnold's theory of poetic touchstones (itself a suspect monument to High Seriousness) he trots out the same instances again and again across the years; the focus has shifted from whatever value they may once have possessed to his own tenacity in remembering them. Here he recalls yet again the impression made on him in 1947 by five words spoken by a walk-on in a production of The White Devil: 'The message is "aren't I sensitive?"' and, latterly since the actor turned out to be Patrick Macnee, 'aren't I a good talent-spotter?' Hobson is an artist in self-congratulation; after their deaths he wrote startlingly uncharitable pieces about George Devine and Richard Burton saying what second-rate men they were and how they always made a point of asking for his advice.

One longs, in the face of Hobson's boast, to blow a few gaffs. To point out that, whatever he and Ken Tynan may have done for its fortunes in London, Waiting for Godot came here (as Tynan admitted) with a ready-made Continental reputation; and that Hobson's deservedly famous championing of The Birthday Party was done from a position of advantage. Alone among critics, he had already seen a Pinter play: The Room at the NUS festival. He knew what to look for.

He is not prepared to allow other crusaders much credit; he lets us know that he had to fight for Beckett and Pinter, while Brecht entered unopposed. This is rubbish; Brecht received a lot of flak -- some of it from Sir Harold -- and still does. These writers, along with Osborne, founded (he says) the modern drama: something he used to approve of but now has doubts about. The argument here gets curious but it seems that the progressive drama has been irredeemably tainted since Auden fled the country in 1938. Since Auden was barely a playwright at all, it is difficult to believe that anyone in the theatre cared much about his defection then or now. But it gives Sir Harold the chance to compare the overtly highbrows with the brave Entertainers who kept the wartime theatres open (presumably because people were actually going to see them).

The intellectuals, he says, despise entertainment and this has caused a 'ruinous rift in our theatre.' My own observation is that the intellectuals are dazzled by entertainment; witness their compulsion to turn everything into a musical. They pilloried Rattigan -- though not as pertinently as Sir Harold suggests -- not because he was entertaining, but because he was an unfashionable kind of serious.

Actually, it is not so much laughter that Sir Harold misses in recent drama, as religion. It sometimes seems that he will give a rave to any play that contains a Biblical quotation, though he does not always salute joy and compassion when they are set before him -- there is no mention here of Jumpers or Nicholas Nickleby. Another oddity is that he seems to think Bob Peck and Jonathan Pryce are the same actor. The danger for the Christian critic (a category into which Sir Harold fits somewhere between G. Wilson Knight and Malcolm Muggeridge) is that, being on the side of the angels, it makes him very cavalier to those below. Which explains why Sir Harold can be the most patronising, and on occasion the cruellest, of modern theatre critics.

From theatrical management, two autobiographies. In Empty Seats (Hamish Hamilton £9.50), Michael White, who progressed from novel-writing in a lighthouse to being our second most prominent independent producer, infectiously conveys the dandyish enthusiasm that he brought to A Chorus Line, The Rocky Horror Show, Joe Orton, Edna Everage, and an aborted disaster called I.T. Sometimes radical and usually chic, he has done more than anyone to keep alive the spirit of Carnaby Street; when he writes, as he often does, of 'a key Sixties figure' he means it as a compliment.

Kitty Black writes of the days when Tennents, under Binkie Beaumont, ruled the West End. That would be worth exploring in depth; but her Upper Circle (Methuen £10.95), occasionally evocative, is more often pointlessly anecdotal. Black and White presumably know more about their own lives than I do; but both books seemed to me full of mistakes. Some of them are grammatical (whatever happened to publishers' editors?) though both can give points to Michael Coveney, a full-time writer, whose Theatre Royal (Quartet £12.95), an otherwise conscientious history of the Theatre Royal, Stratford East, dangles enough participles to cause a hernia.
