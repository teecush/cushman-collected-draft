True love conquers all--as usual

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a new Osborne play and a Pinter revival.

JOHN OSBORNE'S The End of Me Old Cigar (Greenwich) is the latest paean to true love. This is a commendable preoccupation, but his view of it is uncomfortably proprietorial. One favoured couple per play appears to be his limit, and it may not be accidental that the lady in the present case is portrayed by the author's wife.

In this new play Jill Bennett (Mrs Osborne) and Keith Barron share a rhapsodic interlude in the midst of elegant squalor. They are guests at the house of Lady Regine Frimley, an exalted brothel-keeper out to screw her clients in all possible senses; she entertains a succession of prominent men so that she may eventually expose them and implicitly the whole male sex. It is all their ridiculousness (I apologise for the double entendre; they will keep coming). It is surprising to find Mr Osborne endorsing even so eccentric a brand of Women's Lib as this, but, of course, it turns out that he had no such intention. Lady Regine is morally defeated by the joy of Miss Bennett and Mr Barron, and practically by as feeble and arbitrary a plot-twist as a reputable dramatist can ever have employed to bring down his final curtain.

Though he may have little sympathy with the lady and her cohorts, Mr Osborne has found uses for them en route. On arrival, each is granted a streak or two of individuality, but they soon drop indistinguishably into the house-style, the frigid raillery -- spinsterish even when scabrous -- which dominates this author's worst plays, and disfigures even his best (though in A Sense of Detachment it was elevated, unencumbered by the complications of plot and character, into real humour, real passion). Rachel Roberts as Regine seems to have half the lines, and speaks them well, and the play, in Hayden Griffin's designs and Max Stafford-Clark's direction, looks as nice as any country-house comedy in years.

It was tough to be a theatre critic in 1958. That year saw the first production of Harold Pinter's The Birthday Party, and, though a couple of reviewers may now pride themselves on the lonely wisdom which they extended to the play, the rest simply rejected it must surely shudder. Truly, the oldest hath borne most; we that are young shall never see so much. It would be becoming in us to comfort our elders by deciding that really they did not miss so much; that the play, though very well for its time, seems in 1975 no more than a interestingly quaint. I anticipated that I would have some such response to the revival at the Shaw Theatre, but Kevin Billington's production confounded me. The play moves here with the confident autonomous gait of a classic.

It is almost possible to take it for a masterpiece, so astonishing is Mr Pinter's command of dialogue and stagecraft; indeed it is his wholly traditional technical expertise that makes the fifties response to this play seem so surprising. The humiliation of Stanley, the browbeating of Meg's seaside boarding-house at the hands of Goldberg and McCann, is totally hypnotic as a process. It is more than that because our interest, if hardly our sympathy, is proportion to Stanley himself, and almost as much by his external language as by his cross-talking persecutors.

At the birthday party itself, Stanley is momentarily exposed to the two simultaneously; Meg's speech of congratulation assaults his ears, while McCann's torch shines in his eyes. This hint that a man's protective shell may be used to destroy him is the most powerful moment in the play. But it never captures this unity of theme; the rest of the party is a bravura piece of atmospheric writing in which meaning evaporates. I may accept (though I do not really believe) that we need not be told why Stanley is finally reduced to zombiedom, but it seems an inexcusable failure to black out the stage and so we never even find out how. For the rest of the play, the emphasis switches to Goldberg, whose steady line of patter represents his hold on the world; when it falters, as, momentarily, does Petey, we see how close he comes to being as lost as Stanley. But this is a schematic demonstration, further undermined by the realisation that by the third act the play has outlasted its own narrative reserves. What Goldberg's reduction of Lulu or her whole character are meant to add I have never understood.

Mr Billington brilliantly unites the characters' isolation in playing, in pools of loneliness and bewilderment. John Alderton is a regally unsentimental Stan, gruff when he gets the chance, whining when he doesn't; in aspect a desiccated cross between John Lennon and Piggy of Lord of the Flies. (Fate fashions impasses for men who wear glasses.) Sydney Tafler as Goldberg looks, especially in moustache, exact, although neither he nor his director always makes the most of the jokes. Goldberg is, for an actor, the most munificent Jew since Shylock; Lee Montague's television performance remains indelible (it had a great deal to do with establishing the Pinter vogue) and it might be worth recalling that the part was created by John Slater who died just as this revival opened. Anna Wing strikes the proper cloying chord as Meg; and Basil Lord is nobly reserved as her husband Petey, who may represent the closest approximation to a moral force in the whole of Mr Pinter's work. Eliot would have made a guardian out of him.

Female conspiracy (Osborne) and male dementia (Pinter) collide in Laure (Theatre at New End). This is announced by its director, Steven Dartnell, as a modern adaptation of Strindberg's The Father, and it does indeed make a few fashionable lurches, emphasising (though hardly exploring) the theme of insanity, and hinting at incest. More to the point is that Mr Dartnell has drastically telescoped the action, gaining in sharpness if hardly in clarity. That the cast are catastrophically young for their roles (except for one who is too old) may, since there is no attempt to disguise it, be intentional.
