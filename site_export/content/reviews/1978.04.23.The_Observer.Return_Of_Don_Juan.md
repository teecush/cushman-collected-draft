Return of Don Juan

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on plays by Horvath, Shaw and Shakespeare.

ODON von Horvath's Don Juan Comes Back from the War (Cottesloe) was a casualty of the aborted season of Juans planned for the National Theatre.

As it happens, the recent Greenwich production of Moliere's entry in this hallowed competition has provided the context originally envisaged for the South Bank. It's been nice to see both plays, but they have not illuminated each other much. Perhaps they would have done if we had seen them under the same roof, but I am more inclined to regret that the Horvath play reaches us so long after the disappearance of its parent production, Tales from the Vienna Woods.

This Don Juan is the kind of stripped allusive play that dramatists traditionally turn out in their last years. Horvath was still young when he wrote it; he was, all the same, only two years from his death, and the nature of the play - which is death-haunted - is enough to make you believe in premonitions. It assumes the world of soured romanticism more elaborately presented in Vienna Woods. It also assumes, without providing very much detail, the period of hyper-inflation that followed in Germany on the end of the First World War. But this should be no trouble. In 1936, when the play was written, it was recent history; for us, it is myth and more potent than most.

Horvath's Juan belongs to the great twentieth-century line of disenchanted, almost passive libertines: the object, not the instigator, of the chase. He is the only man in a cast of 13; and the dozen actresses play an average of three roles each. In those circumstances no wonder he's Don Juan.

Juan's charm leads him to financial as well as amatory success; in a world where cafe bills mount casually into millions he emerges top of the heap. Much of the play (which is only 90 minutes long) is taken up with the recriminations of the women who throw themselves at him; recriminations aimed at one another as much as at him. A fragmented picture is built up of a society which actually is not fragmented at all; at every level, its preoccupations are grimly uniform.

The play moves into its highest gear, however, when city intrigues are left behind and Juan goes in search of the one girl whom, in pre-war days, he loved and betrayed. We have known from the start that she is dead; have met her grandmother who, from a distance, offers Juan real hatred; from everyone else he gets only spite. He ends, in a scene that reaffirms Horvath's power of infusing melodrama with new life, by his girl's snow-covered grave. He is a doomed figure whose fate has already been prefigured in one brief and dazzling scene: the decapitation of a snowman. Some of the earlier scenes are laboured, but the ending is theatrical poetry as fine as anything since Chekhov.

This beautiful, perplexing play is given a capable if rather tight-lipped production by Stewart Trotter, superbly served by Christopher Hampton's translation and by the mournfully magnetic Daniel Massey. The National Theatre seraglio paraded for the occasion includes such favourites as Polly Adams, Helen Ryan, and the Susans Fleetwood and Littler; but of the many varieties of female bitterness on view it is Janet Whiteside's that strikes home the hardest.

* * *

Love, war and their general unfairness are further irradiated, in an excellent Greenwich production of Arms and the Man. Robert Chetwyn's staging has been condemned as 'un-Shavian.' That, to my mind, is its strength; Mr Chetwyn forsakes the usual rattling tempo and the resultant automatic laughs, and finds real sex, therefore real romance, therefore real comedy.

One actually wanted to see Raina and Bluntschli, the destined lovers, brought together; and Felicity Kendal, in her best performance to date, flashes with bewitching aplomb from Raina's affectations to her blunt realism. Lewis Fiander's Bluntschli, her mirror-image, has to bide his time; the deliberate pacing does rather dampen his jokes (which are most of his role) in the first act; but they allow him to command the stage as the comic judge, and finally the comic victim, of the later scenes. Sergius is unfortunate, neither a real nor a chocolate soldier, but Ursula Mohan as the maid Louka is suitably provocative (which in this play means sensible) and the bewilderments of Major Petkoff become, in Frank Middlemass's hands, the pivot of the plot if not the entire play.

* * *

Not much love of course in the three parts of Henry 6 (as the Aldwych insists on billing this scholar-king, though he was surely at least as good a Latinist as his father V); though all the war you could desire. There is not too much virtue in seeing all three on the same day; unlike The Wars of the Roses aeons ago, they have not been shaped for cumulative effect. Though Terry Hands's production is almost endlessly resourceful (and had better be), he cannot, in remaining so faithful to the text, avoid repeating himself.

Part Two is certainly the best play; a magnificent animation of court intrigue in the long truce between foreign and civil wars. But the most enjoyable is Part One; a glorious re-ordering of history to the playwright's own convenience. Part Three, with even more carnage, plods more; full of battles the author had to include rather than those he wanted, and conducted for much of the time in bellicose but strangely bloodless one-liners.

Its four great scenes are glimpses of another world; two of them - Henry's yearnings for peace on Towton field, and, most especially, his murder by the incipient Richard III - have ripened since Stratford; Alan Howard, fine in the first, is inspired in the second, embracing death while somehow remaining a human sacrifice, not a suicide. Earlier on he delivered less a characterisation than a commentary on the play roughly from Henry's point of view. (Did he want the laughter that greeted the words 'poor soul' severed from their followers - on hearing that one of his subjects had been born at Bury?)

Many performances have gained in amplitude: Anton Lesser's Crookback, Julian Glover's Warwick, Emrys James's York now twitching less, and David Swift's bulldog Talbot, a part once played similarly and as well by his brother Clive. (The Talbots are one race who go always to the Swift.) I am puzzled by a new Stratford puritanism, which decrees that rhetoric shall be ladled out to the audience in full frontal, while jokes, as in the Jack Cade scenes, are coyly hidden. These, to keep the best till last, are democratically-written plays in which any actor, soldier or messenger, can find himself for a moment carrying the show; hardly anybody in this team drops it.

* * *

At the other extreme Alec McCowen holds the Mermaid himself with his rendition of St Mark's Gospel. Mr McCowen is an actor of unexampled lucidity and a master of two opposed modes - the rhetorical and the quizzical. In this performance the two fuse into a kind of defiant humour, an acceptance of mystery that tackles doubt head on. The most powerful line in the evening, a hammering shout, is 'help thou mine unbelief.' That, from an actor to an audience, is the most potent prayer of all.
