Belfast songsters

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

STEWART PARKER'S Catchpenny Twist (King's Head) has the first mortar-opening number I have ever heard.

On this occasion the performers are three teachers in end-of-term drag, the headmaster, thanking them for their 'mirthful mouthfuls, their witty ditties,' refers to them as 'masters and mistress of terpsichore and rhyme,' and may be excused for having other things on his mind; his school is in Belfast.

He loses three of his staff straight off. The lady, Monagh, goes to Dublin to become a club singer; the two gents, Roy and Martin, try to make it in Belfast as songwriters. While waiting for the big break they knock out, on commission, a few commemorative verses on fallen Provisionals. This proves tricky for them, partly because the fallen have names like Quigley, which are difficult to rhyme, partly because they are doing similar hackwork for Protestants.

Reprisals are threatened, so they take off for Dublin, where they resume their partnership with Monagh. Like Crosby, Hope and (toujours) Lamour, they are soon on the road again, first to London, where they almost pick up a recording contract, and then to a Euro songfest, where their past picks them up. (Bob and Bing used to proclaim that 'Paramount will protect us 'cos we're signed for five more years.' No such luck here.)

Mr Parker writes with a smouldering joviality that is very attractive, and his storytelling skill has greatly increased. But his play is to an extent the victim of its own inevitability. His characters are so plainly foredoomed that what they do en route, or even who they are, seems immaterial. Their deaths cannot really be blamed on their being apolitical; we know the committed also cop it.

There are diversions along the way. We see our heroes creating, Martin knocking out the rhymes with a facility that might even arouse the envy of Sammy Cahn. We hear some of their songs, though they work in a watery ballad style too pallid to be parodied; Mr Parker and his composer, Shaun Davey, have much more fun with the German telesong entry, Zig Zag Zong (put over with delightful idiot relish by Tony Doyle). Roy and Monagh have an affair, and Monagh is said to be in love with a television producer emblematically named Playfair. (Trusted by both factions, he still goes to his grave.) But we never meet him and Monagh herself never mentions him; she shapes up dramatically as yet another self-destructive chanteuse.

There is some stiff supporting work, but the main trio are fine: Bryan Murray chipper, Tony Scannell tense and glowering, and Nichola McAuliffe vibrantly depressive. Robert Gillespie's production - monumentally lavish by King's Head standards - is quick and careful.

* * *

Frances de la Tour made some kind of fringe history the night I saw Duet for One (Bush) by getting around entrance applause, though who could be sure whether this was due to her television stardom, her theatrical cult status, or the fact that she entered in a wheelchair? Anyway, as things turned out, she deserved it. She plays a violinist stricken by multiple sclerosis, and dispatched to a psychiatrist by her glamorously talented musician husband. 'I can be sarcastic,' she warns the doctor unnecessarily; and much of the play, as the lady tries to sound practical and uncaring about her plight, finds Miss de la Tour at her most bright and waspish.

When her facade finally cracks, the effect is electric; and the author, Tom Kempinski, caps her tirade with another, in which the psychiatrist (played with unnerving accuracy by David de Keyser) begs her not to fall into the fight-for-life line. The play is biased towards him; we absorb his disease-wise charm, which occasionally he obliges, only by flying into jargon ('we're talking punk-rock') or syntactical impossibilities ('for your cynical information'). I also felt let down at the time by an equivocal end; in retrospect it seems fair.

* * *

The early Elizabethans were not good at women. (They were not, for the most part, good at men either, but they felt less compunction about stuffing them with Marlovian rhetoric.) So Marlowe's Zenocrate is a study in depth compared with the heroine of George Peele's David and Bethsabe (brought by the Riding Lights Theatre of York to the Upstream Theatre, opposite the Young Vic), which is unfortunate for the play when all King David's troubles (rebellious sons, etc.) are meant to spring from his guilty liaison. But Peele's textbook reputation as an undramatic lyricist is here belied; it is true he could hardly stop talking, but he could move a narrative along in bald strokes.

This production is done in modern dress, which lends a certain tickle to the battle scenes between Israel and Egypt. These are curiously staged (the opponents clap hands and dance tangos), but when the bits of production stop, the actors get on with it vigorously enough. I doubt if this heralds a full-scale Peele revival, but his strange folk-comedy The Old Wives' Tale - so naive as to seem furiously experimental - would be worth the attention of any company aiming to involve and bemuse its audience.
