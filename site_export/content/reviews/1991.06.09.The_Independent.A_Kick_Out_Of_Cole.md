Today is Cole Porter's 100th birthday, and what a swell party this is. Porter knew all about swell parties, and his public image - he really was a legend in his own lifetime - was that he was always either giving or attending one. The legend survives, and so does the work. The celebratory CDs have been coming out, compilations of old performances, and collections of new ones. He has even, on one of the latter, acquired a new identity as a crusader against Aids.

This would have surprised him. Porter was never regarded in his lifetime as socially conscious. Society-conscious, yes: he was born rich, and married richer, to Linda Lee Thomas, a bruised divorcee who was content to provide gracious cover for her husband's voracious homosexuality. Song-writing made him a third fortune. He was not just rich and famous; he was famous for being rich. Though not a native New Yorker - he was a back-country boy from Peru, Indiana - he and his work came to typify smart Manhattan society. Icy-smart he often seemed, and so did his work, at least the verbal part of it. The music, though, could be tropically torrid: making for a highly personal mixture - half refrigerator, half hothouse - that had huge appeal. Porter, as an admiring contemporary remarked, made sophistication popular.

He still does. In fact, the composer-lyricist who in Kiss Me Kate advised us to Brush Up Our Shakespeare ("and they'll all kow-tow") has himself been undergoing a Bardic canonisation. Like Shakespeare, he attracts two types of devotee. There is the purist, represented by last year's EMI recording of "Anything Goes", which might have been sub-titled "Everything Stays". Every word and note of text, score and orchestration was recreated from the 1934 originals.

Then there are the modernists, the proponents of Porter our Contemporary. This has been going on since the Twenties when, after a long apprenticeship devoted largely to amusing himself and his friends in Paris and other backwaters, he arrived on Broadway (and subsequently in London) as the composer of scabrously romantic propositions like "Let's Do It" and lush philosophical inquiries like "What Is this Thing Called Love?"; and was acclaimed as the playboy of the West End world, the spirit of the age. Then came the Crash, and Porter couldn't have cared less. All through the Thirties he wrote successful stage musicals that were purely escapist. The only changes he noticed were of style, not substance:

In olden days a glimpse of stocking
Was looked on as something shocking
But now God knows
Anything goes.

For this he was not merely forgiven but feted, as if he had grasped some fundamental American truth.

In the staid Fifties, his raffishness was forgotten and his yearning love-songs enshrined in the respectable fabric of middle America. Frank Sinatra and Ella Fitzgerald recorded them superbly, for many definitively, but they worked from edited texts. To the vast pop-song public that never went to the theatre, Cole's bawdier comedy numbers were unknown. In 1965, the year after Porter died, an American revue that emphasised his naughtier songs ("Do you use the breast-stroke, dear" / Yes, I use the breast-stroke, dear / But in the morning, No!") claimed him as a liberated and liberating harbinger of the permissive society. That was pretty desperate opportunism; Cole, despite the splendour of his buttonholes, was nobody's flower-child.

This year, the charity record Red Hot and Blue - known as "The Cole Porter Aids Album" - inadvertently proved the point. The performers, all current pop acts, show what 35 years of rock have done to the ability to inflect a lyric. Or, apparently, to understand one. A rap reduction of "I've Got You Under My Skin" tells us repeatedly to "Use your mentality, wake up to reality", that being all that is left of the original lyric, apart from the medically suggestive title. To wake up to reality apparently means to use a condom. Porter would probably endorse that if he were alive. But it's not what his song says. In fact, it's the opposite. The lyric tells us that the singer is beyond using her mentality. The music agrees, even more insistently. So do Porter's other trademark love calls, a variant of the torch song that we might describe as a scorch song. "Night and Day", "Begin the Beguine" are about being too consumed by passion to want to take precautions. For a pop song to convey that amount of emotion without making itself ridiculous is very rare.

"Begin the Beguine" is actually about remembering that kind of passion rather than experiencing it, which is just as well, since its exotic throbbing would be pretty hard to take neat. It has an ironic thread running through it, a thread superbly sustained through an extra-long lyric. This is one of Porter's special strengths. His rivals could throw off brilliant one-liners, but Porter had a unique ability to construct in paragraphs.

He never tells us much about his love-objects. "You're The Top" is wonderful fun, but no beloved is actually like the Louvre Museum. What is really celebrated is the ingenuity, skill and effervescence of the admirer. Even an apparently tender ballad like "Easy to Love" ends up focusing on the gently woebegone suitor who sings it: without asking, however, for more than token sympathy. Others could admit to vulnerability and indulge in self-pity. Porter never stooped to either.

"Let's Do It" (1928) was his first classic and in some ways the archetypal Porter song. It is a biological catalogue whose arch subtitle "Let's Fall in Love" allows it to pass itself off as a ballad, risque, of course, but probably sincere. (It actually replaced a more direct and therefore less acceptable invitation, "Let's Misbehave".) The world accepts it as a celebration; the initiated note its jaunty and slightly chilling reductiveness:

Old sloths who hang down from twigs do it,
Though the effort is great,
Sweet guinea-pigs do it,
Buy a couple and wait.

As assured as he was, Porter was an outsider in his professional life. In the procession of lyricists and composers he was the only patrician, the only Yale man, practically the only Gentile. His competitors were mainly lower-middle-class Jewish boys with their ways to make. He ended up composing more minor-key laments than any of them. He wrote riper, more urgent boy-girl songs - sometimes exaggeratedly so - than the heterosexual lyricists.

The comic counterparts of his famous ballads are the countless speciality numbers, many introduced in the Thirties by Ethel Merman, in which every woman is a nymphomaniac and every man either a stud or a dud. He amused himself and his audiences with patter songs that catalogued the Manhattan party-givers and world-cruisers among whom his life was lived. The songs are as much celebration as satire. Porter reserved his bile, expressed with acidic restraint, for social unfortunates: for Miss Otis who allowed herself to stay down in Lovers Lane and was lynched by a vengeful mob; for Mrs Lowsborough-Goodby, too gauche to know how to organise a weekend.

In the quintessential Porter song, love is "a trip to the moon on gossamer wings" (breathtaking invention so precisely matched to its music that we take it for granted) to be dismissed with delicious panache as "just one of those things". We would all love to say goodbye like that. Unless there's a war on, in which case, Ev'ry Time We Say Goodbye, we would like a tug as romantic as the one depicted in 1944:

Why the gods above me
Who must be in the know
Think so little of me
They allow you to go.

At the famous climax, when the lyric talks of the strange change from major to minor which the music proceeds to enact, we react to the emotion rather than to the cleverness. We smile but we are also moved. The serenely beautiful music gives us little choice. It may be his best song.

His tastiest opening couplet is the one that begins "At Long Last Love":

Is it an earthquake or simply a shock?
Is it the good turtle soup or merely the mock?

Porter claimed to have written that song, in 1937, as he lay trapped beneath a horse which had thrown him and crushed both his legs. It inaugurated 20 years of agony, endured with stoic courage, and marked the beginnings of a slow artistic decline - arrested by the creation of Kiss Me Kate, possibly his masterpiece, certainly his greatest success. Porter would attend it with friends and murmur artlessly, "Good, isn't it?"
