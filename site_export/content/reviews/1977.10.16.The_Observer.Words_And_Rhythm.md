Words and rhythm

ROBERT CUSHMAN talked to Crosby a few days before he died

IN A year fatal to superstars -- Callas, Stokowski, Elvis, Groucho -- Bing Crosby may well be missed as much as all the others put together. My one meeting with him occurred just 10 days before his death. I had gone to the Chelsea flat he occupied during his two-week season at the Palladium to talk to him about his deceased friend and colleague Johnny Mercer.

He was much occupied in planning future activities, including a trip to the Far East, and we had some difficulty in fixing a time. He finally agreed to see me at 10.30 in the morning: not, I would think, the best or most convenient hour for a 73-year-old singer working hard every night.

It was an unglamorous meeting; he was in pyjamas and dressing-gown, his baldness and his wrinkles were undisguised, he had a cold, and he had plainly not properly recovered from the fall that had threatened to put him out of action for good. But he was kind and courteous and amusing, and the way he spoke was fascinating.

Not just what he said, though his remarks bore out Shaw's dictum that a man describing others always describes himself; he dwelt on Mercer's feeling for jazz and his love of old vaudeville performers, on the ease and flexibility of his singing, his small-town upbringing, his loyalty to his family and his ability to appeal to both plain and fancy public.

This was a distinguishing mark of Crosby's own work; his corniest songs were distanced, saved from embarrassment, by urbanity and humour. He was often at his best in joky duets (with Mercer, Armstrong, Astaire, Hope, Sinatra) and he assured me that all the ad libs were genuine. The record companies just let them play around.

He had in fact an enormous affinity for language; he spoke to me of Mercer's "little jollities" and "jocosities" and those words, informally formal, illuminated much of his own appeal. He was one of the few American singers who could talk to an audience without becoming unbearably stilted. He slipped very naturally into the role of popular music's elder statesman, since his appeal was always reassuring rather than emotional.

Meeting him gave me clinching proof of his great ineradicable achievement; he established that in popular music the singing voice is valuable only as a natural extension of the speaking voice. That, incidentally, is why his sense of words and rhythm made him the best-liked comedian Tin Pan Alley ever gave to Hollywood.
