Florentine intrigues

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Oxford.

I GRUMBLED recently about the National Theatre's weakness for small-cast plays, but the British premiere of Lorenzaccio (Olivier) does a lot to balance the books. I counted 45 actors' names in the programme, and most of them are doubling. It is a gladsome spectacle and surprisingly easy to keep track of.

Alfred de Musset wrote this Renaissance extravaganza in 1832, and its prodigality has two traceable causes: (a) he and his compatriots had just found out about Shakespeare, and felt liberated; (b) precociously disillusioned with the theatre at the age of 23, Musset gave no thought to practicalities. His text has as many scenes as this production has players.

Lorenzaccio is a denigratory nickname bestowed on Lorenzo de Medici, young cousin of the equally young Duke of Florence. Tyrannicide is his mission in life, and he becomes the ruler's confidant and procurer, so as to get within sword's reach of him.

He gives Florence the chance to become a republic again, a chance Florence - through a combination of clerical intrigue, military intimidation and popular apathy - fails to take. In this, we are assured, Musset saw the reflection of his own society, the bourgeois monarchy of Louis Philippe.

He stakes everything on his protagonist, and wins. Lorenzo has been described as a modern Hamlet, but in fact he is never in doubt about whether to murder. He more resembles Hamlet's Jacobean successors, the cankered sensualists, critical appraisers of their own degradation.

Lining up a female relative for the ducal bed, he more or less re-enacts 'The Revenger's Tragedy.' He feels himself unworthy of his destiny because he has been corrupted, not by his revenge, but by its preparation. He has been the duke's accomplice, so, except as a kind of suicide, his deed is ethically meaningless. It turns out to be politically meaningless as well.

It is a fragile, skin-deep world we are presented with, alive to sensation, but to little else. Its hero, embodying it, gives unity to a sprawling canvas.

It is, in the best sense, a lightweight play, and one informed by a lazy whimsicality very much of its period. In the original - not, unfortunately, in John Fowles's adaptation - we hear of Lorenzo 'spitting into a well to make circles in the water: my greatest pleasure.' That may be camouflage, but it also sums up the play and the character.

One of Lorenzo's pranks is striking off the heads of statues; and one he seems to have decapitated - unknown to art historians - is Michelangelo's 'David.' At any rate, there it is when we enter the Olivier, its head on the ground, but still dominating John Gunter's set, and there it remains. I suppose it is possible that the National imported the original from Florence and dropped it, but it seems more likely that we are being offered a metaphor.

The statue sprouts scaffolding, and a stonemason - who appropriates the citizenry's most defiant lines - spends the evening on it, trying to put Humpty Dumpty together again. He never does succeed.

The stage is patrolled - Florence being a fief of the German empire - by guttural soldiers with gutterish manners. These devices are heavy-going but effective, and this is true of Michael Bogdanov's production in general. It has no special imagination, and the abrupt endings to the brief scenes often leave it with egg on its face; actors stomp off or stay to move the furniture. But it has plenty of guts and energy, and these are the immediate requirements.

Later productions may add in subtlety, but they are not likely to improve on the bloodbath Mr Bogdanov offers at the climax. His casting in the higher reaches is, shall we say, unsurprising: Michael Bryant as wronged honest man and grieved father, Basil Henson as smooth cardinal. Lower down there is a lot of backslapping, more verbal than physical; Mr Fowles's version may not be virtuoso prose, but it deserves finer tuning than it gets here. Frances Viner pummels her way through the moral qualms of the duke's mistress, and she is one of the better ones.

The duke himself is more a condition than a person and Clive Arrindell leaves him that way. Everything rests on Greg Hicks as Lorenzo and he, if lacking in mischief, gives a very clear picture of a disgusted spirit. The play makes sense, grips attention, and smells of immortality.

* * *

Second billing in Marilyn! (Adelphi) goes to a character called the Era, who sings at the close: 'Write your books and write your plays and try to capture her in your songs/I'm the one to whom the girl belongs.' This, preceded by a brilliantly engineered montage of Monroe clips, decisively trumps the show itself: they say one cannot capture Marilyn Monroe on the stage, and we believe them.

To do it, they upstage their own star, Stephanie Lawrence, which is unfair. She acts well, dances splendidly, sings superbly: the last without the self-congratulatory air generally assumed by British musical starlets when they hold a high note. She cannot capture the original's sexuality, but it is a condition of the myth on which the show trades that nobody could.

As an actress Miss Lawrence is at her best before the fall, growing up in an orphanage, marrying teenage, switching off the radio which is only announcing something about Pearl Harbour. As Tom Stoppard might have phrased it, she wasn't Marilyn then; the script is happier with Norma Jean. Once in Hollywood it accelerates through the familiar biography, leaving by default the impression that Marilyn consciously created her own image. If she was that much in control, why did she go to pieces?

Meanwhile one ticks off the echoes, some of them probably accidental: 'West Side Story,' bopping at the prom; 'Annie,' an orphanage and two dogs; 'Sweeney Todd,' a crazy mother; 'Fiddler on the Roof,' a Jewish wedding, courtesy of Arthur Miller; above all 'Evita': distant but omnipresent narrator, media heroine and the experience of being told nothing you did not already know.

Also the form, pop eclecticism with the bits joined together to make a kind of opera. As such, it is a smoother job than 'Evita'; the music may not be distinguished, and the lyrics less than, but they do link up persuasively, sped on their way by Larry Fuller's very tidy staging, which incorporates a couple of fine ensemble numbers, including a dance by adoring males gazing at the famous nude calendar: the one time the show makes a real musical-comedy statement. Bill Byers's orchestrations resound with nervous energy, though the music may be what they are nervous about.

* * *

The Oedipus Plays (Oxford Playhouse) should really be called the Creon plays; he is the one character common to 'Oedipus the King,' 'Oedipus at Colonus' and 'Antigone,' the Sophoclean trio (not trilogy) here combined by Stephen Spender. Arthur Cox plays him as a pot-bellied politician who never dared hope for kingship and is over-assertive when he gets it. Having made his fatal mistakes, he exits moaning 'blind, blind,' thus linking up of course with Oedipus, who puts out his own eyes (and starts the proceedings by announcing that he has come 'to see'), and Tiresias, the sightless prophet.

If this strikes you as facile, I would not argue with you. All three plays are done as bold political melodrama, the last right, the first tight but bafflingly cheapened, and the middle one incomprehensible. Richard Durden as Oedipus maintains his actor's dignity whatever may have been done to his character; Moira Redmond is a smooth and powerful Jocasta; and the choruses seem out of place.

* * *

Vanbrugh's The Provoked Wife (Warehouse) is the best of the three ATC productions I have seen: a sane, bitter view of sex, marriage and divorce. Lady Fancyfull, a spoiled beauty, is serenely played as a frump, but Christine Braddell's Lady Brute is a treasure, an actress more provoking (never mind provoked) drawing her clothes on than many are taking them off.
