Complex Simon

PROFILE

MOTOR-BICYCLES, TV tubes, motor cars, machine tools, ships, House of Commons crockery: Britain may be getting worse at all these things, but we are still good at playwrights. In recent weeks, another of them has broken through the mysterious barrier between obscurity and fame: Simon Gray.

He has had successes before now. Indeed, of all English dramatists to emerge since the war, few have a better commercial track-record. Both the Royal Shakespeare Company and the National Theatre have presented plays of his, but it is in the West End that he has scored. 'Wise Child,' in 1967, was a big hit; 'Butley,' in 1971, was even bigger. Now his new play, 'Otherwise Engaged,' looks as if it will outstrip them both; and it has at last made Gray's name known.

His popular success is not surprising. There has always been a good public for comedy of manners, and this is Gray's field. He specialises in bleakly comic accounts of the personal relations between professional people, highly intelligent and articulate, as they grate against and wound one another, often within some educational establishment.

Gray is a London don. It is a recurrent theme, especially in his later work, that the child is father to the man. In a new play he has written for television, to be shown this autumn, a character remembers a stroke he played in an under-15 cricket match, a late cut to the boundary. It was, he recalls, 'the one moment in my life when I felt a touch of sublimity.' This is a rare case of a Gray protagonist dropping his shield of self-protective wit and revealing the schoolboy still in there. To Gray, school is still uncommonly vivid: a time when anything was possible; when friends and brothers were choosing which route to take, before spending the rest of their lives wondering if it was the right one.

Gray is dark and chunky, with hair over his eyes. He was born in 1936, the second son of a physician of Canadian extraction. He spent the war years in Montreal where, freed from the parental grip, he acquired the habit of smoking 10 cigarettes a day and developed an ingenious form of street begging: this involved writing an address on an envelope and then bursting into tears until a kindly passer-by would prevail on the little chap to explain that he had lost the stamp for the letter he had been sent to post, and would cough up the necessary sum.

Aged 14, having returned with his brother to England at the age of eight, he became mixed up in the Great Tube Ticket Machine Swindle, whereby he inserted a Georgian penny, purchasable for twopence, instead of a florin, and thereby acquired a ticket and three sixpences change. He worked this racket until he and the rest of his gang were nabbed on Victoria station by three policemen.

He went to Westminster School in London, where he was very keen at games; then for three years to Dalhousie University in Canada, after his father took a job there as a pathologist; and then to Cambridge, chiefly because he wanted to study under F. R. Leavis.

He went to all Leavis's lectures and seminars, and Leavis was and continues to be an enormous influence. He regards Leavis as the greatest critic of the century and the greatest teacher he has ever met. He considers Leavis's direct influence to have been on his reading rather than on his writing, but there too it can be seen in the way that Butley or Simon in the new play both, so to speak, act as literary critics in life.

To others, the way they catch at words and phrases and play wittily with them may seem a device for distancing experience and protecting themselves from realities. But it is only through words that we can grasp realities, and it is only by using words precisely that we can properly engage with experience. That fuzzy language is not only fuzzy thinking but also fuzzy morality is one belief at least that Simon Gray's protagonists share with Dr Leavis.

There were some bright people around Cambridge in those days -- Peter Cook, David Frost, Andrew Sinclair, Ian McKellen, Margaret Drabble, and Jonathan Miller among them. But Gray made little effort to cut a figure in university society; he is remembered by his contemporaries chiefly for a lacerating attack on undergraduate writers, including Margaret Drabble.

One thing he had in common with that generation, though, was a feeling that if you were ever going to make it, you had better get started quickly. By the mid-sixties, he had published three novels, moved to London, married, and started teaching.

The first two novels were comedies, rather in the style of early Henry James. The third, 'Little Portia,' showed a considerable rise in temperature, which was maintained in his early TV plays. After a genial first effort about a man with an addiction to hot baths, these became increasingly violent, both physically and emotionally, culminating in 'Wise Child,' which, said the BBC, could not possibly be performed on television. It was thereupon commissioned as a stage play and associated Gray in many people's minds with the late Joe Orton; like an Orton play, it appeared to be a comedy of kinkiness, requiring its leading actor, Sir Alec Guinness, to spend most of the evening in skirts.

The subsequent plays have been, so to speak, straighter. Though there were plenty of intimations of homosexuality in 'Butley,' it was the hero's marriage which, however obliquely presented, lay at the core of the play. This is more plainly true of 'Otherwise Engaged'; the heroes of both these plays shun emotional commitment, and shelter behind walls of irony. In 'Butley' the audience could be fairly sure that this was a sign of the character's inadequacy, but 'Otherwise Engaged' is more unsettling. It almost dares us to reject its hero; where Butley was seedy, he is well set up and supremely witty. The precision of his language makes it clear why Harold Pinter, sensing a fellow-spirit, has been so eager to direct Gray's plays.

After a period in which the word was suspect in the theatre, our writers are rediscovering language; they use it sparely and sardonically, often as an instrument of judgment, and here Gray is in the mainstream.

While producing these plays he has remained a full-time lecturer in English at Queen Mary College, London. The Mile End Road is one of London's least attractive spots, and Queen Mary College is scarcely the intellectual powerhouse of the Western world. Someone who has long-running plays in the West End and Broadway can hardly be there for the money. Nor does he seem destined for great heights in the academic career structure. Though he has written some critical essays, he has had neither the time nor inclination for Eng. Lit. crit. of the kind that brings advancement.

In Academe it is not enough to write literature: you have to write about it. Gray once actually received a letter from some college committee which pointed out that his chances of promotion were being jeopardised by the fact that he had never published anything: this was at a time when he had brought out four novels and three plays.

Why, then, does he go on teaching? First, because he enjoys it. It also gives him a life outside the theatre and keeps him in touch with literature. 'You must have a grotesquely high opinion of yourself,' he says, 'to think that your contribution as a writer is more important than your contribution as a teacher.'

What time is left over from the theatre and the university is lived privately in North London in a big house with his wife, two children of nine and six, three cats, two dogs, a great many books and various lodgers and droppers-in. He works long hours, writes compulsively, and reads with ferocious concentration -- especially Dickens, which he goes through over and over again. He plays squash, watches cricket and football, and goes to the cinema -- often secretly in the afternoon, so that he can have the added pleasure of feeling guilty.

He usually gives the impression of being harassed and about to be overwhelmed by domestic crises and the complications and sheer busy-ness of his life. This is partly a protective device to preserve time to work in and to keep social predators at bay.

However, though he doesn't suffer fools gladly, he does alleviate the pain they cause by taking an almost clinical interest in what makes them such fools, and in why their folly takes the precise form it does. He is fascinated by the motives behind people's behaviour -- including his own -- and has a collector's interest in foibles, mannerisms, and obsessions.

His refusal to accept unthought-out or half-baked judgments, his quickness of mind, and a wit that is not always tempered by mercy, can make him intimidating. But when he is relaxed and with friends, he is excellent company and an extremely funny talker. Though none of the characters in his plays is a self-portrait, there are family resemblances between him and them. With a packet of cigarettes, some good whisky, and a great deal of wit and intelligence, they do their best to cope with the pleasures and pain inherent in the human condition.
