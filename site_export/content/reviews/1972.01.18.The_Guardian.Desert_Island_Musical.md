Desert island musical

Stephen Sondheim's Company opens in London tonight. Robert Cushman interviews the author and promises that Company's successor Follies is even better.

"I THINK IF I were on a desert island and I could see just one musical every night Company would be the one I'd choose." It sounds a ritzy desert island but Stephen Sondheim deserves the best, having authored not only the above remark but the music and lyrics of Company itself. He is by the way an exacting judge of his own work, which now comprises the complete scores of four Broadway musicals, and the lyrics for three to which other men supplied the music. Of these the only other with which he feels totally happy (his previous desert-island choice before Company dislodged it) was Gypsy (his lyrics--Jule Styne's music) which rocked New York in 1959, and has still to reach London. Rumours of its approach are annual; according to Sondheim, this year's has more substance than most. Meanwhile--or as from tonight--we have Company.

What we make of it will be interesting to see since the word has been ever since the show's generally triumphant Broadway opening in 1969 that Company was too much of New York to be relished, or even understood, in London. Sondheim himself has no idea which way the critics will jump. "They could find it a strange and bewildering animal"--and it is a fact that England's gift to the New York Times, Clive Barnes ("He's murdering the American theatre--jovially") hated Company immoderately. It ran for eighteen months, though.

Company had a strange genesis. In the winter of 1968 George Furth, a hyper-energetic actor, decided to try writing plays "to while away the time between classes." He came up with a series of playlets about married couples and sent about seven of them to Sondheim with whom he maintained (he being on the West Coast and Sondheim in Manhattan) "a telephone friendship." Sondheim liked them and was pleased to hear that a production was planned with Kim Stanley playing all the women's parts. When this project fell through Sondheim agreed to show the script to Harold Prince.

This was in January of 1969 when Prince was, as he still is, one of the most powerful and astute of Broadway impresarios who had proved himself (notably with Cabaret) to be unfairly talented as a director as well. That he would present Furth's plays seemed too much to hope; the most Sondheim expected was encouraging advice. Prince gave him considerably more. "Hal asked me if I thought George's plays would make a musical. My first response was that it would be difficult. Those couples weren't singing characters. So we came up with the notion of a third person in each situation--an uncommitted man."

The "third man" had in fact been present in some of Furth's original playlets; he now became the fulcrum of the action, a thirty-five-year-old bachelor alternately amused, perturbed, and bewildered by the activities of, to quote the title lyric, "those good and crazy people, my married friends."

As Furth and Sondheim reworked the material around this new protagonist, the original plays "faded away"; only two of those couples remain in Company (a third has recently surfaced on Broadway in Furth's play Twigs) with three brand-new ones added, along with a trio of girl-friends for bachelor Robert. It was Robert who presented the main writing problem. "It's hard to write a cipher--a man who won't take action and won't commit himself. The show is really about the increasing difficulties of commitment." But here the musical form came to the rescue. Robert reveals little of himself with other people (though we learn a great deal about them from the way they react to him); left alone, he can soliloquise in song.

The advent of Company ended a period of theatrical silence for Sondheim. He had begun in 1957, as auspiciously as any young man could wish, as the lyricist of West Side Story (though he had his doubts then about his lyrics and the entire show; and they have grown with the years); then came the more lasting triumph of Gypsy, clouded only by a fear that two smashes as a lyricist would prevent him ever achieving recognition as a composer. But this too arrived--with A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum (a huge hit) and Anyone Can Whistle (a huge flop). In 1965 there was a moderately successful collaboration with Richard Rodgers's Do I Hear a Waltz?

So what had he been doing since? "Working on Follies. Over twenty drafts. The first one was at the end of 1965." Follies followed Company on to Broadway last year. It was less warmly received and Sondheim himself is less satisfied with it. But seeing them on successive nights in New York just after Christmas I preferred Follies, though Company may well be the tighter show.

Where Company is lean and astringent, Follies is lush and astringent. James Goldman, who wrote the book, presents "a first and last reunion" held by a Ziegfeld figure the night before his theatre is pulled down to make way for a parking lot. The songs and dances of old days are re-enacted; Sondheim has written meticulous pastiches of the words and music of the thirties. At first these work as an end in themselves; as the show progresses they reveal more about the characters who sing them. This is Sondheim's speciality; in sheer facility of expression he rivals the best of his Broadway peers--dramatically he outpaces them all.

I suspect this sometimes makes life a little frustrating for his librettists; a song, after all, being a miniature form, is capable of perfection, while the book of a musical, like a play, is bound to be a messier affair. Sondheim has a knack of pinning down a character or situation so precisely that the audience resents the added efforts of the script. And as Sondheim himself points out, the book-writer has always had a rough time; "The only book that ever got a good press was My Fair Lady and that's because they all knew it was Shaw." Goldman's script for Follies has probably been unfairly treated; it covers a lot of ground in a short space ("it's the shortest book ever except for West Side Story") and it provides a solid framework for an evening of impeccable craftsmanship.

I cannot remember when I last saw a stage put to such satisfying use; Prince has here brought to fruition the theatrical techniques with which he was experimenting in Cabaret. He and Sondheim could well be an unstoppable combination. If I had no other reason for wishing Company well I would do so in the hope that its success might pave the way for a London production of Follies. It's my desert island musical.
