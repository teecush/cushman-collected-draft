Bigots and barons

HECTOR MacMILLAN'S The Sash was first presented by the Pool Theatre at the last Edinburgh Festival but one. It now fetches up a little late at the Hampstead Theatre Club.

The Sash is a displaced Irish play: a Protestant-Catholic conflict, set in Glasgow, with distant echoes of Romeo and Juliet. Towards its close a disaffected Protestant boy and a disillusioned Catholic girl, each living with a rabidly intolerant elder, seem to be reaching some kind of understanding.

Earlier on we get a fuller treatment of the crumbling relationship between the boy and the primly bigoted girl his father wants him to marry, so as to unite the two most zealously Protestant families in the neighbourhood. Dad's attitudes here are not beyond reproach, and young Georgina gives several loud squeals when she finds his hand up her kilt. All the same she seems a much better match for him, a widower, than for his son; at least she is willing and eager to march with him in the Orange Day parade on which the play centres. It is his son's abstention from this yearly outburst of anti-Papist jubilation that sparks off old Bill MacWilliam's bitterest fury. It is the day of his year, and his life centres around its symbols. He even enters drying himself with an orange towel.

Bill is given a towering, roaring performance by Andrew Keir: Scots acting at its strongest. Doreen Cameron's Georgina, pert and blinkered, is similarly archetypal. Her opposite number--the Irish girl from downstairs who has seen too much violence, but has acquired a wider perspective through suffering--is less convincing, perhaps because she comes nearer to having the author's blessing than any other character. Though he may be willing to call down a plague on both their houses, the Protestants get by far the greater share of Mr MacMillan's vitriol. I assume he is avenging himself on his own background; the mildly rebellious son has the look of an author's surrogate.

As domestic drama, The Sash works pretty well, even as domestic comedy. We are in Alf Garnett territory; the more reactionary the characters the more we laugh. Let us hope we are being purged. But the play's naturalism is too limited in scope for us to accept occasional excursions into song and dance or breathy, antiphonal dialogue, designed to heighten our awareness of the major issues. These sadly respond hardly more to miniaturist treatment than to the schematised debating of the previous week's Objections to Sex and Violence.

Perhaps the blame lies less with the material than with our expectations. The reason why new plays are usually less enjoyable than revivals is that we approach the latter knowing what to expect. We go to a new work unwisely hoping, even demanding, to be illumined and/or lacerated, the victims of years of extravagant promises from playwrights, reviewers and publicists. So herewith a warning: The Sash is not bad, but it will not change your life.

* * *

The Royal Shakespeare Company's King John (Aldwych) is now attributed to John Barton rather than, as at Stratford, to Shakespeare. This is honest; though it cannot hide the fact that the few extended bits of Bard remaining are the most gripping in the evening, and the only ones allowing the actors to express anything resembling full humanity. For the rest a rather brittle style (Jokey-Didactic) prevails, suitable to a lengthy morality which merely tells us that Death (who makes a personal appearance in the saturnine figure of Mike Gwilym, this company's most interesting new actor) comes even to kings.

To point this moral any king would serve; and Emrys James needs all his resources to keep us attentive to the fortunes of John, whom he portrays as an unstable clown, falling fecklessly into evil but scarcely deserving the opprobrium he attracts. Denis Holmes lends the evening a little depth as the most scrupulous of the barons; and Ian McKellen is ringingly attractive as the Bastard, whose honesty seems, in this jingling world, such an anachronism. Mr McKellen himself seems somewhat old-fashioned in this context; to put it another way, his is the best acting in the play.
