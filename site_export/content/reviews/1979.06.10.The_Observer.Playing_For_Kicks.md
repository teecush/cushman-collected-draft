Playing for kicks

Theatre
ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Manchester

The penultimate scene of Dispatches (Cottesloe) finds a war-reporter maimed in Vietnam, announcing that he has just received a major commission to write the conflict up. He is required, he says with a mixture of scorn and despair, to take the glamour out of war. 'You might as well,' he screeches, 'try to take the glamour out of sex. You might as well try to take the glamour out of the Rolling Stones.'

This production is here to prove him right, on all counts. It is based on Michael Herr's book, a self-proclaimed amalgam of novel and reportage; though really the only novelistic thing about it was the amount of dialogue it contained, and to that the new journalism should thoroughly have accustomed us. Bill Bryden and his company have added words of their own to the originals, but the joins are hard to see.

There is undeniably something odd about an attempt to put a documentary account of the Vietnam war on to a stage, and particularly on to a British stage. We in Britain have traditionally envied the Americans--envied them most when affecting to despise them; but it comes as a shock to realise that we envy them Vietnam. It was so traumatic, dramatic, cinematic a crisis (we also envy them Watergate); our own seem so squalid and unyielding by comparison.

Dispatches, apart from its obvious technical problems, is rather scrappy material for a play (it's rather scrappy for a book, come to that); but I knew it would infallibly find its way on to a stage. The lure of machismo is irresistible; every stage production is an assertion of power over the audience, and there are few more effective ways of reinforcing this dominance than by the liberal use of loud bangs.

Dispatches offers not only the clamour of modern armaments, but the clamour of modern music. One of the book's theses is that the war and hard rock were symptoms of the same disease ('actually, that's too specific: just say vaguely aspects of the same thing), and the Cottesloe weighs in with a live band.

I am not, by the way, suggesting that the motives of Mr Bryden and company are any more discreditable than are those of all of us (all of us) at work in the self-aggrandisement industry. But the circumstances of this production do make them stand out in unusually sharp relief. There is still a kick to be had from playing soldiers, and there is an additional Walter Mittyish kick for British actors in playing American rock-culture soldiers. In fact their enthusiasm does more than anything in the play itself--or even in the book--to justify the claim made on Mr Herr's dust-cover by Time magazine: that the author shows us that 'war thrives because enough men still love it.' This may just be true for the correspondents, but the soldiers cannot wait to get out; the play's substance is still unexceptionably pacifist.

Mr Bryden's production begins tentatively, but gains steadily in conviction, though the ending is theatrically as well as philosophically inconclusive. William Dudley's sandbagged set makes the scenes of actual conflict seem remarkably credible. (Can this cockpit hold the vasty fields? Yes, it can.) And the actors expand in their scenes of relaxation, generally stoned. Few individuals stand out, though the play tries to keep some character-lines going. The reporters are a notably more pallid crew than in the book (there seems to be no way of relaying the more interesting information about them), but Trevor Ray makes a marvellously solid photographer.

Among the soldiers, a trio of black actors (Oscar James, Don Warrington, Guy Gregory) exactly transmit varieties of resigned or angry desperation; and Oliver Cotton has a good comic solo as a marine panting to get home and avenge himself on an unfaithful wife. The narrator, Jack Shepherd, is beautifully watchful, but loses the flow and the vitality of the writing by trying to make it sound improvised.

* * *

Happy Days are here again; but Samuel Beckett's own production, at the Royal Court, does succeed in being different from anyone else's. There has been considerable rewriting, especially in the first act, most of it amusingly stressing Winnie's impatience with the world in general and her husband in particular. She has moments indeed when she suggests an immured Edna Everage, though this Antipodean connection may have been sparked off in my mind by Billie Whitelaw's delivery of the lady's opening line. Her 'another happy day' was surprisingly like the ironic greeting bestowed on the world by the transvestite hero of The Elocution of Benjamin Franklin: 'Hello, Sydney, I see you've got another lovely day planned for me.'

I am not sure whether Miss Whitelaw's interpretation works out more or less despairing than that of Peggy Ashcroft, who sounded as if she really did expect to have a good time. Miss Whitelaw shows no pleasure or affection until the very last moment, when Willie may or may not be going for her gun. Perhaps it matters little how an actress approaches the role; sooner or later Mr Beckett's strictures will get her; she will stand firmly rebuked for any optimism she may have allowed to escape her lips, and we along with her.

Miss Whitelaw patronises Winnie a little too much when she regards her husband's dirty postcard (Dame Edna again), but she is otherwise prodigiously varied and alive within a vocal range fined purposefully down. For once I did not feel the play's overwhelming statement on the way we compulsively and meaninglessly fill out our days to have been fully made within 10 minutes of curtain-rise. With Miss Whitelaw there is always more in the bag.

Probably no theatre other than the Royal Exchange, Manchester, would risk giving us Hofmannsthal's The Deep Man, a fascinating piece about an Austrian gentleman afflicted, in the dark and brassy days following the First World War, by a deadly realisation of how foolish any human word, any human action, can be. Exquisitely fastidious, he squirms each time any of his contemporaries starts speaking; they are always ready to strike attitudes and to make professions which they believe with all their souls, but which he knows to be ludicrous. He anticipates Christopher Hampton's philanthropist, and much of Simon Gray.

James Maxwell, an actor who sometimes errs on the side of beatitude, this time hits exactly the right note of embarrassed serenity, and Dilys Hamlett gives an enjoyably fussy account of the sister who is faute de mieux, his lifeline to the world. But that world is wretchedly portrayed: Clare Higgins shows promise, and Willoughby Gray and Michael Poole experience, but the rest seem to be vying in ineptitude. Let me for once name names: I thought Daniel Gerroll was awkward until John Somerville came on.

Jonathan Griffin's translation makes nothing easy (and why should it?), but it is composed of English sentences; however formal and convoluted, and the actors should be able to negotiate them sensibly even if they fail to penetrate the European mind beneath. Thanks to Mr Maxwell the idea that underpins the play comes through Casper Wrede's production; thanks to everyone else, the comic action that should expand and modify it is lifeless.

Alan Drury's An Empty Desk (Theatre Upstairs) is a modest but acute play about a local government clerk torn between his office routine, which wants to repress him, and his girl-friend who wants to set him free. (According to her own ideas, anyway; she too strikes attitudes.) Each environment has an act to itself and the upshot is the hero's breakdown.
