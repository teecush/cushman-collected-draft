Drag gag

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN at the 'Danny La Rue Show' and 'Jack and the Beanstalk'.

MUCH money has been spent, but will doubtless be recouped, on the new attractions at London's two premier show shops.

The Danny La Rue Show (Prince of Wales) is a splashier enterprise than Jack and the Beanstalk (Palladium). Neither show is remarkable for imaginative decor, but Berkeley Sutcliffe's creations at the Prince of Wales are more solidly lavish than Tod Kingman's for the Palladium panto. The latter, though, scores valuable points with its all too brief introduction of a large green fire-breathing dragon.

Freddie Carpenter's direction of the La Rue show is superior in terms of sheer efficiency. (One hardly expects artistry in these circumstances, and though one might reasonably demand dynamism it would be more prudent not to.) Here also may be found the better and punchier band: musical director, Derek New. As regards costuming, there is little to choose: Mr La Rue's gowns (designed by Mark Canter, executed by Isolyn) are justly famous, but they are hardly more delightful than the insect casings in which hordes of stage children scuttle fluorescently, if a little aimlessly, across the stage of the Palladium. Both shows are shoved at us through microphones, but whereas the La Rue team have taken the measure of their amplification system, the Palladium gang are nearly overwhelmed by theirs. This pantomime sounds distant and metallic.

Its performers, however, make it in the end the more rewarding entertainment. This is not to deny that all London will flock to see Mr La Rue. He has practically blackmailed us into it by announcing that this is our last chance to see him in drag. After this we must wear La Rue with a difference.

But will we want to? If he carries out his published threat to hang up his tits, he will presumably have to hang up all those dresses alongside them, thus depriving us and himself of the peaks of his acts. (And not just the peaks: think of the valleys.) The evening has very little to offer beyond the breathtaking series of entrances made by Mr La Rue in his multitudinous frocks. Since show-biz propriety demands that some pretext shall be furnished for each disguise, he is forced to dissipate the instant conviction of his appearance as Nell Gwyn, Princess Anne or Carol Channing in some feebly written song or sketch. The sung words are preferable to the spoken, but there is only one joke throughout: phallic double entendre.

Dispensing this material, Mr La Rue reveals himself to be a merely competent gag-man and a powerful but anonymous vocalist. He can do one limited thing supremely well, and is thus a speciality performer, no more suited than an accomplished juggler or trapeze artist to be the king-pin of a revue. (Compare him with Stanley Baxter, a comedian whose female impersonations disclose as much finesse and far more wit, and who can also be very funny in men's wear.) The Danny La Rue Show is monotonous; perhaps a book show, with a satirical plot that might utilise the star's transvestite skills, would have been a better idea.

* * *

One of the merits of Jack and the Beanstalk is that, despite the temptations that commonly beset a pantomime, it never quite loses sight of its story. The perils include the casting of two mid-road pop singers as Jack and his uncommonly tough little Princess; they, who should have the most vested of interests in keeping the action going, are in fact the likeliest of all to halt it. So quick are they to burst into unheralded song that their first duet suggests love before first sight.

The show is rich, however, in comic talent. Even too rich: Alfie Bass, cast as the King, is given nothing whatever to do. Perhaps it is the competition from the La Rue camp that has killed the idea of a male Dame (as of a female Principal Boy), but Dora Bryan makes Jack a good mother, winsome, agile, and very friendly with cows and people. Her Shirley Temple turn is a treat.

Frankie Howerd is the star, and a problem. One senses trouble when the familiar opening "ladies and gentlemen" has to be followed by "boys and girls." This is tampering with a sacred text, and spoils the rhythm besides. Pantomime, and indeed children, circumscribe (no, dear, circumscribe) Mr Howerd; he has to watch his language (though this script hardly repays such attention), but he sticks loyally by the occasion. He regularly sabotages the narrative, as a pantomime comic should, but never undermines the convention. Never, that is, until his final solo spot when he comes into something resembling his own. He rapidly disposes of the dreaded song sheet sequence by taking us phonetically into what purports to be the national anthem of the Common Market (as befits the commonness--let us face it, the dead commonness--of which Mr Howerd can convict all with whom he comes into contact).

Soon he is beseeching us to join in the chorus of the saga of the three little fishes who swam over the dam; yes, he knows we think it's childish. Actually I was more than willing to oblige him since his rendition of this ballad was a cherished part of my own childhood. I regret, though, that he has now shorn it of its tragic conclusion and savagely cautionary moral. In times like these we can hardly afford to shield our children from the harsher facts of life.

* * *

I must postpone until next week proper consideration of The Party, Trevor Griffiths's new play at the National. Let me in the meantime wrap up a few things for Christmas: the Actors' Company have brought their version of R. D. Laing's Knots, seen at the Edinburgh Festival and elsewhere, to the Shaw; while another Edinburgh original, Stanley Eveling's Union Jack (and Bonzo), has reached the Hampstead Theatre Club, whose previous incumbent, Peter Handke's The Ride Across Lake Constance, has transferred to the Mayfair. God bless them, every one.
