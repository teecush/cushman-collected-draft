Hot for certainties

ROBERT CUSHMAN

ARTHUR MILLER'S All My Sons (Wyndham's) is a fit play for 1981, a year distinguished in theatre by revivals and by the re-emergence in strength of middle-aged playwrights.

Miller himself was only 31 at its premiere in 1947 but to us it seems like tradition incarnate: an American family drama with global overtones played out on a Middle West back porch. ('The porch,' as Tynan once said, 'is obligatory.') Joe Keller made money in the war as a manufacturer of airplane parts. He shipped out one consignment of defective cylinder heads, and 21 pilots died flying the planes. Joe's partner, a weakling, went to jail, but he himself got off. Now his worshipping son discovers Joe's guilt; another son was lost in the war; Joe is forced to realise that the dead pilots were 'all my sons.' Exit, to put a bullet in his head.

The play, as everyone has said, is modelled on Ibsen. Ibsenite is the narrative method: a continual reclaiming of the past. Imperfectly Ibsenite, too, is the symbolism of a fallen apple-tree, which serves only to clutter the stage while adding a few lines of portentous dialogue. The avenging son Chris is straight from Ibsen but an Ibsen drained of irony; a carping neighbour arraigns him as an 'idealist' but that idealism passes uncriticised by the author himself.

The play is hot for certainties. Miller convinces us because he convinced himself, both of his technique (we may gag at such devices as the fatal long-withheld letter and wonder about the characterisation of the woman who has concealed it but we still recognise Miller as one of the few great storytellers in modern drama) and of his meaning. He spells that meaning out--'there's a universe of people outside and you're responsible to it'--without diminishing it. Sometimes, his words are awkward, but they carry a self-generating conviction.

And there is something else: something that runs almost counter to the play's declared grain, and in doing so enriches it. Joe's main defence when cornered is that he was acting for the good of his family. Family, declares the play through Chris, is not a man's only responsibility, but it turns out to be the play's main concern. Chris destroys his father out of filial disappointment; he had loved and honoured him too much. (This theme, like much else in the play, was to be more fully and subtly treated in Death of a Salesman.)

In fact there are two families involved. Chris's fiancee is the daughter of Joe's partner; her brother arrives on the scene to smash the engagement, convinced that his old man has been victimised. But he is almost charmed out of his enmity by the massed hospitality of the Kellers, above all by Joe's wife Kate, whom the stage directions refer to throughout as Mother.

In Michael Blakemore's production this whole movement (more Chekhov than Ibsen) is subtly, dynamically handled with Richard Durden, an ominous young actor, first steely, then melting, then snapping tight again. His sister (an even-trickier role, composed of moods but not much character) is given firm definition by Jill Baker, last seen in Goose Pimples, being equally good and entirely different. Garrick Hagon as Chris bears the brunt of the play's gaunt self-righteousness; by refusing to soften it, by accepting its ungainliness, and by making it part of his character and even of his stance, he turns a dramatic fault into a virtue.

Colin Blakely's Joe may be physically smaller than Mr Miller imagined; part of his armour is a continually expressed pained pity for his colleague, 'a little man'; Joe has tried to re-create himself; he trades on joviality and kindliness which Mr Blakely shows to be almost real. The density of Joe's characterisation is the play's other great strength. He believes simultaneously that his crime never happened and that it was justified. Mr Blakely, maybe the most honest actor we have, is ideally equipped both to root out dishonesty and to make us all feel our kinship with it; and when his disintegration sets in it rolls relentlessly.

And there is Mother, even more adept than her husband at shutting out unpleasant truths; without her, in fact, he couldn't do it. In some respects Rosemary Harris is wrong for the role but her wrongness is pretty wonderful. Instead of bovine devotion she offers a strained intelligence breaking under a continuous (and self-contradictory) system of lying. If cover-up is the great American theme here is one of its pristine manifestations, making the play a companion-piece to Long Day's Journey Into Night. Vocally this performance is superbly fastidious; physically it is full, graceful and economic, creating its own white light. Miss Harris is Britain's great lost actress; there should be legislation to stop her returning to America at the end of the run.

Some of the neighbours overplay the bonhomie commonly thought inseparable from American backyards; otherwise there are no flaws. Hayden Griffin's set is colour-co-ordinated; the green of the trees reflected in the handsome house they frame.

* * *

Hanif Kureishi's Borderline (Royal Court) is the best young play of the year. It confirms the impression that Asian writers are taking over from the Irish as the creative rescuers of the English language. Offering a small panorama of immigrant and second-generation life in London, he is angry, despairing, compassionate, witty and charming. His people sound (as they did not in his last play Outskirts) spontaneous and authentic. Radical activists and frightened nostalgic parents are both accorded care and attention.

The production (by Max Stafford-Clark, for Joint Stock) is not always as considerate as the writing: patronage creeps in, and petulance, and that strange over-emphasis (between a whine and a hammer) which actors under 30 now promiscuously bestow on neutral, inoffensive lines. But the staging is visually sure, and there are two or three very good performances. Two British actors, David Beames and Deborah Findlay, have great sad authority as an old Pakistani couple and of the Asian players a total newcomer, Nizwar Karanj, is a nervous delight in the role of a total newcomer.

* * *

In Favourite Nights (Lyric, Hammersmith) Stephen Poliakoff ups his range of urban squalor to include soulless hotels and gambling casinos; also, as a sort of antechamber to the above, language schools for visiting businessmen, the teachers apparently doubling as hostesses. Once again he lets the decay seep through; he remains very good at tone. But he is presently hopeless on narrative; we spend the first half of the evening wondering who and what the play is really about and the second feeling cheated at having found out.

It seems that the Jewish father and the young girl and the Austrian gent (Peter Postlethwaite perfect) were not, despite the great length of their roles, important in themselves but only foils to their daughter-sister-teacher who is also a compulsive gambler and a symptom, presumably, of her self-destructive society. Susan Tracy gives her an appropriate sullen glitter, Gwyneth Strong supports her brightly, and a croupier is played by Marion Bailey, last seen in Goose Pimples, being equally good and entirely the same.

Peter James seems to lose his director's grip with this author; not only does the dialogue meander but the scene-changes are interminable. This, of all plays, might benefit from a revolving stage; baize tables and slot machines rotating towards and away from us might perk things up, and even be meaningful.
