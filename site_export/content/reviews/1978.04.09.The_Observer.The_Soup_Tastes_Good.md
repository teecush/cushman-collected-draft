The soup tastes good

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a Wesker revival and a new Ayckbourn.

IN THE FIFTIES Arnold Wesker's Chicken Soup with Barley (Shaw) made a terrific impact by presenting on stage people who talked seriously about politics. We are used now to seeing people who talk seriously of nothing else; the rare compulsion of the play at this date is that they really are people -- and the real treat, that Mr Wesker writes without a trace of contempt for them, his audience or himself.

The personal appeal of the play now far outweighs the polemical. At its centre is Sarah Kahn, an East End Jewish matriarch and a lifelong Communist; we eavesdrop on her, her family and their friends over a 20-year span, from the invasion of Whitechapel by Mosley's Blackshirts to that of Hungary by Khrushchev's tanks. Sarah is the only person on the stage whose commitment survives this last event: a fidelity made possible through ignorance. We are informed that she never read a word of economic theory all her life; someone told her that socialism meant love and she believed them.

This credulity seems, on one level, admirable; on another it is just foolish. And Sarah's folly both enriches and damages the play. The damage comes when Mr Wesker, pulling himself together and remembering where he is, seems -- as in Sarah's ringing curtain line, 'If you don't care, you'll die' -- to put his full weight behind her.

The enrichment is more pervasive, and it fills the play with ambiguities. For Sarah's husband, Harry, has emphatically not cared; and he, if not quite physically dead, is a mental and spiritual corpse. Having suffered a couple of strokes, he is a shambling, incontinent wreck. Whether his wife's enthusiastic bullying -- which she offers as proof of how she fought all her life -- has propelled him to this point, or whether he would have reached it anyway, is left an open question.

She emerges as a particularly fascinating type of the woman who loves humanity at the expense of those near her. Not that she neglects her family: her charity does not only begin at home, it is rooted there. It is very practical; at almost every moment of the play food is being cooked or eaten or a kettle brewed. But it is a charity fatally divorced from tolerance.

So her friends, while respecting her, are glad to get away -- geographically and, as their faith crumbles, politically. Her daughter goes the same way. Her son -- who seems in his awkward posturing to represent the author's revenge on his younger self -- wonders repeatedly which of his parents he takes after.

* * *

A number of younger playwrights might have imposed a neater ideological pattern on this story but they could not have given us the Kahns. Anthony Cornish's production is journeyman work in some ways (people keep going on strange exploratory walks round their own livingroom), but, through the performances of Barbara Young and Martin Friend, it releases the play's real strength. Jack Chissick, who ages particularly well, stands out among the comrades.

There is actually a clearer political polarisation visible in Alan Ayckbourn's Ten Times Table (Globe). The opposition in Chicken Soup is kept off-stage; Mr Ayckbourn offers a Tory lady, a young Marxist schoolteacher and several indeterminates between, all of them sitting on a committee to organise a village festival. However, the play is literally a departure. Having explored kitchen, bedroom, livingroom and garden shed, Mr Ayckbourn has finally left home.

The journey has done little to impair his faculties. The meetings are faultlessly done, the absentee members almost as vivid as those present. The festival hinges on the re-enactment of a local legend: the massacre 200 years ago of the Pendon Twelve, the village's own Peterloo. Each faction espouses its own side of the cause; at the close the peasantry, clad in new home-made jerkins, take on the establishment, led by a drunken Earl of Dorset unsteadily mounted on a hobby horse (in private life the earl is in electrical goods).

The farce of the last scene is a let-down after the preceding high, or at least uppish, comedy; a reversal of the situation at the Scarborough premiere last year, where the company, though expert and enthusiastic, were often too young for their roles. Two who were not have been brought to London and more than repay the trust: Christopher Godwin as a brusquely demented dog-breeder, a Mad Mitch of the North Riding, and Diane Bull as the teacher's self-effacing lady who converses mainly in mumbles. (This part has been beautifully observed.)

The London contingent -- recruited from the middle-brow, middle-weight, middle-income actors for whom Mr Ayckbourn has done so much and vice versa -- are not all so exciting; Benjamin Whitrow and Tenniel Evans give, by their own standards, routine performances and Julia McKenzie is cast not so much against type as against form; her success in Side by Side by Sondheim came in emotional rather than comic numbers and her best moment here is a breakdown.

Paul Eddington, however, radiates well-modulated bewilderment from the chair, Stephanie Fayerman is thoroughly convincing as a turncoat, and Mateylock Gibbs, dealt all the best lines as a deaf but spirited old lady, plays them excellently to the accompaniment of a brilliantly benevolent beam.

* * *

Last year, when reviewing Illuminatus! I remarked that its success confounded most of the arguments against transferring novels to the stage; all you needed was time. I didn't know that I was being inspirational; apparently, though, I confirmed Mike Alfreds, director of Shared Experience, in his determination to bring Bleak House (Theatre Upstairs) to the stage in four full-length instalments. On the evidence of the first I can only thank Mr Alfreds for proving me so right and himself and his company so talented. The secret of the enterprise lies in the group's name; having enjoyed the novel, they pass their pleasure on to us. I shall explain how when I have seen the cycle entire.

In the meantime I merely recommend and pass on to Manchester, where the old dramatising method -- gallons into pint pot -- has been followed with Crime and Punishment (Royal Exchange). To the credit of Paul Bailey, his piece is a play, rather than a series of snippets. Each short scene has shape and momentum.

But there are crucial failings; Mr Bailey eschews soliloquy, so we are denied an insight into Raskolnikov's mind until the murder is done, after which everyone starts explaining it to us, too late for us to take an interest. Then there is the playing. The magnificent Leo McKern is on hand as the investigating inspector (a ministering as well as an avenging angel), but Tom Courtenay brays excessively as Raskolnikov.

Michael Elliott has perhaps the finest moral sense of any director in the English theatre but he seems compelled to express it through spineless actors and weepy actresses. Perhaps it is unfair to pick on the good prostitute Sonya; she is an impossible character, but Shared Experience managed to bring the priggish Esther Summerson alive and even likeable. It is easy of course to leaven Dickens with humour while leaving the nightmare intact. No one, certainly not Mr Elliott, would try it with Dostoevsky?
