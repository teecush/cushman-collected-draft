Singin' on the stage

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a 'new' musical at the Palladium

Tommy Steele: A lack of iron and irony.

I FIRST heard Tommy Steele on the subject of Singin' in the Rain in an interview. He quoted the Gene Kelly line, "Dignity, dignity, always with dignity." That, he said, was his own motto as a performer. What he did not mention was that in the film we hear those words from Mr Kelly while watching him get a custard pie in the face.

That interview was a long time ago, and somebody has obviously explained the joke to Mr Steele in the meantime. At the Palladium, in the stage version which he has directed and in which he plays the Kelly role, he delivers the line with a hearty appreciation of its absurdity. He is actually over-emphatic about it; and that goes for much of the production.

Singin' in the Rain is the best film musical ever made, but it is far from the biggest. There is not a full chorus number in it until the Broadway Ballet near the end. True, a dancing girl emerges from a cake to be joined by other dancing girls in a quick shimmy, but neither the dance nor the cake are made a meal of. They are thrown away. But it is difficult to throw a dance number away on a stage, and practically impossible at the Palladium. Harold Fielding, the producer, would probably have anyone who tried it arrested.

The best things are the simplest: a buck-and-wing by Mr Steele and Roy Castle to the tune of Moses Supposes, and Mr Steele's wooing of Danielle Carson with You Are My Lucky Star, a relaxed routine on a smoke-draped stage. The first is funny and athletic, the second funny and romantic.

The original Singin' in the Rain is one of the few musical comedies to deserve both halves of the name. Devised by producer-lyricist Arthur Freed as a tribute to his own song catalogue, it was transformed by its writers, directors and performers into a unity. Mischief infects the love-story of silent-movie star Don Lockwood and ingenue Kathy Selden; music supports the satire on the birth of talkies.

The scene in which the dumb, scheming female star Lina Lamont grapples with a microphone concealed under foliage ("I can't make love to a bush") is as hilarious in the theatre as in the cinema: a tribute to screen writers Betty Comden and Adolph Green, whose giddy dialogue is retained word for word, just as the interpolated clips of Lockwood and Lamont's last silent and first talkie have been reshot frame for frame, and the celebrated choreography of the title-song and the Good Morning trio is duplicated step for step.

Nothing that money and basic competence can do to reproduce the images of film on a stage is lacking. Minor roles have been cast and costumed by Identikit. Sarah Payne's Lina is a very funny replica of Jean Hagen's performance (which was even funnier) in the movie. Where changes have been made, one feels that they were a last resort; who knows how long Messrs Steele and Castle wrestled in rehearsal with American accents before it was decided to allow them the line, "We were kids together in England." (Actually the idea of alien Britishers amid the Hollywood corn works well.) Mr Castle brings off, live before our very eyes, every pratfall, every crash, every somersault of a routine that must have cost Donald O'Connor dozens of takes. It's wonderful. So why isn't it wonderful?

Mainly, because it's too slow. And so are the fade-outs, and the timing of the dialogue, and the story. The tempo of the film is stoked up by the play. And this brings us up against the undodgeable, unanswerable question: Why bother? Why try to reproduce--not reinterpret--a masterpiece that is already permanently there, smiling?

Of course, something in us wants to applaud a good imitation; one is awed at the thought of Mr Steele getting very wet, six nights a week, and being squirted by a fire hydrant at the right remembered moment. But even at this level it has to fail: the pulse of the number depends on camera angles as well as choreography.

Gene Kelly's numbers are great because they are an expression of Gene Kelly. Troupe though he is, Mr Steele is not Mr Kelly: not as a dancer, not as an actor. Mr Kelly grinned a lot, but Mr Steele grins all the time. He deprives the entertainment of its iron and its irony. My heart was lifted only once: at the curtain-call, when the principals came on arm-in-arm, singing the title-song: just like in the movie.

One of the things whose origin is delightfully spoofed in Singin' in the Rain is the dubbed singing voice, a filmic practice that has recently begun to plague the theatre: vide that other transplant, Bugsy Malone, and now Y, the long cursed spectacular that has finally opened at the Piccadilly, now transformed into a rather taking dinner-theatre, complete with performing waiters.

This means that you can avenge yourself on the man who poured champagne all over you by watching him look uncomfortable in a rouged, smirking chorus-line. The food is better than expected, and the show itself is not quite the prophesied disaster. The costumes are great and the corps execute a terrific charleston (charlestons tend to be terrific), preceded by something they call the Black Bottom; London's intended answer to the Crazy Horse has not got its accents sorted out.

A modest decadence is the apparent aim (men cracking whips at women, that sort of thing), but it is often scuppered by inadequate performers, numbers that fail to be sexy because they have no climax, and a structure that is arbitrary even by floor-show standards. "I've got some news for you," says the head-waiter, in English unfortunately. "We're going to Venice"; and suddenly we are into an eighteenth-century soft-porn number.

One of the Venetian ladies creates a sensation by opening her mouth in song, and the men act shocked. Well they might, for out of it is coming the voice of Barbra Streisand. This is not a joke, understand; we are meant to be impressed. Just occasionally a lady brandishes a microphone, and may be assumed to be doing her own chirping.

The star turns are Suzy Casabianca, a semi-stripper, who can dance, and Arturo Brachetti. He can dance too; he can be his own partner in a tango, since he nonchalantly combines the skills of illusionist and female impersonator; so nonchalantly that he is in danger of disappearing even when that is not the point of the trick.

* * *

Ghelderode's Escorial is not a play you expect to encounter twice in a lifetime, but I caught it (at the Grove, Hammersmith) for the second time this month. It blends King Lear with Macbeth: a monarch, hearing that the queen, my lord, is dying, goes into a role-reversal act with his jester. It provokes portentous playing.

It is coupled, in Michael Batz's production, with Lorca's bliss, officially The Love of Don Perlimplin and Belisa in the Garden, a light-footed and compassionate study of infatuated cuckoldry, a comic theme brought with superb delicacy to tragedy. The staging is the kind that assumes a play to be automatically the richer for having its actors' faces covered in white paint and all its lines whispered, intoned or sneered. Mr Batz plays leads in both pieces; his Don Perlimplin is passable, but his fool counts as gross directorial self-indulgence.

There is a more effective use of stylisation in Tennessee Williams's Suddenly Last Summer (New End); eerie, insistent music and white-net curtains hung with trailing plants set the tone, and for this piece--the Williams that moves from frozen daiquiris to cannibalism--the tone is right.
