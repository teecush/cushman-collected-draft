Reviewing the '70s

The 1970s saw the start of the nostalgia boom; some Shakespeare, Brecht and Dennis Potter; Tinker, Tailor, Soldier, Spy; and the rise of the TV novel. ROBERT CUSHMAN looks back on a decade of memorable television drama.

BBC television drama entered the 70s with its eye firmly fixed on the past. I don't know if nostalgia can be prophetic but I'd like to believe that it was already entering its fight for survival. For New Year's Day 1970 brought us episode one of The Six Wives of Henry VIII, the series whose immense success pointed the way to costume drama at home and prestige sales (to American educational networks mostly) abroad. For those who have forgotten what they learned either at school or on BBC2, Henry's first was Catherine of Aragon, played by Annette Crosbie. And just to show you how the wind was blowing, playing on BBC1 was a re-run of The Forsyte Saga.

Series were rampant: Z Cars and Softly Softly of course (I never really could get used to the idea of them co-existing) and Doomwatch, the one about technology getting out of hand and killing people. Marlowe's Edward II were brought to the screen, after whom Derek Jacobi was tipped to become the next middle-class hero. Tom Stoppard's Professional Foul was magnificent.

1972's modern classic serial was Man of Straw with Derek Jacobi. Shakespeare's Richard II and Edward II were brought to the screen, after which the costumed past led to the Pre-Raphaelites being given their own series; they called it The Love School. Roots came, and should have conquered.

The admirable Miriam Margolyes (self-described as "fat and intellectually glamorous") was Muriel Spark's Girls of Slender Means. David Hare's only play to date to take a subject and follow it through was Licking Hitler. Dennis Potter had style. Jim Allen's Days of Hope had integrity, and Hardy's The Mayor of Casterbridge was carved into seven episodes and the 30s of The Glittering Prizes. Poldark arrived.

And 1975's most memorable play was Through the Night, Trevor Griffiths's account of a woman with breast cancer. When the Boat Comes In came in in 1976, and stayed; even Tyneside depression could provide nostalgia-fodder. So could 1950s Cambridge and its graduates' subsequent careers; there was more than nostalgia to Frederic Raphael's six-parter The Glittering Prizes but never really came together.

Peter Terson's The Fishing Party inaugurated an affectionate cycle of plays about small masculine pleasures, and Alan Bridges directed Dennis Potter's pivotal Follow the Yellow Brick Road, a play about death drawn from the author's own experiences in hospital. Peter Ustinov narrating and Galton and Simpson writing failed to make Clochemerle funny. Jack Rosenthal's Bar Mitzvah Boy was the nearest thing I can remember to a really funny TV play. 1976 had two Dennis Potter plays: the best, most personal Double Dare and the most celebrated Where Adam Stood.

Brecht's The Gangster Show: the Resistible Rise of Arturo Ui, directed by Jack Gold with Nicol Williamson playing Hitler-as-Capone, took us into 1973 with its fine performances from Michael Bryant, Daniel Massey and Georgia Brown. Television has never served a modern classic better.

Sartre's The Roads to Freedom, the serialisation of Elaine Morgan's A Picture of Katherine Mansfield and A Pin to See the Peepshow showed how fine craft could still be applied to literary and historical material. After a re-run of Six Wives came Elizabeth R, chiefly memorable for establishing the bankability of Glenda Jackson. Shakespeare, who had been contributing one-offs to the BBC for years, was engaged for a series called The Complete Works. He got, on the whole, bad notices. The drama event of the year was Holocaust; the objections raised to it were for the most part extraordinarily frivolous.

In Shaw's The Apple Cart, shown in 1975, Peter Barkworth played the Prime Minister; today he'd be the King. Our appetite for the well-costumed past also gave us Casanova, which launched Frank Finlay as a sex-symbol - but nobody called it escapist. Colditz had caught on in 1972, but Return to Colditz didn't. Colin Welland wrote television's definitive strike drama in Leeds United!, given the semi-documentary treatment by director Roy Battersby and producer Ken Trodd.

1979 is too close to be nostalgic, or even retrospective, about. At the close of the decade the original single play seems more beleaguered than at its start. Fashion and economy both conspire against it, and the rise of the "TV novel" - the cycle of plays by a single writer - is another threat. During the 60s the theatre envied television its writers; now the traffic is the other way.
