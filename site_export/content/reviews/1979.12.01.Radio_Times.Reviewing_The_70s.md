Reviewing the '70s

BBC television drama entered the 70s with its eye firmly fixed on the past. I don't know if nostalgia can be prophetic but I'd like to believe that it was a charmed Entering this time. For New Year's Day universe 1970 brought us episode one of The Six Wives of Henry VIII, the series whose immense success pointed the way to custume drama at home and prestige sales (to American educational networks mostly) abroad. But probably the most impressive dramatic achievement of the year was the serialisation of Sartre's The Roads to Freedom, with its fine performances from Michael Bryant, Daniel Massey and Georgia Brown. Television has never served a modern classic better.

After a re-run of Six Wives came Elizabeth R, chiefly memorable for establishing the bankability of Glenda Jackson. It was a Scandinavian period for ace director Alan Bridges first Bergman's The Lie (for which he won the STTA award) then in 1971 Ibsen's The Wild Duck with Denholm Elliott and Derek Godfrey. Bridges also directed Dennis Potter's Traitor; an untoppable partnership. Edna the Inebriate Woman won applause for its leading actress Patricia Hayes and its author Jeremy Sandford; it made his Cathy Come Home of the 60s seem like child's play. There was a Merchant of Venice with Maggie Smith and Frank Finlay. There was no keeping the Tudors down - even the uncharismatic Henry VII was allowed his fling in Shadow of the Tower. And there was a production of Hedda Gabler with Janet Suzman in a role she would play more successfully in the theatre. Ian McKellen brought his greatly underrated Hamlet to the screen, Coral Browne flourished in Lady Windermere's Fan and, if any actress could play Shaw's Millionairess, it was Maggie Smith.

The BBC embarked on Tolstoy's War and Peace. Peter Terson's The Fishing Party inaugurated an affectionate cycle of plays about small masculine pleasures, and Alan Bridges directed Dennis Potter's pivotal Follow the Yellow Brick Road a play about death drawn from the author's own experiences in hospital. Fine craftsperson Elaine Morgan dramatised the Bywaters-Thompson murder case in A Pin to See the Peepshow, and meanwhile Barlow and Watt had graduated (or been demoted) to investigating Jack the Ripper.

Kisses at Fifty was Colin Welland's best play and in the leading role Bill Maynard came in from the cold. While Colditz had caught on in 1972, 1974's The Pallisers didn't. Colin Welland wrote television's definitive strike drama in Leeds United!, given the semi-documentary film treatment by director Roy Battersby and producer Ken Trodd. The unlikeliest historical series of all was launched - Churchill's People, virtually a blank cheque for its writers. In Shaw's The Apple Cart, shown in 1975, Peter Barkworth played the Prime Minister; today he'd be the King. At the close of the decade the original single play seems even more beleaguered than at its start. Fashion and economy both conspire against it, and the rise of the TV novel' - the cycle of plays by a single writer - is another threat. During the 60s the theatre envied television its writers; now the traffic is the other way.
