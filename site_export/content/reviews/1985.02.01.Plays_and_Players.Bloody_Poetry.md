Bloody Poetry

A few years ago Shelley was reclaimed for the revolution and made intellectually respectable. We discovered that the bits of him we had read at school were quite unrepresentative; he was the poet of fiery protest, not of nature rambles. From this newly received wisdom Howard Brenton takes off in Bloody Poetry, a play boldly and fully conceived but unevenly executed. I assume that he wrote the programme notes as well as the play. Anyway there, alone with a photograph that makes Mr Brenton look (appropriately in this new Eng Lit arena) more like Simon Gray than ever, is the assertion that 'with, for some, the exception of John Milton, he (Shelley) is the greatest poet in the language after Shakespeare.

That 'after' is presumably meant to be chronological; otherwise, what about Chaucer? And other some might put in a plea for Donne or Keats or (though I can hardly speak for them) Wordsworth. And there was Byron. Byron shares the limelight with Shelley in Bloody Poetry. They are seen with their women (Shelley's women, really, but one of them is shared) roaming the more picturesque parts of Europe in their last years. Byron is within sight of his Grecian death at the final curtain; Shelley has already drowned. Byron the cynical rake, with no apparent principles to flourish, gives his life for freedom; though of course the struggle may for him be merely an antidote to boredom.

Shelley, the conscious ideologue, far away from home at the time of the Peterloo massacre, gets as far as commemorating it in the biting, stirring lines of The Mask of Anarchy': recited in the play to great effect. But what is a poet supposed to do anyway? The question and its possible answers - in varying shades of praise and blame, guilt and exultation - chase each other round the stage as they must have done for years in Mr Brenton's own head. Lines cross. Bryon attacks the verse of Wordsworth (though he asks to do the job less vivaciously in this play than in his own Don Juan); Shelley, Wordsworth's sworn political enemy, defends the work because it sings'.

Unlike most so-called dialectical plays this one does not bank its argument in neatly parcelled cases for prosecution and defence. The issues here dart and glint like kingfishers: much helped by being posed, in Roland Rees's production, across the aquamarined screens of Poppy Mitchell's set whose dazzling starkness sets off the flowing line of Sheelagh Killeen's costumes. Felicitous tableaux are the playwright's as well as the designers'; much is made of these Romantics' disregard of their women and children; Shelley's wife Harriet, having gone prettily and melodiously mad at the beginning of the second act, corporeally haunts the rest of the action. It sounds a solemn touch but is actually a mischievous one. All the same, both play and production could use more wit*.

The ideas are beginning to be exhausted in the second half, and the author has increasing recourse to straight-faced biography. But he has not established the characters firmly enough for this; he is more interested in what they can be made to stand for than in what they are. The play is closer to Travesties than to Total Eclipse, and it even boasts a Henry Carr figure: Polidori who follows the bloody poets around memoiring 1 185 Howard Brenton's Bloody Poetry at Hampstead with Jane Gurnett and Fiona Shaw directed by Roland Rees. them and relishing the reflected glory. To do this he has to be a fair masochist. Only a Stoppard - not that there is more than one — would make the impotent onlooker endearing: Mr Brenton, though not a cruel writer, is a hard one and Polidori is a humiliated character.

William Gaminara plays him admirably. Acting is generally good and, in one instance, outstanding. Fiona Shaw, having spent a year at the National breathing life into Julia in The Rivals (and doing, inexplicably, nothing else there), presents Mary Shelley as a warm, lofty bluestocking able to suffer, to laugh and to attack. Miss Shaw, with a Georgian plum in her voice, is sense and sensibility confined: actually the nearest our stage has come to an embodiment of the complete works of Jane Austen. Bloatedness is the note of James Aubrey's Byron (it does inevitably seem to be the Simon Callow role), tense piping high-mindedness that of Valentine Pelka's Shelley. Obviously Mr Brenton's favourite, Percy Bysshe is both judged more harshly and indulged more generously than George Gordon Noel, Lord.

Tastes differ. I went into the theatre believing Bryon to be the wittiest versifier in English, and Shelley a windy sentimentalist, endlessly parading the sensitivity of his own soul, and responsible for foisting the figure of the Poet on the world. I came out still believing it.
