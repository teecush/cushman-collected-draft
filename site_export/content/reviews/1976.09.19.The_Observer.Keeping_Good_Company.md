Keeping good company

MANCHESTER, a city fortunate in its public buildings, has developed the noble habit of housing theatres inside them. The town rep can be found downstairs in the public library; now the Royal Exchange - disused as such since 1968 - houses a company which should be among the best in the land.

At its head is Michael Elliott, a stage and television director who commands a respect almost unique; I have never heard a professional word against him. He and his associate Caspar Wrede ran the 59 Theatre Company which, in the year of its name, made history at the Lyric, Hammersmith, and incidentally provided me with one of the most valuable pieces of my theatrical education; the lucidity and the humanity of the productions, and the steady dynamism of the acting, made it the first ensemble I had seen, and still among the best. It predated the National and the RSC, and its choice of plays still shames them: it introduced us, right off the bat, to Buchner's Danton's Death and Ibsen's Brand.

The same group came together for the last pre-National season at the Old Vic (Mr Elliott having also directed the unforgettable Vanessa Redgrave As You Like It at Stratford) and reformed, a decade after its inception, as the 69 Company, playing in various Mancunian strongholds: the University, the Cathedral and a tent pitched in the same Exchange where they have now erected what they call their "module" and which looks indeed very like a spaceship: an aspiring glass-and-steel heptagon, designed purely for acting and watching plays.

All interval activities are confined to the surrounding Great Hall, which constitutes the most spacious theatre foyer I have entered. The relation of foyer to auditorium is greatly enriched by their reciprocal visibility.

The stage is in the round (still a rare form in this country) and unusual in playing to three levels of spectators; the encircling galleries above give it - what everybody seems to be after this year - an Elizabethan look. In all, the combination of building and personnel raises expectation to an extraordinary level. This week's two opening productions, however, leave me still anticipating.

Clever though it is, it shreds the comedy, which is underplayed, though over-directed throughout; the characters are one-joke creations, and though it may be the actors' prerogative to humanise them, the outlines need to be firmly drawn first.

Patricia Routledge's Mrs Malaprop is no dragon but a quivering jelly which melts before it is properly set, and Susan Tracy's Lydia has difficulties with the Languish. Trevor Peacock's Acres is an energetic routine lumpkin, Christopher Gable makes a cheerful Jack, and James Maxwell, one of the original heroes of 59, executes some nice gradations of parental tyranny which his gentleness as an actor deprives of conviction. Tom Courtenay as the self-tormenting Falkland has the most command, but even he stops disappointingly short. It seemed a long evening.

The second offering, Heinrich von Kleist's The Prince of Homburg, is more interesting but scarcely more felicitous. Visually, in fact, it is a disaster; one accepts that on this stage actors may be masked by other actors, but to hem them in with designers' paraphernalia seems perverse. It is also, in this case, hideous; sets and props are a pattern of squared-off cylinders, as indeed are the costumes, which at close quarters (as in this theatre we are all supposed to be) are uncomfortable for both parties.

In the first half of Mr Wrede's production, when the stage is full of campaigning troops, it is often difficult to tell what is going on; the second act does sharpen considerably, though there is still an over-plus of throatily hopeful acting in the commissioned ranks of the Prussian army.

The Prince of Homburg is the kind of European classic one imagines being tossed around at planning meetings of the National and the RSC and guiltily forgotten; in staging its British premiere (it was written in 1811), the Royal Exchange Company is honouring its best traditions. The play is a heroic comedy, alternately reminiscent of Measure for Measure, Arms and the Man and A Midsummer Night's Dream.

The Prince, bewitched by visions of military glory and his general's niece, leads a triumphant cavalry charge which happens to be against orders. The Elector - a wise and crinkly performance by Mr Maxwell, but subject again to iron deficiency - condemns him to death, but grants him a pardon on condition he requests it. This the Prince, after one attack of funk, declines to do, and he is saved only when the Elector is convinced by his officers that the Prince's heroics are actually his responsibility - which one takes to mean that of the entire military caste and its ethic.

The play ends with the Prince raised shoulder-high by his companions and blinking in bewilderment, a mood which finds Mr Courtenay at his most communicative. He has made a mannerism of economy the way some actors do of excess.

This complex play, with more curves than I have mentioned, feels like a masterpiece. I honour the company for its choice and the philosophy underlying it, but both this and The Rivals (also concerned, satirically, with love and honour) demand a rigorous re-establishment of convention if they are to work on a new stage. Physical closeness does not always mean intimacy; I could have tripped the actors up in The Prince of Homburg, but I felt a long way from the play.

* * *

A rather more recent German work, Peter Handke's They Are Dying Out (Young Vic), shows us a business king, aptly named Quitt, who, by breaking trading agreements, ruins his competitors. What, they wonder, has got into him; and the answer, roughly, is Life. He notices things; his conversation is crammed with discrete phenomena, and his awareness of them breaks him down.

Unfortunately for our concentration, everybody else in the play talks in the same way, down to a small shareholder who disrupts AGMs like Judy Holliday in The Solid Gold Cadillac. William Russell, in the taxing role of Quitt, looks taxed; Paul Imbusch, a fat tycoon, and Martin Friend, a thin one, have bite, but not enough to make the words into swords. For his director, Handke needs a showman, but in John Russell Brown he gets a scholar; Handke plus Brown, all mind, makes no matter.

* * *

Denis Cannan, a neglected playwright who started in the early fifties (which is surely forgivable), has a new play, Dear Daddy, which London should see, though not necessarily in the production on view at the Oxford Playhouse. I caught it, admittedly, at a deserted Friday matinee when everybody except Phyllis Calvert (unaccustomedly besotted and bedraggled) imposed West End rhythms, with which the play has nothing to do, even though it deals with a middle class man of letters who has failed professionally, maritally (twice) and paternally. Mr Cannan impales two generations of selfishness; his material is family rituals and family memories, understood but never indulged.
