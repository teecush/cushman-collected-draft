Variations on a theme

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Song and Dance' and 'Boogie.'

IN New York a week ago I was told by a producer that he could slam Victor Borge into a Broadway house for three weeks, make a profit and enliven the season to boot. He seemed depressed about it; no reflection on Mr Borge, but this was not quite what he thought of as theatre.

The West End scene is currently brighter; all the same, one can feel managers casting feverishly about for stop-gaps. The only gloomy thing about Song and Dance is that it springs from the financial necessity that nothing by Andrew Lloyd Webber should go unstaged. Mr Webber has left lying around a recorded song-cycle and a set of variations on a theme by Paganini. So into the Palace they go.

Mr Webber does his own orchestrations which are even brighter than those for 'Cats.' He is especially generous with solo lines, which the musicians, under the skilled direction of Harry Rabinowitz (God, I never expected to see Mr Rabinowitz; I always thought he lived in a radio) fairly lap up; his music certainly has dramatic punch, but it always sounds like pastiche, never quite authentic. It is also, for all its jazz and rock rhythms and its operatic upholstering, curiously dry.

Actually he has never been freer than in the avowedly imitative 'Variations,' where the music combines with the dancing of Wayne Sleep and his acrobatic cohorts to tilt us out of our seats. Anthony Van Laast's choreography is fiery and funny, its execution still more so. Those of us who don't normally get to see much ballet were exhilarated; and relieved to find our enthusiasm shared by the buffs.

At least I think those were buffs, cheering every time someone described a circle very fast; they out-shouted even the musical fans and made the legit audience seem pallid indeed. Suppose we were to erupt every time Judi Dench smiled or Alan Howard hit a high note? (Though actually, at the last matinee of 'The Forest' at the Aldwych, the house accorded Mr Howard and Richard Pasco some furious bravoes; my wife commented--and I can't argue with her--that it's enough for actors to over-act without the spectators doing it as well.)

The song half, 'Tell Me on a Sunday,' is the story of an English girl whose heart is successively broken by a California movie-man, a young New Yorker and a married New Yorker; in between times, she writes to mum in Muswell Hill. For linking passages Mr Webber writes buttressed recitative for which his lyricist, Don Black, is hard-pressed to find not so much the right words as the right syllables; the stresses are continually misplaced.

Both partners are happier in the set numbers: a bouncy satiric piece about Los Angeles life-styles called 'Capped Teeth and Caesar Salad,' an ingenious threnody to man number one ('Sheldon Bloom'), more sardonically reprised for number three ('Married Man'); the words here are full of assimilated detail. The title-song, a touching plea to be let down gently, rightly suggests that Sunday in New York (no wonder there are so many songs about it) is unique, unlike the place any other day or the day any other place.

Marti Webb sings full and well and gives the piece theatrical weight. The flaw is that nothing happens to her in New York (and what's she doing there? For a living, I mean) that could not equally well have happened in London, while the homesickness theme is underplayed, so the local colour comes to seem meretricious. It is well sprayed on, though, in the blow-up decor. John Caird's production fills the stage with three mobile platforms: one for Miss Webb, one for the conductor, and one for the band. It all looks very perilous. Speaking of which, the Paganini theme is also the theme of 'The South Bank Show'; next time I switch on I hope to see Melvyn Bragg turning cartwheels.

* * *

The Mayfair programme says Boogie! on the cover, 'Boogie Woogie Bubble 'n' Squeak' on the title-page; and I share its confusion. Three energetic South Africans (Leonie Hofmeyer, Sarah McNair, Michele Maxwell) take us through the pop decades as the Andrews Sisters, the McGuire Sisters (the McGuire Sisters?), the Beverley Sisters; and then, deserting showbiz sisterhood for committed Sisterhood, as the Shangri-Las (all leathery as in Leader of the Pack), the Supremes (three to a dress) and the Pointer siblings. (They satirise the 1940s from a pacifist standpoint; Pistol Packin' Mama is backed by a picture of a mushroom-cloud ('Lay that pistol down, babe') and 'Rum and Coca-Cola' by starving Caribbeans. Well, fair enough. They satirise the 1950s from a feminist viewpoint; but masochistic ballads like 'Little Things Mean a Lot' and 'Mister Wonderful' were always laughable anyway. They then run out of things to say; they just want to get into that gear and sing those songs.

One of them sang consistently flat, and their impersonations are approximate. Their Beverleys sound as the Andrewses might have done if each of them had been christened Julie; and I never knew that Diana Ross had a Fenella Fielding lisp.

* * *

Four days earlier I had encountered another lampoon of the Supremes, having gained entrance to the one new Broadway hit, Dream Girls, boosted by raves in both the New York and London Times. Friends, more temperately, had told me I would find it entertaining but empty. They were wrong. Au fond 'Dream Girls' is serious. Its subject is the commercialisation of black music from Motown to disco; and it attempts an unusual density of treatment by reflecting this through half a dozen leading characters. These include a successful female trio called the Dreams, one of whom is cast out and spends a disproportionate amount of the action thereafter bewailing her disappointment in an extremely powerful blues voice.

Meanwhile the Dreams themselves remain shadowy, as do the other principals. The show roars sharply along for half an hour, establishing a milieu; then it loses its nerve and settles for the characters' love-lives. The score, supposedly parodic, offers several varieties of nothing; and the show, despite Michael Bennett's staging, sinks into boredom.

Not that Mr Bennett is short on coups; he can do much with the mere whisk of a curtain, and his basic set--an arrangement of lighting-towers and gantries, ceaselessly mobile--is a technical wonder. There are also two or three exciting dances; but on the whole Mr Bennett choreographs sets rather than people. I owe this phrase to John Heilpern's Times article; he and I differ radically as to whether it is a good thing.
