Restoration house party

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a Congreve revival and a new play by David Mercer.

CONGREVE'S The Double Dealer (Olivier) has the reputation of a neglected classic. Actually, compared to some I could mention, it's a repertory standard. It touches down about once every 10 years and I, who have measured out my life in revivals of minor classics, am usually in its path. Since nobody should be required to summarise the plot of this play more than once in his lifetime I may draw, in what follows, on my own previous writings on the subject.

Right then. The Double Dealer, in the words of Ray Charles, ain't nothin' but a house-party. Lord Touchwood has invited some friends down to celebrate the betrothal of his nephew Mellefont to Cynthia, an arrangement which pleases hardly anyone.

Lady Touchwood, Lord T's wife, fancies Mellefont, as does Lady Plyant who is not Cynthia's mother, merely the second wife of her father, who by the way is Lady Touchwood's brother. Cynthia meanwhile is desired by Maskwell, the kind of friend with whom Mellefont doesn't need enemies. There are also Lord and Lady Froth, but they keep themselves very much to themselves.

I have known the Froths, a pair of litterateurs who have met their perfect match in each other, to dominate the play: a perfect image of the Hampstead or Blackheath couple who meet at a party, find themselves in complete intellectual sympathy, and go to bed on the discovery. Here they are subdued, though Nicholas Selby, at least, captures the lord's complacent solemnity (reining back all intrusive smiles) and Brenda Blethyn's flirtatious lady fits neatly into the evening's principal pattern.

There are four women in the play, and three of them are nymphomaniacs (or, to put it in Restoration terms, married). Cynthia, a coolly intelligent little number in Judi Bowker's performance, looks at them and decides that her match at least will turn out different. Of her three errant sisters, if Lady Froth is a creature of satiric farce, Lady Plyant ascends to high lubricious comedy: her lines, as cooed and purred by Dorothy Tutin, are the poetry of double entendre. Finally, there is Lady Touchwood, in whom passion and jealousy come close to madness, and whom Sara Kestelman in a grandly unabashed reading (her best yet, I think) turns into a pale-faced compound of Phedre and Lady Macbeth.

To each lady at least one gentleman. (There are also a couple of spare gallants to carry messages and make up the occasional threesome.) The most complicated is Maskwell, whose quirk is to tell his dupes what he is going to do to them before he does it. Robert Stephens adds a further twist; his professions of conscience are not all bluff. Glassy-faced, glassy-voiced, he blenches momentarily at the thought of betraying his friend, bedding his protector's wife, yet again. As a tragi-comic creation, he almost unifies the play. (A double dealer works wonders.)

Ironically, I suppose, he is dressed in white (cream, anyway), while Mellefont, the goody, goes about in black, thereby pre-empting his own final disguise as a chaplain (a necessary prelude to marriage, in this kind of play). Lord Touchwood is central to the plot, but peripheral to the play; Ralph Richardson cannot bring him to life until the end, when he, too, has a triumphant stab at intrigue.

I have now cleared the decks for Michael Bryant, who plays Miss Tutin's spouse, an ill-used knight kept in sexual and social subjection, and convinced that it is for his own good. Occasionally he stirs into revolt, but gently rebukes himself. There is a quiet war within him; no word can escape him without being gravely weighed. He has one longish speech describing his state of life (he lies trussed up at night, so that his wife will not be disturbed by his advances), which Mr Bryant breaks up into sections of two words each. Each phrase gets a laugh. His is the most huggable performance I have seen for ages.

Tanya Moiseiwitsch has set the play on a timbered baronial staircase, a solid comfort to behold. It comes equipped with carved heads for decoration, secret passages and exit-windows for action, and a revolve to transport us to the bedroom (and the motivation) behind the display. It leaves the acting-area a bit cramped; a great deal seems shoved into a corner.

Peter Wood's direction suffers from a giggly beginning and a huddled, hurried end, but its middle is stuffed with accomplishment. The variations in style hardly matter (the text invites them - some critics seem to want everyone to give the same performance); the variations in pressure do. There is not the solid mellow enchantment of Mr Wood's Love for Love, but if you relax with it the good things will come to you.

* * *

"We are not," hollers an English upper-middle-class drunk in the wake of a Russian defector who is defecting back again, "a valid statistical sample." There is no reason for Cousin Vladimir (Aldwych) to leave England on account of the collection of soaks to whom he has been exposed on his brief visit - especially since he himself is no heroic dissident, but an industrial swindler a step or so ahead of the police.

The common complaint against David Mercer's play has been phrased in much his own words; if these people are not typical, what is the point of arranging the encounter? It begins to fade when you try to imagine a social group who might give Vladimir a better run for his roubles; disenchantment would surely set in somewhere. The flaw is rather that Mr Mercer's bleak honesty about both sides amounts to a meeting of negatives: a subject, possibly, but for a shorter play than this. Once you have grasped the point that life in Britain may be preferable to life in the Soviet Union but we shouldn't get smug about it, there is not much to take in, apart from exceptional articulateness. But the drinking-gang are not fascinating (not even their leader, an ex-physicist who gets Vladimir asylum by marrying his cousin) and the evening becomes a replay of The Iceman Cometh? (a houseful of lushes embracing and then rejecting a false saviour) without O'Neill's widening single-mindedness to hold it together. The acting is all right, which means not right enough.

The second agoraphobic mum in two weeks turns up in Anne Commire's Shay (King's Head). As a case-study, this American lady, afraid to meet people because she knows that she can talk to them only too readily, is far more believable at first sight than Stephen Poliakoff's Croydon recluse in Shout Across the River, but has nothing like the same imagination or intensity to guide her thereafter.

Shay herself may be disturbing, but the play is cosy. Libby Morris gives her plenty of guts, plenty of despair, but there is no jelly in her; the lady's tic is a showbiz compulsion, but when Miss Morris sings along with a Lena Horne record she does it so well that you wonder what she can be worried about.
