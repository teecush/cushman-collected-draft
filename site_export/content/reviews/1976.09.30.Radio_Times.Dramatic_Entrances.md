Dramatic entrances

30th anniversary of the Third Programme

It was the Third rather than theatres proper that, until the rep boom of the 60s, brought British audiences the drama classics -- from Aristophanes to Shaw. It was the Third, too, that introduced to them the work of newer playwrights -- like Dylan Thomas, Samuel Beckett and Jean Anouilh; Harold Pinter, Robert Bolt and Tom Stoppard. Here Robert Cushman assesses 30 years of broadcasting drama

YEARS AGO there was a radio comedy series called In All Directions, written and performed by Peter Ustinov and Peter Jones. One edition found them wandering around the BBC in search of the Third Programme. They knew when they had found the right entrance: it was guarded by a commissionaire who seemed to be reading Proust. I say seemed to be, it turned out that actually he was re-reading him.

The temptation to rib the Third had good historical precedent. The very first programme it ever presented (at 6.0 on Sunday 29 September 1946) was a spoof on itself, prepared by Stephen Potter and Joyce Grenfell, under the title How to Listen. The Third parodied its own identity even before it achieved it. This suggests an odd blend of nervousness and confidence, with the confidence predominating. In those days, you knew what Culture was. If you didn't, the Third would quickly tell you. Having, on that first evening, been told How to Listen you set about doing it. First there was a performance of Bach's Goldberg Variations; then Reflections on World Affairs by Field-Marshal Smuts; then a good, solid two-hour concert, interrupted by an introductory talk about the Third by the Director-General: presumably How to Listen for real.

TAKING PRECEDENCE OVER TELEVISION

What is amazing about the Third is its monstrous size and appetite at birth. No nonsense about introducing it by degrees: it exercised its principles from the first, seven evenings a week. In 1946 it must have seemed foolhardy, but it may be that only in a period of austerity could it have happened. There may have been a feeling abroad that the war had been fought so that we could have a Third Programme. It certainly took precedence over the re-establishment of television; if it had not, it would probably never have existed at all.

Drama was slow, by the standards set by music and talks, to get into the act; the Third was all of a day old before the first thing you could possibly call a play went out. And that was a doubtful case: Milton's masque of Comus, the author sharing equal billing with his composer, Henry Lawes, and the whole issuing from the nest of poets oddly known as the Features Department. Dylan Thomas was in the cast. Drama Department did not make its bow until the Tuesday when it offered a full-length (i.e. four-and-a-quarter-hour) version of Shaw's Man and Superman. And here was born Third Programme Thrift; over the succeeding weeks there were repeats of the play in its entirety, repeats of the scenes usually played, and, for connoisseurs, repeats of the scenes usually left out.

This was only a foretaste of the role which the Third was to play in airing the classics. Shaw, after all, was repertory theatre stuff; but until the era of permanent companies began in the English theatre in the 60s there were few other dead playwrights you could hope to see performed. But there were many you could hear, and the 50s were in this respect something of a Golden Age. One producer, Raymond Raikes, worked steadily through medieval, Elizabethan and Restoration drama. He was indeed responsible for fixing in many minds the canon of what constituted the English classics. It looks now like a one-sided selection, probably influenced by 19th-century criticism; over-hospitable to lachrymose tragedies with noble sentiments and neglectful of the warped, cynical tragi-comedies that now seem so modern.

But Raikes had a boisterous way with comedy, the kind that the theatre still neglects, perhaps because it has forgotten how to be funny. He could even make Ralph Roister Doister (which the textbooks call the first English comedy) hilarious, largely by cramming it with music. And his version of The Shoemaker's Holiday, with Donald Wolfit, is a classic on several counts; it still turns up. Raikes's versions of Greek comedy, in which he redeemed jokes theoretically dead beyond recovery, outclassed the theatre even more decisively; in my teens I was quite convinced that Aristophanes was the world's greatest radio dramatist. (At least, I ranked him co-equal with Galton and Simpson, with whom he seemed to have certain affinities.)

INTRODUCING ANOUILH TO THE BRITISH

I must admit that as years went on, I grew tired of certain mannerisms in radio's reclamations. There was a mania for having the characters identify themselves long after we were able to recognise them by their voices. When their friends could not mention their names in dialogue, they did it themselves in soliloquy. I always expected to hear a Shakespearean tragic hero announce 'O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I, Hamlet.'

Shakespeare, since we have got on to him, was not the exclusive preserve of the Third; the Home might knock off an Othello or a Romeo and Juliet. But it kept clear of the less familiar plays, and it had no time (literally) for uncut texts. You tuned into the Third for Pericles or for a complete Hamlet, perhaps with John Gielgud in the title role, giving you the soul of a great performance long after the limbs and outward flourishes peculiar to the theatre had been laid to rest.

There was once a series of English classics from all periods billed as The National Theatre of the Air, making official use of a phrase which had long, and quite deservedly, been current. Even the advent of a physical National Theatre, combined with a growing adventurousness on the part of repertory companies, has failed to provide playgoers with the range from which listeners have benefited over the years. Television, picking up plays that the theatre has turned into standards, is still way behind (actually further behind now than it was in the 50s). There might well have been a similar series called International Theatre of the Air; here radio drama has been a no-contest winner almost since the Third was born.

The insularity of the English theatre is proverbial, as regards both classic and modern plays; and even when a European writer has proved popular on the stage, he usually turns out to have been introduced on radio. Jean Anouilh was the darling of the London as well as the Paris boulevards in the 50s, but it was the Third who had introduced him in 1948, with Medea, unseen in London to this day.

SALVING PINTER'S CRITICAL WOUNDS

With new English dramatists the position has changed; radio, with its vast appetite for material and its relatively low costs, still puts bread in their mouths but its position as patron has been usurped -- well, supplemented -- by subsidised playhouses and, more particularly, by the theatrical fringe. Some of the latest generation of playwrights have worked for the BBC (in two senses since a few, like Howard Barker, Mike Stott and Trevor Griffiths, have held BBC jobs of various kinds) but none of them can truthfully be said to have made his name in radio.

For the generation before it could be crucial: Giles Cooper made himself into the greatest of radio playwrights; Harold Pinter recuperated in radio after the critical wounds inflicted on his theatrical debut; Robert Bolt wrote radio plays for years before his West End breakthrough; Tom Stoppard gained most of his early experience in the medium. It is very rare, though, for a highly successful radio playwright not to break out; even those who stay out of the theatre go into television -- like Rhys Adrian, who began writing for radio in the early 60s (winning a major award for his Evelyn), moved into television towards the end of the decade and would certainly be accounted one of its major writers, but is still principally a radio man; a new Adrian play, a hospital piece called The Night Nurse Slept in the Day Room (Tuesday 9.20), is to be part of the Third Programme anniversary week.

LOUIS MACNEICE WORKED HERE

Maybe, if you are looking for pure radio playwrights, you have to go back to another defunct and/or assimilated piece of the BBC: the Features Department which was amalgamated with Drama after the death of its guiding spirit Laurence Gilliam. It was here that Louis MacNeice worked; his most celebrated piece The Dark Tower came a fraction too early to be premiered on the Third, which however lost no time in repeating it. (MacNeice's adaptation of Virginia Woolf's The Waves will be broadcast on Thursday at 9.25 pm.) Henry Reed's famous satires on the London art scene, the well-aired series about the critic Herbert Reeve and the lady composer Hilda Tablet, were quintessential Third/Feature jokes -- and some of them will be heard next month on Radio 4.

It was for Features that Dylan Thomas, almost literally spurred to it by Douglas Cleverdon, wrote the classic of all radio classics, Under Milk Wood. Only Samuel Beckett's All That Fall (from Drama Department, to even the score) can rival it; and Beckett is among the Third's great captures. He was hardly a struggling writer in need of encouragement (Waiting for Godot was already behind him); he worked in the medium because he wanted to. And, with his output getting ever sparer, he still does; his latest work is unveiled on radio this week (For to End Yet Again, Monday 10.10 pm).

Robert Cushman is theatre critic of the Observer.
