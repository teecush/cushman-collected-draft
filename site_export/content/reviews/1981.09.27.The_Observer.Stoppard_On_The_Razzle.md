THEATRE

Stoppard on the Razzle

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Manchester.

PERHAPS the best place to start with On the Razzle (Lyttelton), Tom Stoppard's Viennese whirl, is towards the end, where somebody says indignantly of somebody else, "he's the real impostor." Or towards the beginning, where Dinsdale Landen, contemplating the immediate future, says, "one false move and we could have a farce on our hands." Or the middle, where we do.

But I think I'll begin by telling you a story. A year or so ago I was in Vienna and, conscientious creature that I am, asked to be taken to the Burgtheater. My host shook his head. "They're doing a Nestroy play tonight," he said. "You wouldn't understand it. It isn't in real German; it's in a strange Viennese dialect. Even we don't understand it. Have dinner instead." I decided to let the implied, and quite unmerited, tribute to my command of real German pass unchallenged, and took the dinner: which was excellent.

But in fact I probably could have made head, if not tail, of the play, since it was Einem Jux will er sich machen, on which Thornton Wilder based The Merchant of Yonkers, subsequently re-adapted as The Matchmaker, which I've seen, and musicalised as Hello Dolly, ditto. It would have been good to see the original, of which Mr Stoppard's piece is another free adaptation. Maybe the National should dig up the English farce A Day Well Spent by John Oxenford, on which Nestroy, some 140 years ago, did his own basing. Let's nail this thing, once and for all.

I am not suggesting that Oxenford's script would be as funny as Mr Stoppard's. Apart from Jumpers and The Importance of Being Earnest there may be no script in English funnier than On the Razzle. From political farce and philosophical farce Mr Stoppard has turned to writing farcical farce: both a raging example of the genre and a tribute to it. (The only comparable work I know is A Funny Thing Happened On the Way to the Forum.)

Once again two shop assistants abandon their groceries for a first and last night on the town. Once again they get involved with two ladies, one of whom is in hats, professionally speaking, and is also engaged to their employer. He is also engaged in trying to prevent his niece from marrying the man of her choice. Take the above, mix in several locations, bring to the boil in a restaurant scene and add, most vitally, Mr Stoppard's gift of language.

What makes On the Razzle unique is that every line in it is either a joke or part of one. There are half a dozen puns per page, and it is very rare indeed for anyone to say anything that cannot be misinterpreted, or that isn't. Mr Stoppard does not write wisecracks. His jokes, unlike, say, Neil Simon's, are not there to lighten or to accentuate the situation; they are the situation.

This is not as a rule how plays get written, though we might all be happier if it were. You will notice that I have referred to On the Razzle as a script; it recalls, alludes to, and cries out for the Marx Brothers, Morecambe and Wise, John Cleese and at one point (the "get it -- got it -- good" routine from The Court Jester) Danny Kaye. This obviously makes it rather hard on mere actors.

Mr Landen as the master-grocer and Michael Kitchen as his servant (whose catch-phrase, applied quite correctly in the circumstances to absolutely everything, is "classic") have the beginnings of a fine routine going, and in 30 years' time they will probably be wonderful. That's over-pessimistic; it took Mr Landen a few months to become his own man in Plunder, which presented similar problems. At the moment, though, his eyebrows work more expressively than his voice, which is too anxiously squeaky. Better and more relaxed are Ray Brooks and Felicity Kendal (en travesti) as the hellraisers for a night; Miss Kendal's mounting delight leaves a lovely taste.

Peter Wood's production struck me at first as too slow; that was simplistic of me, since some of the best comedy acts are deliberate. But the right pacing, whatever it is, has yet to arrive; some scenes are actually over-inventive. Carl Toms's sets, especially for the grocery emporium, are delectable; he fills scene-changes with projections of sheet-music, in witty tribute to Nestroy, who used actual songs.

Finally I have a confession to make; apart from a fine fantasia on the glories and uncertainties of the provisioner's trade ("we sit here flicking a duster over pyramids of uncertain dates"), Mr Stoppard has cut the play loose from the moorings it once had in Austrian social satire; and I doubt his wisdom. I found myself wondering what it was all for. This is an unaccustomed mood for me; and I would be very tart with anyone else who raised such puritanical objections. "It's hilarious," I would say. "Think yourself lucky."

* * *

Adrian Noble's production of Doctor Faustus (Royal Exchange, Manchester) is visually superb, both in set-pieces like the Pope's banquet and the show of the Seven Deadly Sins, and in such single moments as the first appearance of Mephostophilis, who shuffles on as an imp with a limp. Later he is metamorphosed into James Maxwell, saturnine in a cassock. The comedy scenes are kept nearly intact and, without raising any roofs, perform their literary function of showing up Faustus's own antics.

The point of the play has to be that any price paid for damnation is bound to look pretty silly; or, in secular terms, that power will bring out the schoolboy in any of us. This at least is how Ben Kingsley plays Faustus, getting a great bang out of disrupting the other kids' parties and developing a very persuasive self-intoxication. He only fails in the last speech, whose myriad gradations are ignored in favour of monotonous hysteria. Even a final coup-de-theatre, with the protagonist blown to Hell among withered leaves, cannot quite atone.

* * *

At the New Half Moon Instant Enlightenment Including VAT (sloppy title) examines the Est-like movements that bully their customers into what they take to be spiritual transformation. It is also rather a good advertisement for them; in Andrew Carr's production of his own script we are present at a series of seminars whose thrill and entertainment value seems extremely high; if one could attend without getting involved, it might almost be worth the money.

But would it be possible? You can't be sure; Mr Carr's resident sceptic ends up as cowed and accepting as any, but his turning-point is not actually shown. That's the trouble; the play has too many case-histories to get through. But it disturbs, not least because the unflaggingly abrasive guru (played with appropriate barking charisma by Robin Nedwell, whose throat will be a wreck by the end of the run) has some sense on his side. The charge that we are all ruled by neurotic patterns in our own minds sticks easily. What remains mercifully unproven is that he has anything better to offer.

* * *

The Orchestra (King's Head) of Jean Anouilh's play is an all-girl Palm Court aggregation, plus male pianist. Between and even during numbers they treat us to their complaints and fantasies, which would be much the same if they couldn't play a note. There is some bilious wit in both the substance and the superstructure, but they don't need each other. Nor does the author's harking back to the squabbles of the Occupation seem of much moment now. The play was written in 1957.
