Thrones, coffins and roses

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the 'Henry' cycle at Stratford

THE three parts of Henry VI (Stratford upon Avon) might be subtitled 'War and Peace and War Again.' They take up the history of England where Henry V leaves off (the RSC has in fact remounted its successful production of that play).

Part one wraps up the Hundred Years War; the French conquests amassed by King Hal slip successively through the fingers of those of his relatives assigned to guard them. They blame their defeats on Joan of Arc and witchcraft, but occasional flashbacks to the home front clue us in to the real reason; the English nobles are becoming factious, i.e. they are hanging around the Temple Garden plucking white and red roses and picking up sides according to horticultural preference.

The occasional throne, coffin or, of course, rose bush is carried on or off Farrah's set as required, but for most of Terry Hands's production the stage is deep, bare and threatening.

Part Two is largely devoted to the gentler pursuits of court intrigue; this is the only section of the trilogy in which the actors get to stand still. Men still die, but in their beds: stabbed or smothered, usually. Mr Hands has staged this one on an ironically bright expanse of greensward, divided by a rope barrier. On one side King, Queen and courtiers perambulate; on the other the lower classes gawp.

As the evening proceeds they make their presence felt first as comic relief, then as the threatening embodiment of riot. After half time they swarm on to the field, uniting both roles in a fierce and bloody enactment of Jack Cade's rebellion. (Object: the abolition of privilege and literacy, not necessarily in that order.) But Cade is merely a front; behind him stands the Duke of York, equipped with a watertight if complicated claim to the Crown, and determined to enforce it. In the last scene of the play, the Wars of the Roses break out.

In Part Three we fade to black again. The action is almost continuous butchery, the stage barer than ever. The mortality rate is such that one actor dies twice before the cycle has reached its first interval, and only four characters go through all three plays: Henry VI himself, best described by Sellar and Yeatman as 'such a Good Man that he was thought to be a saint or alternatively -- especially by the barons -- an imbecile,' his termagant Queen Margaret, and their opponents York and Warwick.

The plays are the earliest Shakespeare and Part One the most primitive of all; no play in the English repertoire is as dependent on pure narrative drive, and the company swing it enthusiastically and infectiously. Many of them, however, seem too lightweight for Part Two, a more rewarding play but dependent on the clash of personality (as opposed to that more complex thing, character). In Part Three they begin, understandably, to get tired.

The last instalment, chiefly concerned with hacking its way through history, lacks definition. Nevertheless, as written, it contains three great scenes. First is the capture, humiliation and death of York at the hands of Margaret. This finds Helen Mirren at her best. She lacks the reserves for her pounding attacks on Henry (the most spectacularly henpecked husband in English drama), but her treatment of York is subtler, crueller, and Miss Mirren paces it with sexual relish. Emrys James, though luxuriantly sniffing at his white rose, presents York as a twisted dandy, a believable conspirator but no soldier, and unable to pour out his heart since he plainly has none.

Secondly, there is the battle of Towton, where Henry witnesses the reality of civil war: two soldiers of whom one has unwittingly killed his own father and the other his son. The scene is badly staged, but the trouble lies deeper; Mr Hands has given us an emblematic treatment of the surrounding action: if, say, the text reads 'Enter Northumberland,' then Northumberland enters, without military support. The commons, so active in Part Two, have vanished, leaving us no sense of the context from which the unfortunate homicides have sprung.

Finally comes Henry's confrontation with his spiritual opposite, the future Richard III. Their duet is classically influenced: Alan Howard's Scofield-tremolo versus the Olivier-yelp of Anton Lesser, a tense and springy newcomer.

Mr Howard's Henry is actually neither saint nor imbecile; he is not even a particularly Good Man, though he is certainly a very nice one. He is an innocent (Mr Howard has discovered a staggeringly virginal walk) who loses his illusions very quickly but gains all the time in intelligence. He can out-observe any man in the realm but he cannot act on his insights. He is recognisably his father's son (Mr Howard having now tilted his Agincourt routine as far towards introspection as is humanly possible, and maybe farther); but with a paralysing extra layer of sensitivity.

This is Mr Howard's best performance, and its final intensity is a perfectly believable trigger for Richard's rage. The murder done, we are plunged back to the newly established court of Richard's brother, Edward IV, who prophesies peace and orders that the royal band stop playing dreadful marches and switch to delightful measures. Unfortunately nobody in the palace knows the steps, and we end on a charmingly sardonic note -- or will, when Mr Hands has got the staging sorted out. For most of the three nights his invention hardly flags.

He has still, though, to get his company together. It has three elements: (a) such RSC trusties as the unshakably stalwart Jeffery Dench and such more recent recruits as Oliver Ford-Davies and Stephen Jenn; both developing finely; (b) experienced classicists from other stables, notably Julian Glover, a commonsense Warwick with moments of grandeur, and Graham Crowden, though his eccentric authority sits oddly on the sanest man at court; (c) actors from all over, and all ages, having a bash.

These last vary greatly, but the verse -- which is confoundedly regular -- often defeats them. I liked Jack Klaff's vacillating Clarence, Charlotte Cornwell's Joan, and as the fighting Talbots, fils et pere, Ian Gelder and David Swift. Alfred Lynch has a cheerful go at Edward, but is much better as a gentle chorus in Henry V; James Laurenson, looking like Geronimo, is a bloodcurdling Cade, but not very funny; the horror of his scenes is there, but not the music hall.

Interest in these plays is very recent; I, stripling as I am, have witnessed most of its twentieth-century stage history with my own eyes. As we entered the lists for Part One, the critic of the Sunday Times, after we had duly struck each other across the cheek with our red and white blooms, inquired if I had seen The Wars of the Roses, the Peter Hall-John Barton adaptation that made history in 1963. I had; and, magnificent though it was and fondly as I recall it, I now recognise that it planted RSC mannerisms that are only just disappearing. It did not just edit, it editorialised, flaunting ironies that are merely implicit in the original and encouraging a flamboyantly acting style still apparent in Mr James and occasionally in Mr Howard.

But before that came a Birmingham Rep-Old Vic production, played with a down-centre vigour now hard to find. Too many of this cast cannot raise their voices without having their words disappear through the roof; it can't all be the fault of the Stratford acoustics. There was besides much nervousness evident last week; much fluffing, leaping of cues, and upsetting of furniture. That will obviously mend; meanwhile, Mr Hands has displayed great courage, great faith and great imagination. A rave review is struggling within me and, though I cannot conscientiously let it out just yet, I shall keep it on ice.
