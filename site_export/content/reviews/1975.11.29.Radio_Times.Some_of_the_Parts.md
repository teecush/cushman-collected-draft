Play for Today - Through the Night, Tuesday 9.25 pm BBC1

When she discovers a lump in her breast, the heroine of Trevor Griffiths's new play does exactly what she should: she goes to her doctor then is taken into hospital for observation. From that moment her bewilderment and fear grow, not because she is ill but because she has become an object, a 'patient' instead of a person. The topic is a new departure for Griffiths, who talks here to Robert Cushman, theatre critic of The Observer

Some of the parts

WHEN I MET Trevor Griffiths he was resilient, talkative but tired. His home is in Leeds but he was down in London for a screening of his new BBC television play, Through the Night. The title must have been beginning to seem very apt; by his own account he hardly ever sleeps, and works an incredible 20-hour day, going to bed only between four and eight in the morning.

'I was one of those babies who didn't sleep so you could say I was learning from an early age. My daughter's the same.' Since she is an adopted daughter it must be an environmental trait, not a hereditary one. As far as Griffiths is concerned, early conditioning or not, the present routine is 'murder. I'm drawing on my equipment colossally. But it seems to be holding up -- I haven't had a heart attack yet.'

Through the Night is a new departure for him. His recent plays -- All Good Men on television, The Party on stage -- have had explicitly political subjects or, like his stage success this year, Comedians, have dealt in metaphors (does a comedian try to soothe his audience or unsettle it) which have been readily transferable into political terms. Through the Night is set entirely in a hospital and though Griffiths sees its terms of reference as wide ('the play will invite people to question what happens to them in surgeries, doctors' waiting-rooms, factories') the impact of the immediate subject could be so great that viewers will not look beyond it. The play touches on a very raw nerve indeed.

It concerns a woman who discovers a lump on her breast and is admitted into hospital for what she believes to be a routine check-up. She goes through much of the play in a state of terrified bewilderment; nobody will tell her anything. Griffiths saw his own father die of cancer without knowing what had hit him, and he has always, in his own words 'had a running battle with medicine.' What angers him is the profession's 'extraordinary insensitivity to the wholeness of human beings. Medicine treats people as just the sum of their parts. If you've ever been in hospital you'll know how you immediately regress to infantilism; because you're being treated as an infant, it's your only way of coping. And that's for you and me -- middle-class, professional, highly articulate, capable of aggression. What is it like for a woman with a potentially terminal condition who is used in precisely that way and without the resources, without the tradition of getting what you want that we have even if we don't exercise it?'

This is the first Griffiths play to have a woman at its centre. Comedians is an all-male play and the position of women in his other plays -- notably The Party -- can be marginal and a bit uneasy. He's aware of this; even a little sheepish about it. 'I've been trying to raise my own feminist consciousness -- certainly for the last seven years. It's very difficult if you've been brought up in Manchester with that heavy masculine conditioning, trained to assert the supremacy of the male. You can get it in your head, fine, but you can't pull it round to actually doing things. When I'm eating and finish a dish I still push the plate to one side -- that kind of thing. That's a terrible limitation on growth, on realising the things that collectively make you respectful of other persons who happen to be women.' He shudders. 'But I'm getting better.'

On a wider scale, this impulse towards 'personal aggressiveness' continued through his years as a student, still in Manchester. He describes himself at that stage as 'a social fascist. Everything in my life led that way. Total incapacity to handle my own emotions, overspill into violence, total unconcern about other people's feelings -- everything that made a man a man in Manchester 1951 -- that was me. The great symbol for that would be early Brando; I'm sure that thousands of young guys like me felt the same. It was years after that I discovered that inarticulateness is not necessarily a virtue.' Inarticulateness is about the last thing that anybody would accuse him of now.

Though he acknowledges some debts to his university training (particularly, and rather surprisingly, to having had to learn Anglo-Saxon) he obviously feels that the most important part of his education only came later -- when he discovered the Left, taking writers like Raymond Williams, Richard Hoggart and Dennis Potter as his mentors. He is now probably the playwright most aware of politicians, of political activists and political theorists. He has however broken away from the traditional method of dramatising politics; his plays are not struggles between Left and Right. They take that much for granted and focus instead on struggles between Left and Left; and this sometimes makes him unpopular.

But Griffiths's great strength (though critics, including myself, have sometimes been slow to realise it and have categorised him as a doctrinaire propagandist) is his ability to dramatise his own conflicts: 'I don't think I know where I'm going when I write a play. I do a tremendous amount of groundwork and I know that I'm scoring over my own fears, anxieties and beliefs -- and counter-beliefs. But the material itself impacts upon all that.' So when researching a play about Lenin in 1903 for the Fall of Eagles series he had no wish to jettison material merely because it was critical of Lenin and Leninism. 'Far from it; I wanted to incorporate it into the play to make it richer and darker and more difficult. I'm not for easy options anywhere.'

So that the Lenin piece is almost a case of distanced autobiography: 'I was getting at something very important in me, but also very important in terms of social and political process, to do with total commitment to a revolutionary perspective which will inevitably eliminate humane considerations -- of relationship, sympathy, care -- and that seems to me a problem that we face all the time.'

It is a problem that recurs in most of his plays. In Comedians (which for me was the stage play of the year) it was there in the conflict between the old comic who hated the cruelty of laughter and the brilliant young apprentice for whom laughter was an instrument of destruction; both represent parts of Griffiths himself. You can find it too in Through the Night with its attack on the impersonality of the medical profession. 'What they lose touch with is the freshness of suffering.' Isn't that inevitable? 'Well, that's arguable. They say it's necessary.' And it is this necessity that the play fiercely questions.
