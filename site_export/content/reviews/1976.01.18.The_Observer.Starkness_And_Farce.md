Starkness--and farce

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'The Ik' and Ben Travers's 'Plunder'.

THERE is no way in which the theatre can escape from its allegiance to effect; to inducing an emotion or enforcing a moral. Everything placed before an audience is a show, Peter Brook's production of The Ik (Roundhouse) as much as the National Theatre revival of Ben Travers's Plunder (Old Vic). Indeed, in terms of the unfamiliar demands it makes on its actors, Plunder may well be the more adventurous production of the two.

Many of us clapped at the end of The Ik; those who did not looked at us reproachfully. They expected us, I think, to be too numbed for so frivolous a response; and they could point to the integrity of a production which never raises its voice, literally or metaphorically, to back them up. But starkness is an effect, like any other; the refusal blatantly to shock or move an audience is the latest way of trying to do both.

In The Ik six actors offer their own image, born of travel in Africa and experiment in Paris, of the lives of a Ugandan tribe who have been denied their traditional existence as hunters and left to starve. They are old at 20; only the cleverest predators survive. They are dying out, since procreation is too much effort for them; their few remaining pleasures include cheating and teasing the anthropologist Colin Turnbull (on whose book The Mountain People the play is based), whose efforts to record them with notebook, tape and whirring cine-camera lend the production a solemn comic ground-base.

The play is given in English, but since the cast are international their speeches have a peculiar tone; they seem quite effortlessly alien. They really do appear indifferent to the public gaze; hence their fascination for Turnbull and for us. They are people who expect nothing and will take anything; they and the production have, in consequence, a unique rhythm. Everything is arbitrary, everything is inevitable.

Seeing them in Paris last year I described them as representing "irreducible, shuffling, nerveless humanity." I will now stand by the adjectives, but not the noun; I no longer feel inclined to generalise or to identify. The reaction that that is how we will all behave is obvious and true, but facile; a case, like that of Barry Collins's Judgment, which nobody would want to question. I doubt if that was one of Mr Brook's major preoccupations anyway; all he is really asking us to do is to look at, and be entertained by, the Ik, or by an ingenious, original, and consistent method of presenting them. (One of them, played by a constantly grinning Japanese actor, has the wiliness of a traditional clown; we loved him.) In other words, he makes voyeurs of us; as theatre always does.

I am not belittling him. What he gives us to gaze at is often stunning. A typical scene shows a woman luring a child with the promise of food held over a fire, withdrawing it and letting him burn his fingers. The production is if anything more sombre than it was in Paris, where the galleries, crevices and mountainous back wall of the Bouffes-du-Nord (interesting Mr Brook should finally have pitched his tent in a theatre, however dilapidated) offered a more exploitable, enjoyable space than the Roundhouse.

One formerly mesmerising sequence--the Ik roaring out "Rock of Ages" to earn food from visiting missionaries--now seems comparatively perfunctory. Mr Brook has a habit of dismissing his own work as soon as it is done, so perhaps the spectator is not to blame if he also finds the work less impressive on a second viewing. It may even be that Mr Brook, in masochistic mood, deliberately truncated the scene so that we would not be impressed. But we were: less than before, perhaps, but still incurably.

* * *

The most obvious thing to be said about the revival of Plunder is probably the most important. This is a play fashioned 50 years ago for an established firm of personality comedians; there is only a limited amount that mere acting can do for it now.

Many of the jokes depend on insult, the kind that is funniest when a family rapport connects the players and extends into the auditorium. Guesting on the Morecambe and Wise Show is about the nearest that National Theatre actors are likely to have come to the style required. One of the characters is a mountainous, fat and mountainously unpleasant lady. Everyone who comes into contact with her remarks on her girth ("You'll have to go a long way to get round me"--"I'd have to take a taxi"), but one-off casting is bound to make such sallies seem not so much rude as repetitive.

Actually at the National the situation is worse than it need be since Dandy Nichols, though she has the looks for the part, has nothing like the force. The play gets off to a good start with an abrupt proposal of marriage by an ungainly young man called Veal. But once we are past this nicely timed example of calf-love the character becomes the butt of all sorts of remarks about his resemblance to an egg. As played by Trevor Ray he is quite a good egg, but we have no cause to relish his humiliation as audiences in the twenties and long thereafter were accustomed to expect and enjoy the doing-down of Robertson Hare.

So it goes, right up the scale. Dinsdale Landen, playing a silly ass inveigled into becoming a cracksman, is technically superb, falling down on a bed (the victim of his own chloroform) or, always the mark of a fine athletic farceur, up a flight of stairs. But when he is questioned by the police, though an exquisite funniness seems to hang in the air, it never materialises. Mr Landen duly tangles up his sentences, his hearers and himself in slippery knots, tied by a blend of innocence, funk and bravado. But the lines seem to call for an unearthly abandon of silliness which could only be begotten by long practice upon matchless individuality.

Most of the other performers are allowed the fun of venality; they are all grabbing inheritances, marrying for money, robbing and blackmailing one another; and when the police are called in, their methods (particularly those of Derek Newark as an inspector of daunting solidity) are far from charming. For an English farce this is a pretty tough play and it ends with its protagonists apparently getting away with robbery and murder. Only apparently though; we gasp for a moment before realising that there are mitigating circumstances on both counts. If it is an amoral piece, it is so by cheery accident rather than (as in Joe Orton's similarly titled and much influenced Loot) insidious design.

Leading Mr Landen into temptation is Frank Finlay as a more experienced gentleman-thief. The outlines of this character are firmer than the others, and Mr Finlay, after some initial sloth, fits briskly inside them. His sang-froid when up before the law is irreproachable; this is a merrier version of his memorable Iago, an elegant shark. And the shark has pretty teeth, dear, and Mr Finlay is no kid when it comes to flashing the old molars. He dazzles.

There are two perfect scenes in Michael Blakemore's production--the burglary and Mr Finlay's interrogation--and much of the rest is adroitly handled. The first act is unduly slow, but much of the eccentric wit of Mr Travers's dialogue (which, of its period, I prefer to Coward's) has been preserved. Most of the disappointments are forgivable in the circumstances: Plunder may be the essence of a magnificent entertainment, but I doubt if today that essence can be bottled.
