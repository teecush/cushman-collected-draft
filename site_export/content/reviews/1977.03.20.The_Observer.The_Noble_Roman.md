The noble Roman

ROBERT CUSHMAN interviews John Gielgud, who plays Julius Caesar at the National Theatre this week.

"WHEN I was young," mused Sir John, recalling the Shakespeare plays that have recurred most frequently during his career, "Romeo and Juliet was my metronome. I was always playing Romeo or Paris or Mercutio or directing it. Then it was Richard II, then The Merchant of Venice; now it seems to be Julius Caesar. A most noble, noble play, so strict to its own shape."

John Gielgud plays Caesar in the production which opens at the Olivier on Thursday. It will be the second time he has played the role; the first was in the film of a few years back in which he co-starred with Charlton Heston. Not a great success, but then, as Gielgud says cheerfully enough, "hardly anyone saw it." Many people, however, saw and enthused over the previous film of the play, made in the fifties, in which he played Cassius, a role he had already done on stage at Stratford. And to complement these two Caesars and two Cassiuses his career also yields two Antonys: one at the Old Vic in the twenties, and the other at prep-school, where he played it, he says, with great elan.

There was also a plan once for him to play Brutus. This was in the fifties, under the aegis of Hugh Beaumont, of Tennent's, but West End economics proved hostile to a play with so many characters, each demanding his own toga. In those days Romans always wore togas. In contemporary productions they are less apt to, which is a pity, since Gielgud's acting seems permanently modern. His comic technique is always something to marvel at; and his austerity (he loves playing Prospero because he's "so remote") perfectly fits the time.

Gielgud speaks more freely than any of his critics of his own mannerisms. His gods (his own word) are the directors -- from Granville-Barker to Lindsay Anderson -- who are "not afraid to tell you when you're using old tricks." Of course one is fighting one's old admirers who don't want you to grow old and are scandalised if you say something indecent on the stage. Knowing, while not quite daring to believe, that he appeals both to the old and the young, Gielgud can contradict himself with quicksilver alacrity: at one moment musing "I've never quite achieved the hardness as an actor that I'd like," while at the next admitting a desire to stage The Merchant of Venice "as a charming fairy-tale" and lamenting the sentiment of the modern world that might torpedo the project. "It's awfully difficult now to know how to present the pretty parts of Shakespeare. Young actors aren't beautiful any more. Of course it's a great help for people who are wonderful actors if they haven't got to worry about their looks."

After Caesar he plays another medium-sized role in Volpone. Peter Hall assures me that it's awfully funny; a way of winding down after No Man's Land -- on stage for two hours, over two years, and on two continents. Caesar is a similar relaxation; it was his own suggestion that he should play it, lending the weight of his experience to a role that can use it. Caesar begins as a dictator and finishes a ghost; for an actor at once so commanding and so haunting (not to mention so aquiline) it seems ideal.
