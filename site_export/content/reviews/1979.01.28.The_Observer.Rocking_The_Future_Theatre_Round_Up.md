Rocking the future

IN A week of bits and pieces the most substantial fragment made its appearance at the Bush. This was The Cockroach that Ate Cincinnati, presented by Hull Truck but differing greatly from that company's familiar group improvisations. This show has a script, and only one performer.

The performer is Alan Aldred, who in A Bed of Roses gave a memorable impersonation of a man with a plate in his head. He was glowering, unpredictable and rather frightening. Psychopathic hints similarly abound in his current vehicle, but they are softened by humour. Mr Aldred has formidable comic timing, but of course he owes something to his writer, Alan Williams. In fact Messrs Williams and Aldred are the same man and freely admit the fact, but their subterfuge does allow them a double entry in what would otherwise be an undercrowded programme.

The title may remind you of a notably unfunny comedy song of the last decade, The Eggplant that Ate Chicago. Such references are constant; our monologuist is a pop-fancier of the fifties and sixties who has found his faith destroyed in the seventies. He has had a vision, or at least he has heard voices. On record, untraceable in any catalogue, he has made out, between the grooves, what he describes as 'a prophecy of the future of rock from 1968 to 1983.' It includes, you may note with whatever response you feel appropriate, the death of Elton John.

Disillusion becomes retrospective; the reputations of the past come up for consideration and are rapidly and ruthlessly dismissed. I must admit that I have never understood why a whole generation -- my own, to add to the confusion -- should have supposed for a moment that rock could provide them with a moral and political programme, and should even furnish leaders to put its tenets into effect. No betrayal can ever have been more clearly foreseeable.

Mr Williams does not do much to explain such a state of mind, but at least he evokes it powerfully and wittily. It is worth going to the Bush to hear, delivered with curling relish, the definitive put-down of Bob Dylan and his worshippers. A poet who didn't know what he was writing down from one stanza to the next -- we were lucky to find him. Evocative name-dropping ('there wasn't really that much to Wayne Fontana and the Mindbenders') duly sets the nostalgia-glands working, as Mr Aldred recalls a youth spent playing his Woolworth Dansette record-player, clearly a gesture of rebellion in a household where Classics Illustrated were the only permitted comics.

Messrs Aldred and Williams lost me in their second half when a misty pretentiousness set in ('son of man -- Manson,' it went) suggesting that the Dylan influence had gone ineradicably deep. But the fun and the anger, while they last, are good value.

They will not however be available at the Bush until 5 February, for one week only; I was admitted to a press preview. Holding the stage until then is something called The Paranormal Review, which I cannot recommend too highly. In fact I am not sure that I can recommend it at all. It purports to be a meeting of the Charles Flynn Society, who collect rationally inexplicable phenomena. They present items from their collection in the form of dramatised sketches, most of which go wrong. The entertainment lies in their confusion when this occurs.

We are presented in fact with a pretty traditional form of backstage humour, to which the occult trimmings are strictly irrelevant. Indeed, since the Charles Flynn societaires are repeatedly shown up as incompetent fools the veriest sceptic (me, for instance) is likely to find all his prejudices confirmed. He will also laugh some if (again like me) he is a sucker for offhandedly delivered theatrical gags. Whether the odd bits that are funny justify the bits that aren't must be a matter of personal taste. There is not much polish in the performances, though Paul McDowell (what, Whispering Paul of the Temperance Seven?) makes a pleasantly wooden compere.

My reaction to The Putney Debates (Cottesloe: one more performance, on Wednesday), was anticipated more than a century ago by Lord Melbourne, who said of an amateur performance of a Ben Jonson play got up by Dickens that, though he knew it was going to be dull, he did not expect it to be 'quite as damnably dull as it was.'

This enterprise, a pendant to The World Turned Upside Down, involves 11 National Theatre actors in reading extracts from the deliberations of Cromwell's New Model Army shortly before their final defeat of Charles I. They were engaged in discussing just how revolutionary they were prepared to be, and notions of the necessity or immorality of property were freely tossed around. So you can't say that the ideas have lost all interest, and there is a sporting attempt to make Cromwell (quite the least radical man on hand) sound like the Prime Minister.

But the readings are appallingly slovenly and quite unco-ordinated. No actor establishes a relationship with the previous speaker; each in turn plunges in, splashes about for a couple of sentences hoping his personality will buoy him up, and then sinks. It is impossible for the audience to know what they're talking about, since they seem ignorant of it themselves. They wear civvies; though period costumes might at least make the proceedings look more appetising and would not, in the circumstances of the current repertory, cost anything.

I don't know if the Roundhouse Downstairs is the most morgue-like theatre in town, or whether successive offerings merely make it seem so. Latest occupants are a callow troupe who seem, to put it charitably, out of their depth with Pantagleize, a satirical fantasy from between the wars by the Belgian playwright Michel de Ghelderode. Centring on a simpleton who blunders into and through a revolution, it now seems unbearably arch.

The translation doesn't help, though, while the performance merely confirms that plays which are at once old, substantial and unfashionable are likeliest to be tackled by companies unequal to the job. A rotten week. Like the man said, I'm waiting for the weather to break so's I can get out of London and see some theatre.

Theatre round-up

THE CHANGELING (Aldwych): Steamily persuasive production of a Jacobean blood-and-lust masterpiece. Diana Quick excels herself as the fair murderess.

CLOUDS (Duke of York's): British Sunday paper journalists go junketing in Cuba and find that everything is in the eye of the beholder. Michael Frayn's best play yet.

THE DOUBLE DEALER (Olivier): The National Theatre regains its grip on Restoration comedy. Michael Bryant and Dorothy Tutin are going to be legends.

EGO JOO (Criterion): Michael Hastings's quick-witted farce on how to become a legal immigrant in one easy wedding.

MARY BARNES (Royal Court): Patti Love gives a stunning performance as a young nurse whose deliverance from schizophrenia (courtesy of R. D. Laing) has been dramatised by David Edgar with a judicious blend of irony and concern.

THE PHILANDERER (Lyttelton): Sparkling revival of Shaw's second play and one of his best; Dinsdale Landen plays, more or less, G.B.S. himself, the author being in a merrily self-scourging mood.

SARATOGA (Aldwych): Early American comedy that may never be revived again, but which Ronald Eyre and his company have made the occasion for a fine frolic that includes some of the best song and dance in town.

STRIFE (Olivier): A very pleasant surprise: Galsworthy's strike drama may lack something as a tract on industrial relations, but in Christopher Morahan's granite-hard production, it lives as a study of human obstinacy on the grand scale -- which is just how the author intended it.
