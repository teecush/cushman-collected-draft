Molière's dying fall

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

The National Theatre is putting 'Le Malade Imaginaire' -- translated for once under the obvious and sensible title of The Hypochondriac -- as possibly Molière's greatest comedy and his last. Molière disposed of the second matter by dying four days into the original run. As for the first idea, I never heard it before -- but whoever believes it ought to be locked up.

Dying is the greatest thing Molière did in this play: that he, a noted hypochondriac, should have collapsed while actually appearing as one, is the finest of all sick jokes. It is not an effect readily available to a modern revival, though in his Olivier production Michael Bogdanov has gone some way towards finding an equivalent. But for most of its length the play is cut to formula: a pair of young lovers helped by a maidservant to foil the heroine's foolish father, who wishes her to marry some unsuitable suitor in line with his own obsession: a religious zealot or a fashionable poet or, in this case, a doctor. Between times the old man executes comic cadenzas in accordance with his particular humour.

At one point Argan, our imaginary invalid, feigns death in order to test his family's loyalty. 'Are you sure,' he asks before assuming the supine position, 'that this isn't dangerous?' For Molière, writing and then speaking that line while actually dying, it must have carried an irrecoverable charge. The rest of us have to be content with the basic joke of a thoroughly healthy man indulging himself with fantasied illness, and it is this joke that Daniel Massey plays, on all the stops of his flexible vocal pipe, balancing cures against enemas (his favourite vice) and both against cost. (Molière seems to have carried some of The Miser over into this play) or somersaulting over his sickbed to beat his younger daughter; the only dark moment in the play.

Around him Mr Bogdanov builds a presentation, not only of the play, but of its tradition, its author and its historical moment. The original is interspersed with commedia dell'arte sequences; Mr Bogdanov offers a whole festival and a troupe, who at one point -- in deference to Molière's repeated use of this plot and this subject -- perform a potted version of one of his earlier medical farces. At the end Argan himself becomes a doctor in a musical ceremony, which here culminates in an apparition of death: meaningless if you don't know the circumstances of the play's first production, but chilling if you do.

He also shows -- aided by Polly James's small northern maid, Emily Morgan's delightfully doubled daughters, Clive Arrindell's resourceful swain, and Anna Carteret's sweetly smothering stepma -- how much life Molière could still extract from the old stereotypes; indeed how at this stage he could do it without apparently trying.

A chorus of quacks with syringes rampant lend appropriate support; and the medical satire is delivered loud and clear, which seems to me all you could reasonably ask of it. (Sometimes it's too loud; Michael Bryant makes the regulation rational brother unexpectedly raucous.) The sum, in a rather pedestrian translation by Alan Drury, is solidly amusing rather than elating, but I doubt if a really great production of this play is possible. And I don't want Mr Massey to die to prove me wrong.

* * *

Doctors have always taken a hammering in French comedy. In Feydeau they are usually lechers; his Fitting for Ladies (Orange Tree, Richmond) shows us a fashionable physician who, to evade his wife and his mother-in-law and his ex-mistress, is forced to pretend a sudden conversion to the dressmaking trade. Hence the title: though in this production the play proves far more fitting for gentlemen; the actors are economically funny, while the actresses fuss about. This is a pocket-sized version of an economic play, reassembling all the loved familiar elements. I prefer my Feydeau full-blown; on the other hand it might be pleasant to see a commedia troupe whip through it in five minutes.

* * *

Two regional sidelights on the new depression. In Nick Darke's The Catch (Theatre Upstairs) an ageing fisherman, hit by the loss of his territorial waters to all the frog fisher-folk, turns first to junk-selling, then -- at the instigation of a disliked unthrifty son-in-law -- to cocaine smuggling. But he can't stand the pace. Neither, at first, could I; Cornish leisureliness began to turn my thoughts in other directions. Eventually though I smelt some fragrant dialogue, the beginnings of a story, and a fruitful Steptoe-ish relationship. Later Mr Darke turns some effective screws. There is a proddingly comic performance by Mike Grady, and a masterful one by Tom Watson.

From Liverpool Playhouse comes Blood on the Dole (Tricycle), a first play by Jim Morris, set in a Job Centre. At least the production is; the play roams all over town, and the orange poles and furnishings become constricting. That of course is the point; but it would have come across anyway. We are with four school-leavers. The girls take secretarial courses, and end up working in a supermarket. The boys can find no jobs, and try to set up their own, with invariably farcical results. Finally one settles for the army and is killed in Ireland; his friend is left to mourn him in a scene over-written but (by Andrew Schofield) powerfully played. The play probably cohered better in its home studio than in the narrow open spaces of the Tricycle; if it seems desultory now, then so, perforce, are the characters' lives.

* * *

The Aldwych is running a Ralph Koltai season; to the towering barracks and foundry of his Love-Girl and the Innocent and the thrusting platform of his Hamlet are now added the flat walls (less menacing than at Stratford) of his Romeo and Juliet. This will probably go down as the revival without a balcony; the lovers wander together in the garden, which is a pleasant shock while it lasts, but ultimately destructive to the careful escalation of their four-scene romance: from public meeting to impeded rendezvous to chaperoned marriage to bed. They are alone and untrammelled only once.

Judy Buxton is a pallid Juliet but Anton Lesser's feverish Romeo is now in control of the text, which is more than can be said for most of his contemporaries. However, Trevor Baxter and Barbara Kinghorn as the mismatched elder Capulets graphically illustrate why Juliet should have left home; so, in her warmer way, does Brenda Bruce's Nurse, making the girl's bed so as not to face her when telling her to marry Paris.
