More murder

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Simon Gray's Molly and King Lear.

A FEW belated thoughts on David Jones's recent article in this newspaper concerning the relative merits of stage and television drama.

First, he is undoubtedly right when he says that a great deal of professional theatre is done essentially to impress other theatre professionals; it is surprising, though, that he considers the situation in television to be any different.

Talk to any TV writer or director working on anything more ambitious than Coronation Street (and I am not sure I should make even that qualification) and you will find his standards to be every bit as rarefied and inbred as those of his theatrical counterparts. This does not imply indifference to either viewing or box office figures. Aesthetic attitudes, though, are largely determined by who you drink with, and few punters find their way into the studio bar-rooms.

Again, Mr Jones correctly attributes the fragmentation of stage drama into numberless short scenes to the influence of television; he omits to mention that, except in the rare case of an Edward Bond, this is not a blessing but a curse. Finally it remains true that a television play is a smaller (not necessarily lesser) thing than a stage play; it can more easily arouse intense interest in a miniature subject.

Which brings me to Simon Gray's Molly (Comedy), a stage version of his teleplay Death of a Teddy Bear; a good and clever piece, but one which now leaves its audience with an uncomfortable feeling of confinement; its subject is not quite large enough to fill its space.

It is based, like Terence Rattigan's Cause Celebre, on the Alma Rattenbury murder case, and though Mr Gray's characterisation is considerably more subtle and detailed than Rattigan's, and he completely avoids the platitudes to which Rattigan sometimes resorted, his is still the less powerful piece. The subject seems to matter less to him. And Rattigan's instinct in providing a parallel plot to the Rattenbury story itself is now vindicated; it made the experience worth going out for.

Still, given that you have got yourself into the theatre in the first place, there is plenty to enjoy in Molly. Mr Gray has restructured the story around four characters--all, as Molly (his Alma) says, essentially children. There is Molly herself, the unproductive amateur songwriter, once divorced, now back from Canada with her new husband and "needing her sex." There is Teddy, the deaf and ailing mate who cannot give it to her (and never has), but who spanks her instead. There is Olly, the young chauffeur-gardener, "not quite right in the head," to whom she turns for relief and who has the bad luck to fall in love with her as a result. And there is Evy, the housekeeper, censorious but faithful, and also in love with Molly.

They make a sad collection (bleak you would call it if Mr Gray's style were a little more expansive). Olly's is the only consummated passion among them and it causes him to murder Teddy. For Molly actually loves Teddy, and it is the last-minute revelation of this fact (as he bleeds to death) that furnishes, as in Rattigan, the strongest moment in the play. Mr Gray's heroine is far less dependent on her lover than Rattigan's; she may need him physically, but she could always find another and she never hesitates to pull rank. At the end of the play, with both men dead, she settles down with Evy for--presumably--another lifetime of passion denied.

Stephen Hollis's production puts the play, firmly but discreetly, into what we must now recognise (give the telly its due) as the Dennis Potter 1930s. The period is perhaps most palpably there in the pinched, waddling vowels that Billie Whitelaw employs for Molly's silly coaxing moods. It is plain that she despises this part of herself (the refrain "pretty please with sugar on it" takes on a thicker layer of disgust each time it appears), but this cannot quite conceal a discontinuity with the woman of the world who appears when Molly sets about subduing her admirers.

Miss Whitelaw in effect offers two stunning performances: less satisfying than one would be, but still the most powerful female acting seen in London this year. She gets good support from Barbara Atkinson and Anthony Allen; T. P. McKenna as Teddy offers no more than routine testiness until his end approaches; then he takes off into a splendid tirade.

* * *

Prospect's King Lear (Old Vic) begins with thunder, causing the unwary to suspect that the first half of the play has disappeared. A couple more cliches follow. When Lear swears at Cordelia all present drop on their knees and shield their faces with their arms. Gloucester in the next scene should enter talking to himself; here he addresses his rambling remarks to an attendant, who then has to exit as if he has received some kind of instruction. After this the production disappears and the actors are left to their own devices.

These are frequently oversimple, but Christopher Neame and Isla Blair make a glamorous couple of Edmund and Regan, while Trevor Martin as Kent does the basso rugged honesty bit better than any actor I know.

Anthony Quayle's Lear is incomplete; he has the raw power for the rages, but not the transfusing variety. What he does convey, often very movingly, is the sense of a mind long set and forced to reorder itself. There is a military directness about him; when he calls on Nature to dry up his daughter's womb or speaks, admitting his ignorance, of the terrors of the earth, then he means what he says. "I would not be mad" is apprehensive, lightly tearful, so that "I shall go mad" a little later is hopelessly, crushingly logical.

I have known no actor make more than Mr Quayle unfussily does of Lear's gentlest moments: his concern for the Fool or his sudden fear of "the little dogs ... Trey, Blanch and Sweetheart." This is a very English Lear who, whatever his failings with people, must always have been wonderful with animals.
