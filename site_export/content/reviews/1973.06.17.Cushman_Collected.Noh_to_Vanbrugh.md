Noh to Vanbrugh

THEATRE

Robert Cushman on a week of classic plays.

THERE has hardly been a play to be had this week much below the age of 300. It began, appropriately, with the Umewaka Noh Troupe, concluding their two-week season at the Aldwych.

I found them very restful, and less boring than many groups supposedly more accessible. It is heartening anyway, at a World Theatre Season, to meet with visitors so far outside our comprehension as to be beyond the reach even of simultaneous translation. Free of those crackling translations and their monotone messages, we could attend to the sound of the actors' stockinged feet upon the polished wooden floor, as much a part of the spectacle as the masks, the silken gowns, the chanting, and the guttural croakings that are the nearest these Japanese actors come to expressing recognisable emotion (though having once seen one of last week's plays in an English translation, I can testify that, divorced from its traditional presentation, Noh drama can be made to yield explicit emotional content).

While I doubt that the Umewaka troupe have taught our theatre much that it did not, in theory, know already about the strengths of formality and simplicity, it is pleasant to see the theories put so thoroughly into practice. It is pleasant, too, to have a demonstration of the basic principles of comic relief. Sandwiched between each pair of Noh dramas is a short farce; after half an hour of masked solemnity, the impact of a human face--emitting sounds that could only be human laughter--is almost physical.

Once there were modern-dress productions of Shakespeare. They were delightfully shocking, meticulously detailed (telephones always figured prominently) and only done occasionally, so as to jolt the audience out of their presumed complacency. They look, in photographs, extraordinarily dated. These days a new convention has taken hold. Pop has come among us, and, by introducing a style of clothing that is tied to no particular period, but thrives on joky anachronism, it has made casual, timeless Shakespeare a possibility. Two Gentlemen of Verona was cast in this mould, both at its most recent Stratford appearance and in the current rock version; so was As You Like It, first with an all-male cast at the National Theatre, and now in a new production at Stratford.

The style only obtrudes itself where, logically, it should be most useful. A unisex costume for Rosalind would seem to be the perfect solution to the problem of her male disguise. In her tight-fitting jacket and jeans, she could just as easily be a boy as a girl. She could, however, just as easily be a girl as a boy and I have never felt less inclination to suspend my disbelief.

Eileen Atkins has a wary, fine-drawn talent, and there is one corner of Rosalind--the mordant satirist of romantic attitudes--of which she takes complete possession. But the undertow of erotic abandon is barely there at all, and even the irony is muted. At moments, David Suchet's vigorous, confident Orlando (an understudy performance, calling for no indulgence whatever) was running rings round her and, in this play, that is the wrong way round for rings to be run.

Miss Atkins delivers the epilogue splendidly (with the houselights up as they should be), but the end of the play inevitably suffers at the moment from the ankle-injury which keeps her out of the final marriage-dance (as it nearly kept her out of the first night altogether). By this time, anyway, Buzz Goodbody's direction has run out of steam. She clearly recognises the importance of the marriage theme, but her methods of underlining it are tired. Grinning supers fling confetti into the house, and the audience gamely grins and wishes it would all go away.

These tactics spoil an agreeable lightweight production which has little to say on the play's main themes (court or country?--you choose), but makes good sense of individual scenes. The mod trappings are a great help to Brian Glover, puffing with professional pride as Charles the wrestler, and they work well, too, for Clement McCallin's icily capricious Duke Frederick. When we reach the forest, it is a great comfort to find it in the charge of Tony Church, a pipesmoking banished Duke, mercifully unembarrassed by his own pronouncement. Particularly telling is his rebuke to the cynically moralising Jaques: "For thou thyself hast been a libertine/As sensual as the brutish sting itself. . ."

Who would have guessed that Shakespeare had Malcolm Muggeridge's number so accurately?

In Richard Pasco's hands, Jaques becomes almost the central character of the play--paradoxically, really, since he spends most of his time prowling its periphery, peering at the action with beady distaste through rimless glasses. The mere sight of the amorous Touchstone and Audrey sends a visible shudder through him. I sympathised; they are played grossly enough for me to contribute a few winces myself.

For an even richer study of an Elizabethan malcontent, you go--reasonably enough--to The Malcontent; John Marston's play has now come from Nottingham to London's Bankside Globe Playhouse.

The experience is not without hazard, since neither playhouse nor play is innocent of chilly gaps in its structure. Shakespeare was almost unique among his contemporaries in knowing how to pace himself. In this piece, the work of an inchoate satirist whose quest for moral certainties finally took him into the Church, words and events tumble out, still knotted together and at breakneck speed.

Breakneck is the word. This is the conventional English idea of an Italian Renaissance Court, every man someone else's hired assassin, and yet--for once--every character ends up alive. The play is a comedy or, as Jonathan Miller has chosen to direct it, a satiric farce. His court is an assemblage of shadowy white-faced grotesques; setting and costumes suggest gorgeousness gone into decay. The treatment is sometimes too insistent; these dramatists were prone to sudden changes of gear, and Marston probably intended some of his twists of plot to be a good more than a giggle. The play, like many of its age, including the genuine revenge dramas of Webster and Tourneur, is apt to slide into parody with disconcerting speed; Mr Miller hardly ever lets it slide out. But he has accurately located the play's lunatic gusto and has, more surprisingly, done serious justice to its fits of morality.

Mr Miller's style is well suited to a cast generally stronger on enthusiasm than finesse. Hazel Hughes's old bawd is irreproachable, and there can be no reservations at all about Derek Godfrey. This malcontent is another banished duke, but one who has no intention of waiting for his supplanter to be converted by an old religious man (though he gets him to impersonate one). Mr Godfrey is always a witty, stylish actor; sometimes he lets loose a gleeful demon who, on this occasion, has one of his richest outings ever.

I would have welcomed his irruption during a leaden revival of Vanbrugh's The Provok'd Wife (Greenwich), which gets us as far as the 1690s. Next week I hope to venture into the twentieth century.
