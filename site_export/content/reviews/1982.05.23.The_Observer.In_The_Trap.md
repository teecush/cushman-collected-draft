In the trap

HERE they are again, Uncle Vanya and Auntie Hedda, trapped in their lives and their families, unlucky in love, mucking about with firearms, tramping round large rooms in impossible houses -- a point much stressed in both the new revivals.

Professor Serebryakov complains in the course of Uncle Vanya of the impossibility of finding anyone in a ramshackle establishment with 26 rooms. The National production makes it easy to sympathise with him. (An achievement, since he is not generally a sympathetic character.) Just as we may all have thought we were coming to terms with the size of the Lyttelton, here is a production that puts us back at the wrong end of the telescope. John Bury's sets extend to the edge of the proscenium and apparently beyond. Curtains doing duty for walls seem to extend into infinity.

Michael Bogdanov's production, maybe making a virtue out of necessity, is concerned to distance us, anyway. Like Mr Bury's designs, it is fine on its own terms, but unsatisfying. In the years since Tynan described Vanya himself as almost unplayable, a host of fine, occasionally great, performances have arisen to confute him. Michael Redgrave, Paul Scofield, Leo McKern, Nigel Hawthorne and Basil Henson's account of the gouty great-man himself is the best I have seen, the only one to make a case for the character.

The character has become a myth; the scene in which he talks feverishly of his wasted life and blighted ambitions, realises his foolishness, attempts to shoot the professor, bungles that too, and collapses in tragicomic fury, goes deeper than anything in modern drama. You have to be very smug not to recognise a piece of yourself in it. (And, as the Actors' Company once showed us, it carries some of the same charge even in the imperfect early version; The Wood Demon proof that Chekhov had struck gold even if at first he was unsure how to invest it.) But here it goes by with remarkably little disturbance.

From the moment he enters -- or, in this version, falls out of a swing -- Michael Bryant's Vanya is an outstandingly capable performance; too capable, perhaps, to have landed in Vanya's morass. As prescribed, he dresses well; but it seems less compensation than his natural mode. Self-hatred burns fiercer in Diaghilev; Langton's Astrov; there is unusual emphasis on the doctor's drunkenness and bitter sense of professional incapacity; he is, too, an active ladykiller.

One of Mr Bogdanov's happiest notions is the procession that follows the professor across the stage at the beginning. It establishes Cherie Lunghi's Yelena, indolent and inhibited, and Patti Love's Sonya, bustling to keep from crying, on lines from which they never deviate.

* * *

Hedda Gabler (Cambridge) inhabits an amphitheatre of a living-room, hung with draperies depicting spread-tailed peacocks. There is a French window for Judge Brack's regular entrance, a prominent bust of General Gabler and, stage-centre, a resplendent stove with a funnel so thick it might double as a Grecian column. This might be what inspired Hedda, who sees herself as a tragic heroine, to choose the house; one feels that, having chosen it, she deserves everything she gets.

This seems too to be the view of Susannah York, resplendently gowned twice over, and with as bronzed a voice as you could wish of a leading lady. She sails hatefully and uncompromisingly through the night. The play is one of which I must confess myself thoroughly tired, but Miss York's briskness does much to rescue it. It is far more satisfying, and fairer to women, than the new idea of Hedda as victim: whoever respected a victim of anything? It is not, for all its strengths, the most vapid, joyous performance in the world, and the same is true of its accomplices.

Tom Baker gives us a Brack whose every smile turns into a satyr's grin, but whom you can certainly imagine bullying witnesses. Ralph Bates is a nice old Tesman, even though young; Irene Handl a kind and even perspicacious Aunt Julie, who would certainly have driven Hedda to distraction (and who gets proper support in this line from Gretchen Franklin's maid); and Tom Bell a Lovborg with the proviso that Mr Wilson and Mr Firbank worlds collide; instead of running teasingly in parallel.

* * *

Valmouth

That at any rate is supposed to be the point, and I will accept it with the proviso that Mr Wilson Dexter, way off form, would have written jaunty melodies with surprising modulations anyway. His dialogue is cunning in its authenticity, but it is unfortunate that since 1958 camp has moved on, leaving Valmouth with the fatal sound of self-conscious, self-willed sophistication. All that now remains is the occasionally highly concentrated line, like Lady Parvula's about an unresponsive shepherd: 'Oh, I'd like to spank the white walls of his cottage.' That gives you dainty lust, quaint pastoralism, everything except religious satire.

Four members of the original cast remain and, since they are all playing freaks or degenerates, time has hardly impaired them: Fenella Fielding as Parvula, the role that established her and has haunted her ever since; Doris Hare as Grannie Tooke; Marcia Ashton as a singing nun; and Bertice Reading as Mrs Yajnavalkya, transformed from Oriental masseuse to Pearl Bailey jazz queen.

Robert Helpmann makes what amounts to a guest appearance as Cardinal Pirelli, but the best newcomers are Cheryl Kennedy as, most definitely, a wench, and Judy Campbell as the arch-religieuse, going through the action spectacularly dominoed, in search of a heretic, whether to burn or to convert remains uncertain. Indeed none of the various plot-lines amount to much, and they desperately need a proscenium arch to make their inconsequence charming. I suspect the production, rehearsed in tandem with Of Thee I Sing, opened unprepared: on the first night even Miss Reading lacked verve. The director is John Dexter, way off form.
