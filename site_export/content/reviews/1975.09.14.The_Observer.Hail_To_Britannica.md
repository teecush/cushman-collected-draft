Hail to Britannica

REWORKING Racine's Phedre under the title Phaedra Britannica (Old Vic), Tony Harrison has moved the action forward from mythical Greece (where Racine found it in Euripides and where he left it) to nineteenth-century India.

Phaedra becomes the Memsahib (all three syllables pronounced in order to preserve the rhythms of Mr Harrison's couplets) lusting after her stepson Thomas Theophilus. Theseus is now her husband the Governor, working hard at slaying tigers, seducing native girls and attempting to stave off the Indian Mutiny. The divinities who tear Racine's characters are now the gods of India--or, as the baffled English characters habitually exclaim, merely "India" itself. The mythology has to be fed to us along with the narrative, which may be why the adaptation seems both longer and slower than the original.

There is nothing wrong with this; not, at least, in the National Theatre production. John Dexter, directing, has literally got Mr Harrison's measure; he has adopted the same hypnotic gait. Tanya Moiseiwitsch, the doyenne and quite possibly the best of British stage designers, has furnished a sweeping colonnade which seems to stretch further the longer you look at it; classical columns are interspersed with tropical blinds, while a menacing deity squats over a huge carved doorway. Within this baleful surround Mr Dexter has conjured performances of uncommon dignity, passion, discipline and weight.

We wait for the weather as much as the characters to break. "Could even come today, the first cloudburst/This damned hot weather's at its very worst," is among the earliest of Mr Harrison's couplets. The refrain develops in time with the fable; the lines that have stayed with me most surely are delivered by Michael Gough as the Governor, gazing through the blinds to summon the cataclysm: "I hope whatever happens happens soon/Then everything washed clean by the monsoon." He gets his wish.

Historically Mr Harrison's achievement is remarkable. We measure Shakespeare's maturity by his abandonment of rhyme for blank verse and even Dryden (whose preference for exotic settings may have influenced Mr Harrison) made the same transition in his one actable tragedy. Mr Harrison's may be the first serious success of any English dramatist in rhyming couplets. Sometimes his very ingenuity works against him; our delight in his adroit solution of his problems unduly lightens the texture of the experience. His cleverness cannot always escape the kind of frigidity endemic to English versions of classical drama, French or Greek.

He has built his edifice with enormous care and cunning. Just as the sea encircled Racine's play, so this version is surrounded by the jungle, Phedre's rejected nurse drowned herself; when similarly treated the Memsahib's Ayah (given a brilliantly incisive foxy performance by Alaknanda Samarth) heads for the woods. "The jungle swallowed her." What have initially seemed self-conscious references to the savage strangeness of the landscape have sharpened and strengthened to culminate in the horror of those four words. As with the country so with its inhabitants; beasts, monsters, brutes (tigers are obviously the least of it) stalk the text until the last arrives to destroy Thomas Theophilus as the sea-dragon put paid to Hippolytus.

The catastrophe is stunningly reported by Robert Eddison, a strange kind of great actor who flourishes on the edges and in the corners of great drama: witness his murderer at the tail-end of Edward II. His iron control of form allows him an untrammelled passage through the verse, a smooth flow which in the first scene combines with David Yelland's staccato attack as Thomas to demonstrate the resource of the production and the possibilities of the text; though Mr Yelland, while characterising excellently, betrays a weakness for forced, ingenuous pauses.

Fearful though Mr Harrison's jungle monster may be, he has not quite the logic of Racine's sea-beast; to ask a hostile sub-continent to devour your son is not quite the same as begging the favour of a marine god who happens to owe you a good turn. And a Phaedra who can find no peace even in death because her father is judge of the underworld must exert more force than one who merely trembles at the thought of what her daddy would say to her. Racine's heroine was of erotic lineage; she brought sex with her. Diana Rigg's Memsahib arrives an innocent and blames lust on the climate. To obscure her from half the audience when she declares her love is one of Mr Dexter's rare gaffes; but a significant one. The bias of the performance is towards justifying the woman's repression, which is done with ashen conviction, and towards expressing her jealousy, in which Miss Rigg is electric. Technically she matches Mr Eddison--surpasses him considering the demands made on her--and she is obviously our next-but-one Cleopatra (Dorothy Tutin being first in line), but as Phaedra she skirts eroticism; indeed her skirts, which make her look like a young Queen Victoria gone untimely into widowhood, may be to blame. The atmospherics finally count for more than the narrative and the adaptation could be self-defeating (even with our classical education in tatters we might find Venus more accessible than Shiva), but it aims higher and gets farther than anything else this year.

Certainly it dwarfs the National's two latest offerings at the ICA. Barry Collins's Judgement (to be restaged in the main house, wherever that turns out to be) is a two and a half hour monologue, undeniably well written but what of that, delivered by the only sane survivor of a group of men driven to cannibalism. We are supposed to judge him; though I would not dream of doing so, Colin Blakely plays with a fearful relaxed urgency, his voice like a stream of unwelcome thoughts pressing upon the brain. He impresses like a man, not an actor; few others have that gift. But he was so casual that little of what he said penetrated to my corner of the room, so I left dissatisfied. Out of the Mouth of Crabs, Ron Daniels's compilation on Sir Walter Raleigh, piling anachronism on inconsequence, is pretty well indefensible.

* * *

Happy as a Sandbag (Ambassadors) is an anthology of entertainment 1939-45, written by Ken Lee, though what he wrote is uncertain. Not the songs, which are standards, nor the best jokes, which were the property of Max Miller. They prove (as did Lenny) that first-class stand-up material may even outlast its original exponent, who is here lovingly and lovably impersonated by Roy Macready. Darlene Johnson gets several acute cameos; indeed the performance level, under Philip Hedley's precision direction, is captivatingly high. Much fun is to be had, but so is some discomfort. Not that anything nasty is shown on stage, apart from some rations. I would be happier if it were.

The evening ends with the cast singing (and the audience, berserk with nostalgia, joining in) "Here's to the Next Time." As the envoy to a show about war this strikes me as sinister.
