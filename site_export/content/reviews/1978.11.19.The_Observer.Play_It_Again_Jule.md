Play it again, Jule

ROBERT CUSHMAN talks to Jule Styne, composer of 'Bar Mitzvah Boy'

JULE STYNE is 73 years old, short and very active. When I first met him four years ago, backstage at a theatre in Toronto, I took him to be a 50-year-old who, if he kept jumping around like that, would certainly give himself ulcers. He tended to jump in time to his own music, and that music was generally very rhythmic, coming as it did from Gypsy, the most highly-regarded of the 29 musicals he has composed. Currently it is more likely to be that of Bar Mitzvah Boy, the latest in the line and the first to be launched in London rather than New York.

Two more British musicals are lined up for him--if, that is, he can overcome the impression left him by some of the Bar Mitzvah Boy reviews that there is an unbridgeable gulf between himself and the British critics. "What we call honest sentiment," he says in equally honest puzzlement, "you call schmaltz."

Among currently practising Broadway composers only Richard Rodgers has a more extensive and impressive catalogue. The Styne shows include Gentlemen Prefer Blondes, Bells are Ringing, and Funny Girl. And there has also been a long list of song-hits, many of them written for the movies in the forties before his theatrical career got under way.

Stephen Sondheim, who was his lyricist on Gypsy, says that Styne's fecundity of invention is extraordinary. "He'd rather write a completely new song than re-write an old one." He prefers not to work at the piano. He was a child-prodigy pianist, and it seems to have been his size that kept him from an adult concert career (his hands weren't big enough). So playing remains for him a different kind of work from writing--and separating the two allows him to concentrate on melody.

All the same, when he does get to the piano--at a party, perhaps--interesting and fruitful things can happen. What started as an improvised parody of the economic, repetitive songs of Vincent Youmans (Tea for Two, I Want to Be Happy) ended up as Just in Time, the big hit of Bells are Ringing. Then there was Time after Time, one of his Hollywood songs, sung by Frank Sinatra in a 1947 movie. Alec Wilder in his indispensable book American Popular Song describes it as "his best song ... It bespeaks personal involvement and great warmth."

It is doubtful if Mr Wilder realises the exact nature of that involvement. Styne tells it like this. "A lot of us used to go round to Sinatra's house and play parlour-games, to try and keep his marriage together. One day Sinatra asked if anyone had heard any of the songs Jerome Kern was meant to be composing for the show that Irving Berlin eventually wrote as Annie Get Your Gun. I said I had--I don't know why, actually I hadn't heard a note. So they said, play us one.

"I couldn't go back on it. I crossed to the piano, honest to God not knowing what I was going to play, only that it had to be in the style of Kern. I sat down, and I improvised the first eight bars of Time After Time. Then right on, through to the end. And everybody said 'it's wonderful, that Kern, he's done it again.' And having made the tune up, I couldn't remember it. But Sammy Cahn could, and he wrote a lyric to it."

Despite such casual triumphs, Styne left Hollywood for Broadway "to become a composer instead of just a songwriter." Though most of his early shows were successes, it took him some time to achieve this to his own satisfaction. Jerome Robbins staged his sixth show, a musical version of Peter Pan. "Robbins taught me to use music to underline the plot and develop the characters."

So what, applying these standards, could music add to Jack Rosenthal's television play Bar Mitzvah Boy? Well, according to Styne, "emotion, vitality, and a new dimension for Eliot, the boy whose bar mitzvah it is." According to the critics, a certain softness. Maybe there is not so much disagreement there. The new dimension, in Eliot mainly, involves his relationship with his cab-driver father who, late in the evening, sings him a conciliatory ballad called The Sun Shines Out of Your Eyes.

But Styne insists that family affection is what the love musical stage could best add to the story, and insists that it means a great deal to him personally. "When my son had his bar mitzvah he had a hard time too. But I got through to him by telling him what an important experience it had been for me. This is my Jewish show; I'm paying a personal debt."

Which does not quite make up for a certain stickiness in the way the song, like the parents' duet, We've Done All Right, comes across on stage. However: I went along to the recording of the original cast LP and found that, out of visual context, both grew on me appealingly. They sounded like more than respectable additions to his list of ballads.

"You hear that?" he said during a short Hebraic chanting section from the first number, in which Eliot is jauntily lectured by his rabbi on his doubts about the coming ceremony. "There's the whole guts of Jack's story in that music. In America I would have got credit for that." He didn't spend too much time brooding on it, though. One of his first jobs was as a vocal coach and he still has the instincts. He continually disappeared into the studio to admonish or encourage the performers. "The trouble is, when they get in front of a microphone they think they're singers. I need actor-singers; I can't stand singer-singers."

When you watch a musical in rehearsal it's difficult to believe that it could ever fail: all that vivacity, all that discipline. When Bar Mitzvah Boy was being rehearsed in London I asked Styne whether he saw himself having to write new material during the Manchester try-out. He was confident that no more would be needed.

I saw a few minutes of a number about catering arrangements: "When it's once in a lifetime, when it's your little boy/You don't want to settle for, say, the Savoy." It seemed fun (lyrics by Don Black, who is philosophical about being ignored in reviews), but it perished on the road (if Manchester is the road). "It didn't add up," says Styne, "a one-joke song." They replaced it with a yet more rousing chorus called The Bar Mitzvah of Eliot Green (pom-pom).

Maybe that should have been the title song. Styne now thinks their greatest mistake was keeping the TV title; it invited irrelevant considerations about fidelity to the original. How sore is he at the carpers? "Listen. I believe in the critic-system. If it wasn't for you fellas, everything would run forever and we'd never get any new shows on." He's done all right.
