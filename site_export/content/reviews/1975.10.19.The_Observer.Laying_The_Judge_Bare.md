Laying the judge bare

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

THE title-figure of Howard Barker's Stripwell (Royal Court) is a man of variable disposition, and this seems to be one of the things his author has against him. Different people know him as different things: to his wife and son he is Graham, to his mistress Strip, a name to which her profession lends a certain displaced appropriateness. She is a go-go dancer, and you do not have to delve very far into contemporary mythology to discover what kind of elderly man would be hanging around her. Stripwell is a judge.

We catch him at it - the judging, I mean - at the very beginning of the play. He is about to hand out to a young tearaway what may be the toughest sentence imposed for misappropriating a motor-car since the celebrated case of Mr Toad. Asked for his comments before sentence is passed, the prisoner offers the following: "If you put me away, I'll escape and murder you."

Hearing this, Strip takes a recess, during which he consults his girlfriend (who arranges her touring dates to coincide with his) and returns to administer a term of 12 months. "Will that," he inquires nervously, "be all right?" The accused says that it won't and reiterates his threat. At the end of the play he returns to carry it out, his reappearance charged with just the right blend of necessity and surprise.

The play thus boasts a witty start and a powerful finish; only the in-between, which is of course the longest bit, goes wrong. Stripwell's nervous behaviour in court has shown him not to have the courage of his convictions; the rest of the play denies him even the convictions.

The promise from the dock impels him to examine his threatened life. He determines to introduce some truth and decision into it; to begin with he will leave his wife. Unfortunately his go-go girl sees him mainly as material for her autobiography and ditches him, in the interest of youth and her next chapter; Stripwell's own son, a prankish drug-trafficker who smuggles heroin in the private parts of female elephants, supplants his father. Stripwell is left bemoaning his fate: "She didn't want me because she'd fallen for somebody else."

There is no escaping the indebtedness of that line and of the whole situation to a hundred matinee-dramas. On what Mr Barker has given us we can feel sorry for Stripwell or bored with him or (as in my case) a bit of both, but no more serious response is possible; we have not the evidence.

Strip and his wife converse for ages without giving us any sense of what their marriage was like; Constance Cummings is left being stylish in a vacuum. The two young characters - conventional caricatures of cool - carry even less conviction in the writing and, though Patricia Quinn jerks a mean abdomen, hardly any in the acting.

All our sympathies go to the leading character which, with Michael Hordern playing him, was probably inevitable. He has all the best lines or at least (an attribute common to most of Mr Hordern's roles) he sounds as if he has. At first the character seems likely to become another Hordern eccentric, victimised but jovial. With superb resource he lays this familiar spectre, but in doing so makes a better case for hesitant liberalism than Mr Barker can have envisaged. His good intentions count for more than his backslidings and his isolation for more than either. With his extraordinary face (indestructibly lean, for all its pouches), he becomes a figure of pathos, if not of tragedy: a sardonic Don Quixote with, unfortunately, nothing to tilt against.

* * *

Earlier this year, Mr Barker's play Claw, at the Open Space drew apocalyptic conclusions from cartooned premises; the imbalance was plain, but, since the apocalypse was gripping and the cartoon fairly funny, forgivable. Moving up to the Royal Court he has tried for greater density, but only achieved sobriety, which is far less interesting. It has infected the production too: Chris Parr's handling of Claw was beautifully fluid. At the Court, given sets to play with, he lets the brief scenes trundle.

* * *

The kaleidoscopic method works much better in the studio confines of the Bush, where Stephen Poliakoff's City Sugar puts a supermarket, a bed-sit and a local radio station simultaneously on stage and leaves us to decide which is the most depressing. The play itself - though it enlarges on the pessimistic view of life in Leicester propounded by Mr Poliakoff's Hitting Town - is far from depressing. It pits a disc-jockey who has known more hopeful times against a salesgirl for whom there is only now: hard to say which cynicism is the more corroding. Both are to an extent their author's puppets, but they give Lynne Miller and John Shrapnel enough to go on.

Mr Shrapnel indeed is given, and returns, rather more. On one level he obviously enjoys the DJ bit (as his author has enjoyed writing it: it's a generation's fantasy). On another his round owlish glasses and curving moustache give him an air at once appalled and appalling; his depression at the girl's ignorance of the Beatles is very real. So is his relationship with his nudgingly ambitious sound engineer (admirably played by Leon Vitali), his butt on and off the air.

* * *

Thomas and the King confirms that, after all their play and movie exposure, Henry II and Becket have passed as securely into showbiz folklore as Robin Hood - whom I kept expecting them to meet. They wander on, thoughtless playboy and tight-lipped retainer, for all the world like Bertie and Jeeves in the last musical shipwreck at Her Majesty's; this show is similarly maladroit and also (unlike 'Jeeves') plain stupid.
