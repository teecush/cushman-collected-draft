Happy impotence

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'I Love My Wife'

THE weaknesses in I Love My Wife (Prince of Wales) are fairly blatant.

Two couples in Trenton, New Jersey, decide with varying degrees of reluctance to celebrate Christmas Eve with an orgy. Husbands Alvin and Wally have some trouble persuading their spouses, Cleo and Monica, to play along, but finally break down their inhibitions by getting them high on marijuana.

The hash turns out to be modelling clay, and the game of four-in-a-bed is over before it has begun, since Alvin, who was always the shyest of the quartet, finds himself unable to perform with a woman not his wife. He sings the title song instead and Wally joins in.

This may be the first occasion on which impotence has constituted a happy ending; otherwise this musical is cut strictly to Broadway-type. It titillates with sex and drugs, but allows no consummation. The argument that these characters would, in fact, beat just that kind of retreat in real life is a powerful one, but they would surely have gone a little farther in experimentation first.

In order to keep them pure till the final curtain, Michael Stewart has had to pad out his slender story beyond its natural span. He does actually get his characters to bed, but he employs them, once there, in a series of coy slapstick routines which would be thoroughly embarrassing were they not so amusingly staged.

A note of praise having crept in at the end of that paragraph, I shall sustain it for most of what follows. My objections were raised more out of duty than conviction; I enjoyed the show mildly in New York last spring, and greatly in London last week. The British cast are not actually more accomplished than their American equivalents--in fact noticeably less. But their klutziness works for them.

I Love My Wife is a do-it-yourself musical; the four actors have to take care of the dancing and most of the singing, and the numbers are set up, out of dramatic context, as a series of essays in various pop genres. For the performers they are like rounds in a talent-contest and the bunch emerges slightly breathless but justifiably cheerful. Richard Beckinsale is the undoubted winner; his singing shapes steadily up towards a captivating rendition of the closer, and his comic timing--stonewalling until laughter becomes happily inescapable--is beautiful.

His Alvin is a different kind of slob from the one presented by his gifted predecessor, Lenny Baker; where Mr Baker gangled, he shambles, and where Mr Baker, even with a visibly unblemished skin, seemed ineradicably spotty, he is grinningly wholesome. Wally, the slickest of the foursome, gets a performance to match from Ben Cross; Deborah Fallender is an appealing and vocally stylish Cleo; and though Liz Robertson's Monica inclines to shrillness, she never really lets the side down.

Another quartet play piano, bass, drums, guitar, and the employees in Alvin's furniture-removal business. They are also a chorus; they are liable to break in brandishing their instruments (no, I don't suppose you can brandish a piano) and dressed, as the situation or the music may require, as tempting devils (with pretty red tails), reassuring Santas or simply as a band. They have their own share of musicians' charm--the appeal, like the actors, of being slightly out of their element.

Director (Gene Saks) and choreographer (Onna White) put the performers through some neat and funny paces; and everyone is buoyed up by Cy Coleman's sparkling score. Through rock, country, cod ballad, straight ballad, military march, buck and wing, and barrelhouse sing-along, Mr Coleman manages to keep faith with each convention while adding to it a lilt, a rhythmic pattern or a sense of fun that is entirely his own.

Mr Stewart's debut as a lyricist falls somewhere between the auspicious and the astounding; his images pile crisply and wittily up, many of his rhymes have a satisfying snap. What lyrics and music lack, however, is dramatic weight, which is partly an intrinsic failing and partly a comment on the action--which, in fact, is what they are designed to provide. But the action hardly calls for comment; it's too thin.

I am veering back to censure, so I shall end by humming a couple of the tunes to myself and assuring you that they make--after the hyped-up melodies of A Chorus Line and the denatured classics of Bubbling Brown Sugar--a very healthy sound.

* * *

Happy again, I turn from actors indulging in song-and-dance to a musical-comedy man giving a stunning home-straight performance. Bob Sherman appears in Are You Now, or Have You Ever Been? (May Fair) as the actor Larry Parks, who, to twist the irony a little further, became a star when he played, but did not sing, Al Jolson.

Soon afterwards his career crumbled as a result of his appearance before the House Un-American Activities Committee. Not that he was an unfriendly witness; he named names. They had to be wormed out of him though, and Mr Sherman gives an unforgettable performance, as real and immediate as the passing moment, of a man striving to make his judges set limits to his degradation.

Eric Bentley's play is taken verbatim from the records; it is concerned only with HUAC's siege of Broadway and Hollywood. A cast-member has recalled her disgust at hearing McCarthyism, HUAC's twin, described as the best goddamned television ever. I see what she means; all the same, I have to declare this piece very nearly the best goddamned theatre around.

I cannot imagine anyone failing to be gripped by it. Its three moods--of inquisition, confession and defiance--offer the actors immense opportunity and the response is the most direct we have enjoyed since Kennedy's Children. (Again the cast is mainly American.) There is not much heroism on view; the imprisoned Hollywood Ten are briefly dealt with, except for those who subsequently recanted.

Apart from Thomas Baptiste's ferocious Paul Robeson, we have to do mainly with uneasily repentant sinners, the more frightening since their brainwashing was largely self-administered, and petty persecutors; Ramsay Williams's chairman, a hoarse and thickset toad, is every Jack in any office.

* * *

The third Yankee import is The Dog Ran Away (Hampstead). The author, Brother Jonathan, is a monk who seems to be travelling all over, getting his plays put on, and his characters are hardly your conventional monasts either. There are three of them, in a Long Island house, with no visible sense of vocation--which could mean (a) that they have none or (b) that it exists too deep to require discussion.

One--probably a case of (a)--stays on and works; the other--a presumptive (b)--runs away like the symbolic hound of the title. They are both dully played, but their companion is more arresting; he is old and mad, and he is given by Robert Flemyng a performance so mannered that I wanted to follow the dog, but so artful that I had to stay.

I had the same divided feelings about the play: impatient that the subject was under-dramatised and under-explained, tempered with a respect not, I think, wholly inspired by the habit which--if his characters are any guide--Brother Jonathan hardly ever wears, even when home.

* * *

Phantom parody

KEEN fans of Phantom Captain, a group of controlled and grave actors who do what I can describe only as a sort of verbal performance art, will probably enjoy The Changeless Congress (King's Head), a parody of science fiction movies played first as a serious black-and-white thriller and then again as a Technicolor musical comedy--but its jokes and ideas were a bit thin for me. And I suspect that only Agatha Christie fanatics will hold their breath during A Murder Is Announced (Vaudeville), a plodding, if well-performed adaptation by Leslie Darbon which treads an interminable space of fishless water before it deigns to bait its hook.
