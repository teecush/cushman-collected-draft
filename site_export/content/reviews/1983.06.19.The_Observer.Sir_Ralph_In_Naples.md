Sir Ralph in Naples

THE cherished National Theatre production of Saturday Sunday Monday is still the image most of us have of the plays of Eduardo de Filippo. It was set in a Neapolitan kitchen and it was to a great extent a celebration of food. People in it may have suffered but, emphatically, none of them went hungry. However that play dates from the comparatively equable 1950s. Inner Voices (Lyttelton), the National's new de Filippo, was written soon after the war; and it depicts a Naples with its stomach emptied by occupation and defeat, and its morale depleted by years of Fascism.

It begins all right. In the kitchen of the Cimmaruta family macaroni is being cooked, for breakfast. An amusing neighbour drops in and amusingly wolfs it. There are family tensions, but of course there would be. A spinster aunt is possessive, Father is jealous of Mother, who supports the family by telling fortunes, in suspiciously long private sessions. Their son is a disrespectful lout. We have been here before.

There enters Don Alberto, brother of the guzzling neighbour and his partner in a junk-shop. He seems agreeably whimsical; since he is played by Ralph Richardson, overpoweringly so. He trades jollities about moving the Cimmarutas' furniture around and letting them drop in to move his. (He has, as we discover when the scene shifts, mountains of it.) Suddenly the mood changes. Police swarm on to the stage. Alberto accuses the family of murder. He cannot, however, provide any evidence. Perplexed, he says that he may have dreamed the whole matter.

Naples, we learn from the programme, was full at this time of frivolous accusations. But Alberto's dreams are portent. De Filippo, though mercifully lacking his master's mannerisms, was a disciple of Pirandello; and the Cimmarutas reveal themselves as six characters (and a seventh who has died) who have found an author. They fall in with Alberto's scenario.

In a classic succession of scenes (that is, predictable but thoroughly entertaining) each says in accusation against another. Later they are to combine, to sinister effect, but before that Alberto is to find treachery as frightening on his own doorstep. The play keeps its balance by making him a victim as well as a seer. He is the sanest character in the play, but he gets his facts wrong.

Richardson, of course, is perfect casting. No actor, for a start, can get more out of the vital word 'dream'; none can so delicately kick himself for foolishness that may be wisdom after all; and none can pass so effortlessly from a world of his own to the crispest of everyday comic timing, as demanded by N. F. Simpson's neat translation. He has an old uncle, who, despising of the world, communicates only by spitting and setting off fireworks. (The stage looks wonderful in the green glow of his last Roman candle.) Sir Ralph understands him perfectly, and interprets with the ease of C3PO responding to R2D2.

Mike Ockrent's production radiates authoritatively outwards from its star. There are major performances from Michael Bryant, deceptively harmless, and Robert Stephens, self-tormenting and self-abasing; good work, too, from Marjorie Yates, Glenn Williams and Stephen Hattersley.

Raimonda Gaetani, de Filippo's own designer, supplies a monumental store of bric-a-brac. The author wrote the play, like Alan Ayckbourn, for his own company, and he knocked it off with Ayckbournian speed; the two, despite obvious differences in temperament, have much in common.

'I come no more to make you laugh,' says Richard Griffiths, speaking the prologue to Henry VIII (Stratford), and his subsequent performance in the title role is a triumphant -- though, one hopes, temporary -- repudiation of his clowning past. His Henry is a serious young man working his passage from dependence to despotism. (Benevolent, says the play.)

At first overshadowed by Wolsey and by his queen, but intent on learning, he is erotically aroused by Anne Bullen (as most of the cast remember to pronounce her) and discovers his own strength in wrecking the divorce-court when the cardinals fail to deliver. From there it is an easy step to humiliating Wolsey; and his further progress is only checked by his encounter with Cranmer. 'Poor man, he weeps,' says the King, impressed, not accustomed to emotion in courtiers.

This performance, developed from hints in a distinguished text, is the main prop of Howard Davies's production; though how Mr Davies works out that it shows the transition from absolute to constitutional monarchy beats me. Henry finishes a lot more powerful than he starts. True, he has a council; but he can cow them with an entrance.

Mr Davies's production is the most thoroughgoing Brechtianisation of Shakespeare I have seen (and therefore rather old-fashioned). Ilona Sekacz's music ('How play the musicians?' 'Weill-ly, my lord') underlines or, more frequently, sends woodwindily up. Hayden Griffin's sets, toytown fragments hanging from rails, have belief suspended alongside them. Such patriotic irritants as the coronation procession are disposed of with playful minimal pageantry. Question: if all the ironies assumed by Mr Davies were really present in the text, would he have to labour so hard to bring them to our attention?

The answer is plainly no; but the treatment is generally lively enough to inspire interest if not conviction. Besides, Mr Davies might plead (though I doubt that he would) divided intentions on the part of his authors. This may be the first production to give programme credit to Shakespeare's presumed collaborator, John Fletcher.

An earlier director, Margaret Webster, wrote for example that the character of Buckingham is 'full of salt in his first scene (Shakespeare) and of sugar in his second (Fletcher),' and maybe the production is backing the Bard against his apprentice in accentuating the struggles and parodying the pathos. (David Schofield, previously obeying orders, goes to Buckingham's death with a self-satisfied smirk.)

Katherine never gets off her high horse, and it is fascinating to see how unbendingly regal Gemma Jones can make her. Such apparently harmless supporting acts Sands and the Old Lady (here, less endearingly, a middle-aged lady) are sternly placed at the service of comic purpose, both of them. On the other hand the porter and his men (Roger Hume and Paul Mooney tremulous) are allowed more extensive and affectionate treatment than the christening they precede.

John Thaw's Wolsey, being a prelate persecuted by nobles, lets loose for his lamentations; and John Thaw's authority in the role, like Mr Griffiths's flexibility, returns to front rank the interpretation to the more important matter of technique. They have it; the others are mostly known to be talented but listening to the play, as too often at Stratford these years, is like hearing an opera done entirely by tenors: short-winded ones at that.

I am afraid I still find the script of Woza Albert! (Criterion) flimsy and predictable. But since its parable of Christ returned to South Africa gives plenty of opportunity for acridly humorous depiction of life in the land of the passbook that may not matter. Certainly the performances of Mbongeni Ngema and Percy Mtwa remain dazzling. Their combination of economy, involvement and virtuosity may make them the only performers on the contemporary stage with the right to be called naturalists.

Portrait of the Artist as a Young Dog (King's Head) is a winsome, twitchy and subject to constant infection from an arch piano accompaniment, but Roger Hume's Dylan, who looks like a sulky puzzled Rowan Atkinson, is as young and sexy as D. Thomas said he was.

Compellingly played by the American actress Linda Hunt (a daring and wholly successful piece of casting), Billy is a most engaging creation, and at times powerfully eloquent in a somewhat woody style. But he is forced to bear an impossible dramatic burden. Eventually he reacts to public events in so dramatically unspecific a fashion that we first distrust his intelligence, then cease to believe in him as a character.
