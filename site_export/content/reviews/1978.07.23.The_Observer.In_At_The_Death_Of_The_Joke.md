In at the death of the joke

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

REVUE returned to the London theatre last week though not, I suspect, for long. Two shows were offered, their titles radiating honest hopelessness. At the Bush In at the Death; at Hampstead Beyond a Joke. They can say those again.

The Bush show is a Dying Newspaper. Dusty Hughes, the Diaghilev of Goldhawk Road, enjoined some half-dozen dramatists to bring him songs and sketches inspired by stories in the public prints. Whether he really meant them all to find their inspiration in minor disaster stories, is open to doubt. But that is where they went and what they came back with; and Mr Hughes himself has entered into the spirit of the thing by writing a number called Ghouls about a family who buzzard off, picnic hampers at the ready, at the first tinkle of the passing bell, frequently beating the ambulance men to the scene of the catastrophe.

Setting the evening's tone, Mr Hughes also puts himself, his team and his audience on the spot. We are necrophiles for the night; but why, after all, is it wrong to be young and ghoulish? The evening's fatal lapses are not of taste but of technique. Too much goes on too long; the numbers repeat themselves and one another. Mr Hughes's family group seems largely interchangeable with the one invented by Ron Hutchinson to populate a sketch about How to Survive when living in Belfast. You subject your oul da over the years to multiple amputation, and collect the insurance on each limb. That is not a bad idea (in context, I mean) but Mr Hutchinson hugs it to himself for ages, in the meantime grossly overestimating the comic potential of the phrase "the all-Belfast Ruby Murray look-alike contest." I reckon those words are good for only one laugh; which you have now had.

Of the other writers involved, Ken Campbell is at his most heavy-handed and Snoo Wilson at his least penetrable. Nigel Baldwin contributes the most substantial of the episodes, reiterating the strengths and weaknesses of his full-length work; his dialogue way above average, his themes and characters cowering from the light.

A newcomer, Victoria Wood, is responsible for most of the songs, notably a snappily rhymed lament for Guy the gorilla (the nearest the show gets to lampooning a world figure) and a really haunting, worrying piece called Abortion (the nearest it gets to real feeling). She also appears, early on, as a gleefully malignant schoolboy, an aura she never quite sheds.

Otherwise the actors are as the writers, known talents failing to make an impression: even Clive Merrison, even Alison Fiske. The glowing exception is Julie Walters, who is developing a cherishable line in Fairly Emancipated Women, ready for a giggle but capable of instant recoil when the new limits are overstepped.

* * *

In at the Death suffers from having only one subject; the show at Hampstead, far worse, has none at all. Its participants are making jokes about jokes. They are appealing, that is, to their audience's nostalgia; my own was tickled by the final number which is a cod-opera about brigands. I have never met a brigand but I have met several cod-operas and I like being reminded of them. I have less affection for numbers about pompous young men who take girls to restaurants and are defeated by the French words on the menu, and for men with high-pitched voices who make xenophobic remarks while sitting on benches.

Beyond a Joke has a cast of three, most of it called Rowan Atkinson. Mr Atkinson has eyebrows, curly hair, a lower lip, and timing. These are all good things, but they are not in themselves hilarious; though Michael Rudman, the indulgent director, looks to believe that they are. Mr Atkinson is not a natural charmer; he needs material, and only once does he get it, as a schoolmaster contemptuously informing his class of the number of jokes to be found in Shakespeare.

The other performers are Elspeth Walker, a Valkyrie, and Peter Wilson, a straight man. There are two musicians, one of whom sings two inaudible songs. The influence of television is apparent in the lack of punchlines; unfortunately there are no electronic tricks to compensate. The show wants to please, which is nice of it, and is totally uncommitted, indeed unaware; which should make the post-68 playwrights who talk so proudly of "our generation" realise how old they, we, are getting. There's a new lot coming up and they don't care. This collection emerged, recently, from Oxford; it is not true that university humour has stood still for 10 years. It has gone round in a circle, and is tired.

* * *

In Cambridge, things are brighter, not to say older; the Arts company having revived Thornton Wilder's The Matchmaker (1955) which afterwards became Hello Dolly and had already been at least four other plays. Wilder's eclectic dramatic reading may account for the smallness of his output; not only must it have taken up his time, he may have found the weight of tradition inhibiting.

In this case he blends farce with moral comedy, and though it would be false to claim he gets the best of both worlds, he blended them attractively.

For his setting he chose elitist New York, thereby guaranteeing, as far as I am concerned, instant enchantment: enhanced in Jonathan Lynn's production by judicious musical interludes and in Saul Radomsky's designs by a captivating abundance of stripes.

The play, carefully filled with stock characters (healthy stock, of course), should be cast up to the nines; some of the small parts here are at sixes and sevens. But most positions are well held: Bernard Spear, Leueen Willoughby, Angus MacInnes, Tom Kleh, Richard Simpson all lift the spirits and Vivienne Martin, playing slyly and confidently along in the first three acts, emerges in the fourth as a dazzling Dolly. The play arrives with all the credentials of a classic: the production proves them to be fully in order.
