Aboard the good ship Snoo

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN finds excitement at the Court.

THEY are raising spirits at the Royal Court; mine among them.

Snoo Wilson's The Glad Hand should do more to restore this theatre's reputation than any play in years. It concerns a man who tries to summon up the Antichrist; and the one thing that can be predicted of such a quest is that it will always succeed. If you believe in monsters or in scapegoats you will find them.

Mr Wilson's snark-hunter is a South African capitalist called Ritsaat, who credits the Antichrist with the dissemination of Marxism. To root out him and it he charters a ship; fills it up with actors, a playwright, a CIA man, an electronics freak, and a family of villainous Irish domestics; and sets off in the direction of Cuba.

I'm sorry, this isn't making sense. But the play does, perfectly, so I'll try again. The thing is, Ritsaat believes that the Antichrist was last sighted in 1885 during a cowboys' strike (this -- the strike, I mean -- really happened) in Wyoming. He's not going there, of course, directly. But he hopes, within the propitious geometric confines of the Bermuda Triangle, to perform a play about the strike whose vibes will be so powerful as to propel him and his quarry towards each other.

Sceptics like me have sometimes fallen foul of Mr Wilson's concern with the occult; here he makes it easy for us. Like Tom Stoppard he sets up impossible situations and explains them; and his wit in this piece has a Stoppardian exhilaration. Then, again, if Ritsaat is an exploiter he is also exploited; his psychic surgeon is a self-confessed charlatan. We expect, maybe dread, a magical explosion; it is deferred and deferred until we mentally discount it; finally, with the full weight of the play behind it, it explodes, bringing what have seemed random or playful elements into new alignment.

Meanwhile we have been entertained with brilliant variations on the all-in-the-same-boat theme; and, in the second act, with the promised play-within-the-play. Much of this is parody of the didactic strike-breaking dramas, all short scenes and starvation, usually served up in community theatres. The sheriff, traditionally moustached, is the reactionary villain ('cowboys don't belong in unions, cowboys belong in bars'), but his opponent's speech is genuinely stirring stuff -- enhanced, not nullified, by the complexity that surrounds it. (Its speaker is, in the outside play, distinctly unsavoury.)

Another drama of labour unrest takes place on board, directed against Ritsaat himself, who must be the most disarming dictator-figure in modern drama. Antony Sher, who is shaping up into a truly remarkable actor, plays him crew-cut, trim-bearded, humourless, and implacably eager.

The last time I felt this excitement at the Court was with Howard Brenton's Magnificence, directed, like this play, by Max Stafford-Clark. The production here has some of his Joint Stock characteristics; off-centre but still precise, it is in its way a social panorama. (An American girl, sort of passing through, defines the English disease as 'standing around leaning on unsprayed car doors,' which is really all that needs to be said, though some writers say it at greater length.) The cast -- Tony Rohr, Julie Waters and Will Knightley are among them -- leap on the play, and win the freest laughter I have offered any play in ages.

* * *

Additional proof is provided by Per Olov Enquist's The Tribades (Hampstead), which shows us Strindberg hysterically asserting his own masculinity in the face of an affair between two actresses, one of them his wife. Peter Woodthorpe and Susan Hampshire make -- in the Strindbergian sense -- an admirable married couple; that is to say they bitch beautifully. But of course Strindberg's miseries have already been exhaustively dramatised by Strindberg and this piece is pretty heavy for a footnote. I suppose, and dread, that young American playwrights are already hard at work on O'Neill.

* * *

The Club (Regent) is the latest girlie show; seven ladies portray the inhabitants (five members, two staff) of a male enclave in fin-de-siecle New York. They sing ballads glorifying women and tell bar-room jokes reviling them; the point is accurately made that these are opposite sides of the same limited attitude. That is established in about 10 minutes; afterwards everything, as in most musicals, depends on performance level.

In the successful off-Broadway production, staged with great spirit and resource along a projecting ramp, this was very high. In Tony Tanner's London version, performed on a handsome but constricting set, the cast don't seem to know where to put themselves. They have to (a) sing and dance (b) look male (c) sound American. The only performer to succeed in all categories is Linda Thorson, who should take the town as a man about it. The material would sound far sourer if performed by men; I am grateful that the author, Eve Merriam, tried for something more delicate, but not even androgyny is a guaranteed source of theatrical interest.

* * *

Sandy Wilson's The Clapham Wonder (Marlowe, Canterbury) is about levitation: an intractable subject, even for a musical, and surrounded by domestic misery to which Mr Wilson's polite, jolly style is totally unsuited. Pip Simmons's adaptation of The Tempest (Riverside Studios) has more unity than some straight versions, largely owing to its sandy set. Most Shakespearean cut-ups end up as parodies, and this one is at least good-humoured.
