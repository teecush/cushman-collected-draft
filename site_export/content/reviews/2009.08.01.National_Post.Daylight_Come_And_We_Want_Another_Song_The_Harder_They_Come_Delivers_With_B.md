Based on a 1972 movie that the program calls "one of the first cult films" (though there've been cults almost as long as there've been movies), The Harder They Come is an enlivening musical with some boring bits. Structurally, it ain't nothin' but a house party, which puts it in the tradition of such other shows from the Theatre Royal, Stratford East -- East London, that is --as Five Guys Named Moe, the Louis Jordan anthology. This show is a Jimmy Cliff anthology, with the same good-time feel, but also with a lot more plot and a darker overlay. The elements don't always fuse.

The party here is a wake, held in memory of its hero, Ivan, whose story is told to us in, you guessed, flashback. Ivan, the role played by Cliff in the movie, is accordingly a reggae singer, who comes to town, gets work with a preacher, joins his choir and attracts the attention of the listening world, in general, and the preacher's ward, in particular. He elopes with her, on the preacher's bike, and auditions for a record producer, who represents the sleazy side of the music business; the show doubts there's any other. He strikes out on his own, incurring a lot of hardship. Asked to name his record label, he lights on "Rhygin," meaning "wild," which was the nickname of Ivanhoe Martin, a popular outlaw with whom our Ivan identifies and with whom the show identifies him. This historical Ivan was killed in a firefight with the police in 1948; our fictitious one operates in, presumably, the '70s. Superimposing one story on the other doesn't work; by all accounts (well, by Pauline Kael's account), it didn't in the film either. The show tries to annex the old Ivan's Robin Hood reputation, but it's rather a long road to travel from being swindled by a record company to shooting sprees with cops. Perry Henzell, adapting his own film script, tries to bridge the gap by showing the record mogul in league with, if not in control of, the corrupt police, who in turn are hand-in-glove with the marijuana trade at which Ivan, in desperate need of bread, also tries his hand. But it feels forced, and confusion overtakes the long stretches of the second act that pass between numbers. The heavy Jamaican patois helps make these incomprehensible for the uninitiated.

Though, it also helps the overall feeling of authenticity. In its grafting of pre-existing pop songs onto a story-line, the show resembles Mamma Mia, except that its songs have backbone and its story relates to something real. These songs belong to these people, even though their dramatic relevance is generally confined to their titles, with the title number becoming a metaphor for getting up and fighting. The best musical-dramatic sequence resurrects dear old Day-O, the Banana Boat Song, performed on the docks with the bananas a sly metaphor for weed. The villainous police chief sings Pressure Point many times as cue for a bit of audience participation: standard in British shows of this kind, but reducing his menace potential.

Directed by Dawn Reid and Kerry Michael, the show feels laid-back and uncluttered, helped by a cool and simple set from the great British designer Ultz and lighting by Jo Joelson in highly atmospheric shades of red. Jackie Guy's choreography uses simple formations to intoxicating effect, especially when Rolan Bell's Ivan, with his elastic legs and sunshine personality, is at the centre of them. The best individual singing comes from Lain Gray as his buddy and protector. The long reprise finale is a blast, and an indication of where the show's heart, and certainly its soul, really reside.

Another show with some songs (old-fashioned but nice) is The Menaechmus Twins, one of whose characters, a Roman slave, at one point makes as if about to sing about being free but then stops himself, as if he doesn't want to go into competition with Stephen Sondheim. This makes sense, as this play is by Plautus, whose ancient Roman comedies inspired A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum, though it's one that Forum didn't use. It did, though, inspire Shakespeare, whose Comedy of Errors added some sentiment and an extra pair of lookalikes. You may wonder if he really improved it much, since this open-air production is a total delight in its own right. Plautus at least explains why his long-separated twins should have the same first name, which is more than Shakespeare did. He also provided a tidier expository prologue, which the uncredited translation (in which someone "fills a fine freighter full of ... freight") gleefully exploits; as does Melee Hutton's production, brazenly presenting us with supposedly identical twins who are, at most, of about the same height. Wayne Ward gives a wonderfully self-satisfied account of the dark-haired married one, who finds himself excluded ( "I'm the most shuttest-out man alive") by wife and mistress, both of them played, as in some Freudian nightmare and deliciously, by the same actress, Sarah Evans. His fair-haired bachelor sibling is Ryan Hollyman, who plays on one flustered note but holds it well. Adrian Proszowski as one brother's servant and Jeffrey R. Smith as the other's parasite are towers of supporting strength. There is a basic two-door set, neatly used, and no need of lighting, since the show starts at 6 p. m. and is over at 7:20. It's early, short, hilarious and you pay what you can. The best theatrical deal in town. - The Harder They Come runs through Aug. 23 at the Canon Theatre.
