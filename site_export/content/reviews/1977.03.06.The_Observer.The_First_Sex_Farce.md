The first sex-farce

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

IF ALL the young acting talent working in this country were laid end to end, in A Thought in Three Parts (ICA) some of it is. It gets laid in many other positions as well. Wallace Shawn's play, as you would gather from its title, is divided into three; the second third, called The Youth Hostel, is, as far as action goes, mostly orgy, graphically staged and energetically performed.

After a short time you stop gaping at the array of pretty bodies, tempting spread, and begin to laugh. This, however, is a reaction that Mr Shawn has not merely anticipated but encouraged. Youth Hostel is a play about sex without involvement. Its characters behave like demented automata. It is, classically, a farce; maybe the first real sex-farce to reach the British stage.

Four young Americans are mainly involved. Bob, who is nervous and seems repressed, asks Helen to take her clothes off, just so that he can have a look. She, apparently for the sake of a quiet life, complies, and next moment he is on top of her. Meanwhile, in the room next door, aggressive Judy comes upon solid Dick ("I'm sorry about that") and absolutely wrecks the quiet afternoon he had planned for himself. Before long, all four are together, and ceaselessly at it.

"Tired's not the word," groans Dick, understandably, after much activity, and adds engagingly, "I need such a rest, lots of swimming." But all that actually lies in store for him is more of the same, varied by some strenuous competitive masturbation. Finally, all passion presumably spent (though "passion" here is not quite the right word), they disperse to their respective quarters, Helen is joined in hers by Tom who is apparently her husband, and who, after a crisp introduction ("smells like sperm in here, darling, have you had visitors?") proceeds to beat her up. That's love.

I doubt, whatever breakfast foods may have done for the stamina of young America, that Mr Shawn means his playlet to be taken absolutely literally. It is a fantasia on the idea of sex as a trek towards a constantly receding nirvana of orgasm, yielding some fun en route, but ultimately frustrating. He is in fact using the audacious means to point a traditional moral: turning permissiveness against itself, you might say, though if all that bouncing flesh begins to look comical, that is maybe less a comment on sex than on voyeurism.

The two enclosing plays, however, darken the vision. The closer, a monologue, presents the morning fantasies of a businessman and they do not seem satisfactory. At least, I didn't get much out of them; neither did the rest of the mystified audience, and the actor seemed a touch baffled as well. In the opener a holidaying couple -- probably married and, the most sinister thing, certifiably in love -- reveal their relationship to consist of complementary solitudes.

Mr Shawn performs the same trick here with words as he does in the second play with actions; he uses stylised rhythms to create a recognisable reality. Love-making at the end of the first piece leads naturally into the second; and beneath all three violence simmers. The author's cynicism may be self-willed (a line in the programme "for the past five years Shawn has lived happily on 16th St. in Manhattan with a Miss D.E." suggests that he is holding something back, and should in all charity share his secret with us), but he tells no outright lies and many truths. It is easy to see why his work attracted the attention of Joint Stock, an ascetic group whose previous work has shown a curt disregard for worldly pleasures. Max Stafford-Clark's production is in their best traditions, with particularly good work by Jack Klaff, Stephanie Fayerman and the versatile Robyn Goodman.

Mr Shawn's viewpoint received unexpected confirmation two nights later at the Mermaid. Terence Frisby's It's All Right If I Do It (emphasis on the "I") is concerned with the double standard. Husband and wife look back on their respective infidelities, compare totals, become explosively disgruntled, finally settle for disenchantment. I found Mr Frisby's play far more depressing than Mr Shawn's, partly because its hopelessness (as the pronouncement of experience rather than innocence) must be taken more seriously; but mainly because someone has been at pains to impose on it the veneer of West End comedy. A passage of recrimination at the beginning of the second act is authentically painful; otherwise, every time the play veers towards truthfulness, it is thrown off-course by a mechanical gag. Ayckbourn, Nichols, Stoppard, Mike Stott in Funny Peculiar, see the world comically (joyously or bitterly, it makes for the moment no odds). Mr Frisby simply applies jokes. (So does Joint Stock's Devil's Island; it is not simply a commercial disease.) Prunella Scales, a fine actress, is ferocious when she gets the chance, but her familiarity from TV domestic comedy makes her casting seem part of the strategy. John Stride, another disarmer, builds up a good thunderous roar at climactic moments.

* * *

To take a moral stand over Vaclav Havel, the imprisoned Czech writer, is so easy and so inevitable that the excellence of his short plays Audience and Private View (Orange Tree, Richmond) seems almost suspect. But it has to be said; he makes most, not all, of our political plays look shabby, not so much because our playwrights lack his advantages (e.g., jail) as for his ability to see a situation, humanely and ironically, in the round.

Thus, in the first play -- which is considerably the better -- the dissident writer sent to work in a brewery, though intolerably treated, is no mere martyr; he feels but cannot stop himself being patronising to the drunken Head Maltster, who offers him protection if he will -- a mere formality -- inform on himself. When the Maltster, having imbibed gallons of his own product, explodes with the accusation that even persecuted intellectuals do all right in the end, while he himself has no hope, the situation is very uncomfortable. Gordon Reid plays unwilling pin to Bill Wallis's overbearing balloon; and comedy lives.

* * *

For reasons I do not understand, but which probably have to do with the balance of payments, visual spectacle, condemned in native productions, is always applauded in visiting companies. Peter Stein's production of Summerfolk (brought to the Lyttelton by the Schaubuhne am Halleschen Ufer of West Berlin) looks magnificent: not just its expansively wrapped sets, its lighting, which gets the chill of sundown into your bones, but its sense of a life being lived.

A whole picnic takes place on one side of the stage while non-participants agonise and philosophise on the other. At the end of the scene the doomed bourgeoisie seem about to advance on the audience, but they are swallowed up in darkness first: a master-stroke. One actress, Edith Clever, cracks quietly and memorably up amid the throng.
