I saw Sybil Thorndike only once on the stage: as the Nurse in the famous Chichester 'Uncle Vanya,' in which she was magnificent. I never met her. All the same, I feel this week as though I have suffered a personal loss.

The Family Dance (Criterion), by a new dramatist, Felicity Browne, is about a celebration-hop in the Berkshire home of a stockbroker called Ben Musgrave. Musgraves and dancing seem to go together in the theatre, though I doubt if John Arden's stony serjeant would relish the idea of a stockbroker for a descendant. At that, Ben is the only one of the new Musgraves to do any work at all. His brother, his sister, and their respective spouses live off his earnings in what he describes as 'a family commune.'

There are also, of course, children; it is they and their friends who are actually having the ball, though they are at it offstage; we are in the kitchen (a large one, of course) watching their elders collapse from underwork. It takes them in different ways: sister Diana has become the kind of hearty tyrant who, if our playwrights are to be trusted, may be found beneath every roof in the Home Counties, brother Toby has taken spinelessly to drink, and Ben's wife Sophie marks the evening's half-way point by gulping down an overdose of sleeping-pills.

Before taking this step, Sophie has asked Ben for a divorce, only to receive the reply--a witty one, I thought--that having been married for 17 years she might as well stick it out to the end. Miss Browne displays a great talent for the stylish display of selfishness; it is obvious that Ben has assumed the role of universal breadwinner largely in order to put the family in his debt. In Alec McCowen's bravura performance he careers around the stage looking for grievances, upbraiding his brother for a weakling, and his wife for a bad boiler of eggs and an even worse lay.

These bad manners are diverting, but they are not remotely believable. Or rather, they are only remotely believable: fine in long-shot, but not in close-up. Regarded as a verbal ballet of family discord, the play is very entertaining. Miss Browne has an eye as well as an ear and by putting Sophie, played waifishly by Annette Crosbie, into a biscuit-coloured dress ('the same colour as the rest of you,' says her sister-in-law, possibly meaning well) she isolates her effectively.

But she cannot get inside Sophie's character; cannot explain why she has stuck with Ben so long, or why, having done so, she should want to leave now. Indeed, despite the efforts of Miss Crosbie, Michael Bryant and Judy Parfitt (whose main contribution to the proceedings is to look handsome in green), the more downtrodden members of the menage never come to life.

The monsters, so glitteringly played by Mr McCowen and Helen Lindsay, overpower them. If their performances sometimes seem a bit relentless, the writing gives them little option. Nobody should accuse Miss Browne of glamourising family life or even Berkshire; when sentimentality breaks in, it is the character's, not the author's. But once we have agreed with her that the situation she presents is indeed unpleasant, there is little left for us to do. It is a watchable play, but not an energising one.

* * *

Anyway, there is hope for Miss Browne, as once there was, I suppose, for John Mortimer. However, Mr Mortimer is now one of the strangest phenomena in the English theatre: a brand-name dramatist for nearly 20 years, with only one play to his credit ('A Voyage Round My Father') that was even half-way good. There have also been some tolerable sketches: one of them is the second half of the double-bill Heaven and Hell (Greenwich). 'Hell's funny,' as we used to say in the sixties; heaven though, as tenuously dramatised in the curtain-raiser, is a drag and, as usual, difficult to believe in. The evening does offer Denholm Elliott, Peter Woodthorpe and Eleanor Bron; and, of course, many evenings offer less.

* * *

The Winter's Tale (Stratford) seemed promising, directed by John Barton 'with Trevor Nunn.' Mr Nunn staged this play excellently a few years ago and this season's 'Much Ado About Nothing' showed Mr Barton at the top of his form. But with the permanent Elizabethan set (of which the RSC seem already to have repented) masked in with furs and hieroglyphs, and the actors furry as well, this is dehydrated Shakespeare, the earnest tanning of a monster play.

Talking of monsters, this is the play in which a character exits, 'pursued by a bear'; at Stratford he merely finds himself confronted by what looks like a walking totem-pole, which escorts him offstage with the apparent intention of taking his name and address. Not surprisingly a subsequent speech describing his dismemberment by the bear is neither credible nor funny. (Funniness may be assumed to have been among the author's intentions, since he entrusted the speech to a character called the Clown. Another RSC director, Terry Hands, has recently declared in print that Shakespeare never means what he says. This is as simplistic as claiming that he always does.)

Throughout the evening the cast seemed engaged in ritual games which they probably understand, but which leave the audience in the cold. There are good performances, of course: Barbara Leigh-Hunt a formidably nannyish Paulina, David Waller's warming first moments as the Shepherd, Marilyn Taylerson as statuesque a Hermione as could be imagined, and even Florizel and Perdita no worse than usual. Above all, Ian McKellen's Leontes is splendid; the actor's technical control (which in Shakespeare has never been more evident) throws into relief the character's disarray. As Leontes's lucidity goes, Mr McKellen's increases, the course of each shaft of jealousy precisely plotted. Particularly revealing is his clinging to his son, counterbalancing his revulsion from his wife.

* * *

Robert Stephens in Othello (Regent's Park) is similarly exact, though his jealous transports are messier. I always forget just how powerful an actor Mr Stephens is, but there are speeches here whose weight he cannot shift. But I should like to see him try again, under a roof (since my Elizabethanism is by no means fanatical) and with an Iago who has a finer sense of rhythm than Edward Fox.

* * *

Liza of Lambeth (Shaftesbury) is the latest overweight British musical based on an Edwardian novel: Maugham this time. It has one good idea (the substitution of short comic monologues for full-blown songs) and seems to despise its own insistence on Cockney jollity (big number: 'Going Down to Chingford on a Chara.'); it compensates with overpowering slum sets, and must be the dingiest musical ever. The authors offer us no reason to be interested in their heroine, and Angela Richards, poor girl, cannot make the deficiency good.

* * *

The rock 'n' roll musical Leave Him to Heaven (New London) is also divided; its starting-point is a Stan Freberg musical parody, but while Freberg hated rock, the authors here do not. How much was intended as a mockery and how much as straight nostalgia I cannot guess, but there is no doubt that nostalgia wins. Even a song as awful as the necrophile 'Tell Laura I Love Her' has grown charm with the decades. Brian Protheroe has awesome energy as the teenage idol and his rendition of 'Chantilly Lace,' the era's very best song, is irresistible.
