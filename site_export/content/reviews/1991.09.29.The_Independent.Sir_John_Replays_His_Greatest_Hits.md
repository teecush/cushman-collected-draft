Sir John replays his greatest hits

SHAKESPEARE: HIT OR MISS?
by John Gielgud with John Miller
Sidgwick £17.50

JOHN GIELGUD and Shakespeare have not kept company much in recent years. Although a compulsive theatre-goer since the late 1950s, I have seen Gielgud in only three Shakespeare roles. Two of them, Wolsey and Julius Caesar, he was essaying for the first time and neither ranks among his greatest hits. The third was Prospero, which he had already played twice and which I saw him do twice more. He also had behind him multiple assaults on Lear and Hamlet, and was reckoned supreme in both parts - as he was in Richard II, Angelo, Benedick, Cassius and Leontes.

All of which suggests that Shakespeare is a young man's game. (Or, increasingly these days, a young woman's game: we breed far more Rosalinds than Hamlets.) Sir John, in this book of reminiscence, seems inclined to agree, constantly protesting how out of touch he must seem, how long ago his triumphs were, and how seldom he goes to the theatre.

Actually this most effortlessly modern of actors seems to have kept up with all the best things, from Peter Brook's Dream to Richard Briers's Malvolio, and he is discerningly enthusiastic about them. Even at his most apparently reactionary he talks sense, as when he doubts that young audiences are much beguiled by seeing the plays done out of period.

This is a light, anecdotal book, pleasing because you can hear the Gielgud voice in it but frustrating because he says so little about the characters he has played. He checks them all off, unchronologically, but almost the only specific comment he makes is that Richard II "has no humour". This is startling, given the actor's own comic genius, but it does reveal what the subsequent line of waspishly witty Richards have been reacting against. His editor/collaborator should have pressured him for more.

Far more instructive, and almost justifying the graceless title, is his constant emphasis on the theatre's fallibility: on the things that work one night and not another, and on the actor's quixotic insecurities ("I was afraid," he says of an early success, "of becoming a bit too pleased with myself"). He combines specific modesty with an overriding assurance of his own place in history. "Audiences," he says "often credit you with far more intelligence than you actually possess."

Critics, I am sure, will often take a hint from a performance and proceed to father on the actor their own fantasies about the play; several set-pieces of criticism are reproduced here. There are also some invaluable notes on Lear from Granville-Barker, who once directed Gielgud in the role, and whom he reveres as he does Brook. This book could use a director.
