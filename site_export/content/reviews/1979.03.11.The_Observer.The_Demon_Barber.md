The Demon Barber

Theatre

Robert Cushman on Sondheim's 'Sweeney Todd' in New York--and the Louisville festival.

I WROTE at the end of last year about a Broadway musical called 'A Broadway Musical' and wondered whether it would get past the New York critics.

It didn't; it died at birth. A pity, for though horribly confused, it was still appealing; it both dramatised and exemplified the lunacies of putting on a show in the face of murderous economics, artistic bewilderment and the theatrical withering of the popular song. It would have been pleasantly symbolic to have it around, welcoming subsequent arrivals with a deranged grin on its face.

For still they come. The blockbusters continue to bust blocks: 'Annie' and 'Ain't Misbehavin'' and grand-daddy 'Grease.' All in a row on 44th Street stand the choreographic musicals, 'Ballroom' and 'Dancin'' and 'A Chorus Line,' almost making a sentence. Then there is 'The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas,' a success and possibly the worst little musical in the world. It has country music and is stupid.

Of 1979's early comers, 'The Grand Tour' starring Joel Grey as a Jew on the run through occupied Europe, died young, possibly because its narrative techniques were old-fashioned, possibly because the world was not waiting for the musical-comedy version of 'Holocaust,' possibly because the authors (the 'Hello Dolly' team of Michael Stewart and Jerry Herman) hadn't realised that that was what they were getting into. 'They're Playing Our Song' looks like surviving, it being this season's Neil Simon two-hander embellished by smart production and negligible songs; its first half is bright, its second--in which the audience is invited to take an interest in a relationship it hardly knew existed--discouraging.

The palm would have to go to 'Whoopee,' a cheerful exhumation (the best of its kind I've seen) from 1928, were it not for the advent of a show differing in everything but expertise: the Stephen Sondheim-Harold Prince 'Sweeney Todd.'

This, too, might count as an exhumation (it is full of graveyard imagery and there are a couple of diggers on stage at the start to set the mood), since it brings to Broadway the fabled Demon Barber of Fleet Street. The source of the musical is a version by the English playwright Christopher Bond staged at Stratford East in 1973. Mr Bond took the central penny-gaff situation--the cut-throat artist practising above a pie-shop and supplying it with cheap and easy meat--and gave it with a beginning out of 'The Count of Monte-Cristo' and an end out of Elizabethan tragedy.

His Todd is an escaped convict, sentenced to transportation on a trumped-up charge so that the judge could have unimpeded access to Mrs Todd. Our barber returns years later to find his wife a suicide and his daughter, now adolescent, the judge's ward. He plans revenge.

The convict's return is a familiar Dickensian motif, and this 'Sweeney Todd' has more of the spirit of Dickens--the nightmare spirit of 'Oliver Twist'--than any show based on the novel. It presents Victorian London as a plague-spot. Eugene Lee's set, which just about takes over the vast and antiseptic Uris Theatre, is a combination of chapel (with organ), street and--overwhelmingly--factory; and a factory whistle sounds unnervingly at each curve of the plot.

The veneer of social criticism, though, is less important than the passion beneath. Len Cariou's death's-head Sweeney is a magnificent study of a monomaniac--and, in its bitter frantic clarity, the best rendition of a Sondheim score I have heard. It is a musical frenzy that carries Sweeney across the bridge from specific to general bloodlust, but the process is prefigured when he first appears, singing in a low, terse staccato: 'There's a place in the world like a great big pit/And the vermin of the world inhabit it/And it goes by the name of London.'

There is more to the score, and the show, than this: a laconic humour ('So what if none of their souls were saved/They went to their maker impeccably shaved') which expands to epic proportions with Angela Lansbury's pie-woman, looking with her hair piled high in unruly braids, like a monstrous tabby-cat. She and Todd make a marvellously complementary pair of villains: she jolly and practical, he the visionary and thwarted romantic. The tenderest strains of the evening sound around the barber's chair, as he regards his instruments ('my friends') or prepares to use them. This goes beyond grotesquerie or easy irony to a feeling for the criminal as outcast that is absolutely true to the underside of Victorian literature.

More conventional romance comes off less well: Todd's daughter and her sailor-lover are indeterminate figures. They can afford to be, in Bond's play (which reads as elegant, funny and gripping pastiche), but not amid the far greater density of this musical. I should add that Bond's plotting is excellent and the final retributive section of the show--in dialogue, song and Harold Prince's staging--stunning. There is no choreography, but there is consummate choral work. (The show is at least three-quarters sung.) I am sure there is a large, mainly young, audience for this show: the problem is getting them to Broadway. London must see it; it is as near as anyone has come to writing the great British musical.

It is a good season for Victorian misfits. The play of the moment is Bernard Pomerance's 'The Elephant Man' (seen last year at Hampstead) with its caustic use of a painfully intelligent freak as a moral measuring-rod for the society around him. A pellucid production is about to transfer from off-Broadway.

The other touted play is Arthur Kopit's 'Wings,' which begins as an interesting attempt to represent linguistically the dislocation caused by a stroke but falls apart when the author gets down to cases. (Just like Neil Simon.)

Recent American drama is a desert of promise; what is lacking is mature performance. Off-Broadway now has to compete for its white hopes with the increasingly prosperous regional theatre movement. In Louisville, Kentucky (home of Muhammad Ali and Colonel Sanders), the Actors Playhouse last week concluded its third festival of new plays. The theatre, housed in an old bank building, boasts two stages and, for the festival, a very good company. They have also found (and launched as far as New York) a local playwright, Marsha Norman, though her contribution this year was unimpressive: a play about a circus that just looked as though someone had sat down and doggedly written a play about a circus.

Slightly better were a double bill of musicals based on one-act Shaw plays (though performance style proved a problem here) and a programme of playlets entitled 'Holidays' contributed by 10 prominent dramatists, several of whom appeared to have dug out old scripts and written in a few lines about the Fourth of July or whatever.

A new playwright, Beth Henley, offered us 'Crimes of the Heart'; I never quite overcame my disappointment at finding that her piece, about three Mississippi sisters with problems, was not after all going to be a gothic comedy, since it started out that way. Miss Henley went some way towards reclaiming the domestic hearth (or, this being a kitchen play, stove) for respectable drama, but the antique associations proved overpowering.

The find of the festival was James McLure, whose two plays, 'Lone Star' and 'Pvt. Wars,' turned out to be exhilaratingly funny on old subjects, like growing up in Texas, and comparatively new ones, like looking back on war-service in Vietnam; this last hitherto a taboo subject, for laughter. Mr McLure writes cross-talk that builds; he could become a major comic writer, which would be fine, or relax into being a gag man, which wouldn't be so bad either.
