Sandy's 'Aladdin'

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Sandy Wilson's new Christmas show.

SANDY WILSON'S Aladdin is not a pantomime.

It might have been; when the new Lyric Theatre, Hammersmith, asked him to provide a Christmas show for them he dutifully looked up some panto scripts. But though he liked the traditional Victorian ones, he saw no point in competing with the Players' Theatre, which has made them an annual speciality, and the modern versions he found awful: 'Jokes about television interrupted by pop songs.' So he went back to the Arabian Nights.

Not having any children himself, he consulted relatives who did, and discovered what cannot have come as a great surprise, that children do not particularly like shows specially written for them. So the author-composer of The Boy Friend and Valmouth set about writing a musical like any other, but far more difficult than most. Difficult because 'everyone knows the story so well' and also because it requires a great number of numbers to tell it at all. 'Eighteen songs - the biggest score I've ever written - plus incidental music.'

Anyway, there it will be at Hammersmith (previews from Friday); an authoritative unpantomimed Aladdin, complete with Genies of Lamp and Ring, wicked Abanazar, simple hero, that least pronounceable of heroines, Princess Badroulbadour, and Aladdin's mother, known since pantomime immemorial as Widow Twankey, but now, as a concession to Chinese realism, christened Twankey-chung. She is played by a man, Joe Melia, an experienced song-and-dance artiste. There is also a wise woman named Fatima, who foretells what's going to happen to Aladdin. Wilson wrote her a number in the first act; then Elizabeth Welsh was cast in the part, so he wrote her one in the second as well.

Wilson was an obvious choice as creator of the first musical entertainment to be seen on the main stage of the new Lyric. His connections with the old Lyric go back a long way. In 1948, soon after coming down from Oxford (where he was part of the Tynan generation, an era copiously recalled in his autobiography, I Could Be Happy) he contributed numbers to a Lyric revue called Oranges and Lemons.

He supported himself as a revue-writer until the production in 1954 (at the Players') of The Boy Friend, the definitive spoof of the twenties musical. Indeed it is one case where a parody has superseded the original. Nobody thinks of reviving The Girl Friend, the Rodgers and Hart show from which Wilson borrowed a title and some elements of plot, or indeed any other of the indistinguishable shows about love in the playgrounds of the rich that were contemporary with it. But The Boy Friend seems always to be in production somewhere.

'It is astonishing that it continues to do so well,' says Wilson, who adds that it's marvellous that he can 'still live the way I like to live' (with comfort, but no particular extravagance) on its proceeds. For a time it also provided him with a second and much-relished career as a director. His own productions of the show extend from a version in Australia to a full-scale London revival in 1967 which proved the show to be still, in its own peculiar kind, a masterpiece.

Wilson's second show, The Buccaneer, was his only attempt at what was then a typical subject (the fifties rash of children's horror-comics) and is accordingly the only one of his shows to have dated. It is also one of his two previous musicals to have premiered in Hammersmith; the other was the much-remembered Valmouth, based on Firbank. It came along in 1958, and demands for its revival have gone on unceasingly since about 1959.

Wilson would like that himself - it's his own favourite among his shows - but he is realistic about the chances. It is, to begin with, an unusually difficult show to cast, requiring more than a dozen stylishly unlikely vocalising hags. Michael Codron cast it marvellously in 1958; Cleo Laine and Fenella Fielding were among the newish faces in the company, and the vivacious Big Best Shoes is still in the Laine repertoire.

More crucial is the question of expense. It is no longer economic to present Wilson's shows in the medium-sized theatres which housed them in the fifties. And in Drury Lane or Her Majesty's they - like all the better British musicals - would be lost.

Despite the discouragements, Wilson has gone on writing book, music and lyrics, a triple proficiency that puts him in the Noel Coward class. (To find an American equivalent you would have to go back to George M. Cohan in the early years of the century.) One show, Divorce Me Darling, which in the sixties took The Boy Friend characters forward to the thirties, was he feels 'a good show ahead of its time.' Then at the beginning of the seventies there was His Monkey Wife, successful at Hampstead and bought by a West End producer who could not, however, raise the money for it. (It makes you wonder when you see some of the things they do raise money for.) More recently there was The Clapham Wonder, a surprisingly sombre piece tried out at Canterbury. Next year there will probably be another show at another regional theatre.

Stirrings in small places suggest that, artistically if not economically, there may be a place again for a British musical that is not just a jaded showbiz juggernaut. This has not really seemed possible since the fifties. It was Wilson who gave us hope then, and it would be fitting if he - like his contemporaries Monty Norman and Julian More, successful with Songbook after a theatrical silence of nearly 20 years - were able again to join the parade.
