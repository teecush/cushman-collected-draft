The Strange Case of Martin Richter

Every so often I get lucky. I don't suppose that I would have got out to Hampstead to see *The Strange Case of Martin Richter* had *Plays and Players* not pushed me into it. To you who were not pushed and so missed it, a treat was denied: the best new play (in itself, admittedly, no great praise) that I have had to review in the last two years.

Martin Richter's case is of a strangeness analogous to that of Jekyll and Hyde. He is a man sleek and civilised enough to be the perfect butler (which is what, in private life, he is) who, for his own purposes, assumes the mark of the beast. Unlike Jekyll's, though, his transformation involves no loss of control; it brings him in fact as near control as he can hope to come. In the name of an organisation known as the National Health Party he leads the domestics in a revolt against the master of the house, whom he proceeds to imprison in a cage, as Tamburlaine did Bajazeth. (I hope you're counting the literary echoes; they're important.) The scene is presumed to be Germany; at any rate, the characters all talk like stock Teutons. The only exceptions are the deposed sovereign (actually some kind of industrialist) and, in his butlering moments, Richter himself. This use of a frankly stagey accent to denote an assumed persona—a persona with which other characters in the play are stuck for real—has, incredibly, brought down on Stanley Eveling's head the accusation that he is dealing, in all seriousness, in racial stereotypes. Of course Brecht sincerely believed that all Caucasian landowners wore masks.

Richter has his own motives for involving himself with the National Health: partly fear (they outnumber him in the household) but more especially revenge. Unknown to them, he is a Swabian. For National Health of course we read National Socialist, and for Swabian Jew. In Eveling's piece the anti-Semitism is real and only the name has been changed to protect the play's comic identity, which could certainly not survive the intolerable emotive effect of the real words. As things are, it survives triumphant, recalling an early and superior Frisch play, *The Fire Raisers*, lingering in my memory as a blaze of candles on an ill-omened festive table. Martin Richter repeats the image and enriches it; a boar's head glowers out at the audience as the successful conspirators gobble, quaff, and remember the great days. In a moment Richter will proceed to secure his revenge by setting them against each other in approved classical style, and it is a tribute to this author's mastery that he can get us not just to swallow, but to relish, the hoary antecedents of the situation (which even extends to the casually murderous dropping of a knife within reach of two death-locked combatants).

Let me reiterate the levels on which the play has thus far (that is, for two-thirds of its length) succeeded: (a) as pungent political satire, or if you like as a rough allegory of neo-Nazism; (b) as a perfectly acceptable study of the behaviour of grotesque but believable characters—in this respect, Richter himself shows up *The Man in the Glass Booth* for the hollow conundrum he was; (c) as an essay in irony of language and situation—the writing in the scenes between Richter and his caged but still urbane master is of a quality to make you, if not roar with laughter, then purr with quiet delight; (d) as a brilliant literary exercise, impudently weaving familiar texts and situations into a new and individual fabric; and (e) as an exciting story for whose concluding third I could hardly wait.

In the concluding third, unfortunately, the narrative underpinning lets the structure down.

Michael Blakemore's production matches the play in wit, tension, and inventiveness, and actually surpasses it in narrative lucidity. Leonard Rossiter, whose attributes apparently include the enviable ability to walk effortlessly with his body arched back at an angle of forty-five degrees, solves the problems of playing an outsize character by going out and creating a world of his own, and daring the rest of the play to match up to it.

Tony Steedman, caged or enthroned, is the perfect complement; the poise of this duo probably accounts in some measure for my enthusiasm about the writing of their scenes together.
