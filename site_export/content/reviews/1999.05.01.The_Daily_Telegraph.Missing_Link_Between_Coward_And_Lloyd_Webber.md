IN 1960 a song called Do You Mind? - as in "If I say I love you, do you mind?" - reached number one in the British pop charts. Lionel Bart wrote it, Anthony Newley sang it and it had a cheeky cockney twang, overlaid with imitation American. Some time later, Newley, who had turned to songwriting himself, said what a brilliant idea it had been of Bart's to base a song on such a sharp conversational phrase and how he wished he'd thought of it himself.

I hadn't thought about that song in years, but the deaths of Bart and Newley have got me humming it again. It may have been the only time their paths crossed professionally, but it wasn't all they had in common.

Both, for a start, owed a considerable debt to the same literary classic. Newley first came to notice, in 1948, playing the Artful Dodger in David Lean's film of Oliver Twist and his performance remains indelible: an urchin wise beyond his years, whose insolence had something mournful about it. Bart, of course, entered the big time in 1960 as author and composer of Oliver!, the most successful British musical of its era.

Now what era was that exactly? It's a lost period of popular English theatre, between the end of Noel Coward and the beginning of Andrew Lloyd Webber. It began with a roguish little bang in 1954, and had petered out by 1970. Bart and Newley were among its leading practitioners, but they took some time to emerge.

For the decade after the war the musical had been an American preserve. It was the period of Oklahoma!, Annie Get Your Gun, Carousel, Kiss Me Kate, South Pacific, Guys and Dolls, The King and I, and so on. Home-grown musicals were produced, but few were successful. The big shows (I'm speaking of scale, not quality) were either blown-up seaside entertainments, or imitation Broadway.

This changed with that curious masterpiece The Boy Friend, written by a young man called Sandy Wilson. I say curious because masterpieces are supposed to be original and Wilson's was an imitation - of the fluffy musical comedies, both British and American, of the Twenties. But the pastiche was so loving and skilful and sure of itself that it became its own creation. Promoted to the West End in 1954, after a club try-out in 1953, it ran for ever. It is, like Oliver!, always being done somewhere.

In its time it suggested that British musicals might best assert themselves against the Americans by staying small, maybe even quaint. (Note that this was also the time of the Ealing film comedies.) The idea was reinforced when Julian Slade's Salad Days came to London.

Unlike The Boy Friend it was set in what might just, in 1954, have been the present: a world of debs and perpetual students and harmlessly mad scientists and everybody transported by the sound of a magic piano: "Oh look at me, oh look at me, I'm dancing!" Clearly the real world could not be kept at bay for ever, and the small musical, with roots in the old intimate revue, seemed to call out for satire.

Wilson realised this and followed up The Boy Friend with The Buccaneer, which was rather like a parable on the state of the musical, in terms of children's comics; it set the wholesome English model against the brash American horror strips.

I have a fondness for it since it was the first musical I ever saw. I persuaded my parents to take me on the understanding that it would be, in the words of one its song titles, "good clean fun". It was. However, for the angry young Fifties it was altogether too middle-class. The musical needed a proletarian voice. Some years on, in the wake of Albert Finney and the Beatles, this might have been North Country, but the Fifties was a London decade. Musically, the period belonged to jazz and to Tommy Steele, our first rock 'n' roller.

The breakthrough musical was actually a satire on Steele's rise to fame, entitled Expresso Bongo. Set in the capital's musical and social underworld, it established the convention that a socially significant musical had to involve tarts and small-time crooks, and it inaugurated what I call the Soho musical.

Much of this was like Salad Days in reverse: "Oh look at me, I'm sophisticated!" There was The Crooked Mile and Irma la Douce. The latter, a French Soho musical, was put into English by Julian More, David Heneker and Monty Norman, three writers who had collaborated on Bongo and who combined and separated throughout this period.

Norman wrote music and lyrics for Make Me an Offer, which was about squabbling antique dealers in the Portobello Road and was the best show of the entire period. Heneker, the oldest and most persistent of the trio, had hits a little later on with Half a Sixpence (a cheery adaptation of H G Wells's Kipps) and the monstrous Charlie Girl.

These were both wholesome vehicles for cockney former pop stars (Tommy Steele, Joe Brown) and they served notice on the Soho school. Before doing so, however, it had thrown up Fings Ain't Wot They Used T'Be, the seamiest of them all, with a debut score by Lionel Bart.

Bart was a theatrical natural who had come up through pop (he wrote all Steele's hits) and had a wonderful ear. He had already written lyrics only for Lock Up Your Daughters, which, based on a play by Henry Fielding, was an 18th-century Soho musical, a bit of slap and a lot of tickle. Political correctness makes a revival unlikely. The source play is called Rape Upon Rape. Actually it's the word "rape", not the act itself, that the play and musical make fun of, but I wouldn't envy anybody who tried to make that case in detail.

What is remarkable is that Bart, who was probably not brought up on Restoration comedy, caught the style exactly. He was a lot less fastidious a year later when he set about musicalising Dickens, but the music-hall panache and narrative energy of Oliver! carried it, and continue to carry it. It was the apotheosis of the Soho musical (all the crooks and whores you could desire) and also its death knell. Social significance had given way, inexorably, to family entertainment.

Anthony Newley followed Bart into the arena in 1961 and pursued an opposite course. With his collaborator Leslie Bricusse (dyed-in-the-wool showbiz and still active) he took theatrical cliches - principally the broken-hearted clown - and tried to wring a moral out of them. As a performer, Newley had preserved a dark anarchic streak. He had done some adventurous if whimsical TV work and he had made some funny records. He had started out satirising pop singers and had briefly turned into the real thing.

So when he did his first musical, Stop the World - I Want to Get Off, we all wished him well, even though the show's idea of satire was to put stereotypes on stage and get angry about them. Its idea of emotion was to shed buckets of tears over those same stereotypes. They got away with it, at least commercially, at least once. The follow-up - The Roar of the Greasepaint, the Smell of the Crowd - was a statically self-important allegory that took its imagery wholesale from Waiting for Godot and never made it to London, though it did run on Broadway.

Both these shows, though, had several songs that were hits at the time and some of which - Once in a Lifetime, Who Can I Turn To? - are familiar even now. And this was significant. In the Fifties and early Sixties, before the rock takeover, you could always turn on the radio and hear American theatre songs. You never heard any English ones. Bart began to change this: As Long as He Needs Me, the big one from Oliver! (and a rotten song, but never mind) had a Shirley Bassey record that was number one for weeks. American heavyweights, notably Sammy Davis Jr, adopted Newley and Bricusse.

Both Bart and Newley went into steep personal declines, and neither had a second success to rival their first. Bart's career post-Oliver! was outright disastrous. But they had moved the British musical into the commercial mainstream, and paved the way for the mid-Atlantic musical, as personified by Lloyd Webber. They represented both the triumph of an era and its decadence. Those were interesting times, and bits of them still linger.
