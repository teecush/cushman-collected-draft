Triumph of a Salesman

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Arthur Miller at the National.

NO play written in English since the war has the emotional impact of Arthur Miller's Death of a Salesman (Lyttelton).

It may not quite be a universal work: I don't suppose it means much to orphans, but anyone else must respond to its scenes of family quarrel, with brief reconciliations giving way to fiercer misunderstandings.

It sounds obvious enough, and there have been plenty of critics to sneer at it on those grounds. But if it is so easy to make audiences cry by reminding them of their home lives, one can only wonder why more playwrights haven't brought it off.

The play's supporters have sometimes been as damaging as its detractors. It was hailed, after its premiere in 1949, as the apotheosis of American social drama, as the tragedy of capitalism. But Miller pointed out in his preface that the most sympathetic character in the play is Willy Loman's neighbour, Charley, whose values are in no way different from the salesman's own. Only he pursues them with far less tenacity.

As Charley himself says, "My salvation is that I never took any interest in anything." Willy takes a ferocious interest: in his career as a salesman, and in the future of his sons. Finding them to be good athletes, he thanks Heaven that they are built like Adonises, but there is no doubt that if they had brilliant academic records instead, he would have decided that their minds were all-important, just what was needed to get ahead in business.

Indeed Willy's downfall is that though he believes, he never knows from one moment to the next what his beliefs are. One moment he is telling his elder son Biff that nobody lends a kidder money, the next thing he is telling him to soften up a prospective benefactor with a few jokes. "We never told the truth for 10 minutes in this house," says Biff, but that's not because they prefer lying; it's just that they never know what the truth is.

"Mendacity" -- the Tennessee Williams word -- may be part of the Loman trouble; a greater part of it is credulity. Willy swallows things, and not just because he's a salesman. A revolution is unlikely to destroy the play's relevance; in a socialist society he would be just as gullible. He would still want to live again through his sons, and they would be just as unable to oblige him.

He would probably end up as before, rejected by the son who loves him but can hardly bear to look at him, while the shallower one prepares to emulate all his father's worst traits. (Nothing in the play is funnier or more telling than young Happy's repeated assurances -- quite sincere at the moment he makes them -- that he is about to get married: as if that would solve everything.) And Willy would still have a wife like Linda to bear with him, to try to save him from himself, and to storm at him, without realising that her fatal acquiescence has helped to make him what he is. Probably, if he took the chances he keeps talking about, they wouldn't have worked out, but she stops him even from trying.

While things go well, she believes what Willy tells her; when they disintegrate she sticks by him, knowing that he is trying to kill himself but unable, when it finally happens, to figure out why. I think that Miller keeps the precise degree of her knowledge unnecessarily foggy -- this is one point at which the play is open to the charge of sentimentality -- but the fuse leading to the suicide is immaculately laid.

Biff's last display of anger proves to Willy (quite rightly) that his son loves him; he commits suicide so that the boy will inherit the insurance money and make glorious use of it. That's not the only reason (Miller's aim of conveying an infinity of motives -- of writing clearly about something essentially confused -- is fully achieved), but it's the one that pushes him over the edge; just as it is the well-meaning Edgar who administers the final shove to the sanity of Lear.

To argue that it is Willy's nature, not his profession, that destroys him is not to deny that much of the play's value derives from its keen eye for the petty realities of salesmanship, and from its ear for the poetry. For when the younger, brighter Willy is reigning his sons with his triumphs on the road, he does seem momentarily to have, if not "a good dream," then an exciting one. He isn't necessarily lying all the time.

The language of the play has been grievously underrated. Line after line flashes out. If Willy's dream of his funeral -- "all the old-timers with the strange licence plates" -- obviously strains for poetry, it just as obviously achieves it. And even the supposed clinkers have their validity. Why shouldn't Linda tell us "attention must be paid to such a person"? Greek choruses make equally explicit appeals to our concern?

Michael Rudman's excellent production is graced by a superb Willy from Warren Mitchell, absolutely clued in to the man's essence, which is a fearful jauntiness; his mind hops, terrified or hopeful, from one subject to another. It is impossible to hold a coherent conversation with him. The scenes of semi-flashback (where Willy talks simultaneously to one character in the past and another in the present) are brilliantly done.

Almost equally good is Doreen Mantle's Linda, steadfast but rather bovine, and entirely free of false nobility. (The epilogue, though, is surprisingly unmoving; awkwardly directed against a new and superfluous backcloth. I also wish that John Gunter's set did not trundle so noisily -- or trundle at all -- whenever a time-slip occurs. They ought to take us by surprise.)

Stephen Greif, looking like a young Tony Bennett, and with some of the same ox-like sincerity, grows through the evening; his one calculated attempt to play his father's game and lie about a failed interview is thinly done. And David Baxt's Happy, especially when taking his ease in a restaurant, is a shiningly shabby heel.

The Mercury, Colchester, has made a speciality of reviving rare period comedies: currently, Hubert Henry Davies's The Mollusc, which has understandably been retitled Never Lift a Finger. All the same, the molluscs and even "molluscry" constantly assault the ear, less euphoniously than the author may have designed. The sea-creature is his metaphor for a special kind of idleness, one that consists of shuffling off all exertion on to others, while administering repeated doses of self-pity and self-deception. An Edwardian wife and governess are victimised by it, and her brother attempts to cure it. Languor can of course be sexy, though Davies never explicitly turns it on. All the same there are depths to be explored; direction chooses. David Buxton apparently doesn't, but still offers a pleasant evening with a pretty performance from Bronwyn Williams and a vigorous one from Philip Anthony.

Richard Crane's Gogol (Theatre Upstairs), only distantly indebted to the Russians, is a three-part saga progressing from repression to exultance to collapse. Both as writer and performer Mr Crane is immensely skilful: if I shuffled, blame it on my low tolerance-level for 50-minute monologues.

Finally an apology: my praise last week for the performance of the sergeant in The Case of the Oily Levantine was somewhat devalued by my calling the actor Roger Hood. He isn't a Hood; he's a Leach.
