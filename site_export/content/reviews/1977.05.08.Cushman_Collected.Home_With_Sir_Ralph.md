Home with Sir Ralph

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

RALPH RICHARDSON plays every role as though he had written it himself. He does not actually give the impression of making it up as he goes along (he is far too deliberate for that), but he bestows upon his lines a weight which, however eccentric, registers always as unarguable, inimitable, right.

In The Kingfisher (Lyric) Sir Ralph as playwright is off form: or, to drop the pretence, William Douglas-Home's script is an unworthy choice. As in No Man's Land, Sir Ralph is a lonely literary lion -- in this case a literary laird -- reaching out for companionship while the effective prisoner of his own domestic staff (which this time numbers one). You do not have to be a Pinter fanatic to find yourself aching in this piece for a comparable wit or resonance. Sir Ralph, delivering his customary goods, keeps you aching. His words sound as ever like oracular utterances, if you can conceive of an oracle possessed of both humour and vulnerability. They ought to go beyond the commonplace, but they never make it.

Of course Sir Ralph is grouchily splendid, and of course Celia Johnson, as the lady of his past whom he hopes to make the lady of his future, is sweet and sensible, and of course Alan Webb as the jealous valet is superb. The triumphant narrowing of his eyes when he hears that his master's romance, once on, is now off, provides the most memorable moment of the evening, closely followed by Sir Ralph's defensive mockery of an unseen crippled neighbour.

* * *

Two breaks in the bland surface, though, are hardly enough; a third -- plainly intended to be the biggest of all -- misfires. Miss Johnson alleges that Mr Webb has been in love with Sir Ralph for 50 years. In a play of course you can allege anything; unless you can also demonstrate in action, though, your audience will refuse either to believe or to care. All that is really on offer here (stressed in Lindsay Anderson's direction and Alan Tagg's arboreal setting) is "atmosphere."

There are other atmospheres and one more invigorating may be breathed in Barrie Keeffe's A Mad World, My Masters (Young Vic). The title has been used before -- by Thomas Middleton in 1605 -- and though his play might repay revival, it is good to have the title to be going on with: an excellent one which sums up a whole genre. Middleton excelled in the Jacobean city comedy, which recognised corruption and obsession, shook its head, dried its eyes, shrugged its shoulders and laughed.

Many modern playwrights, feeling their own London to be every bit as steamy as their ancestors', have hankered after the form; few have plunged into it with as much spirit as Mr Keeffe. If some of his set-pieces of invective reinforce the suspicion that the English language now swings less easily than it did 350 years ago, what distinguishes Mr Keeffe is the ceaseless energy of his plotting; the play never stops moving. It has, what is more, a destination; everything converges on the Royal Mews where the Queen of England (this is a jubilee production) frostily announces that she is not in the habit of bestowing knighthoods on geese.

Middleton and company may have been Mr Keeffe's springboard, but other influences have come between; I was reminded of the Goons, of Theatre Workshop in its bustling heyday, and of the stage version of The Fosdyke Saga. Jane Wood, who was the scheming Mrs Fosdyke, is now the scheming Mrs Sprightly, deprived by a legal technicality of the insurance due on her son (who died when all he really wanted was, profitably, accident) and bent on revenge. Her prey is a tycoon, a fanatical monarchist whose main desires are under-age girls and a knighthood. His daughter careers through the play on a bicycle, dripping, in Cecily Hobbs's aptly dewy performance, with social conscience. A police inspector sizes up the opposing forces, decides that might could well be right, and goes into disguise as, roughly speaking, Margaret Thatcher: a role in which Simon Callow outdoes himself.

* * *

An Irish doctor feeds a whole housing development on tranquillisers and the occasional aphrodisiac. An out-of-sight (and out-of-mind) musician wanders through. Miss Wood doubles as the Queen. A younger Sprightly, persuaded of her resemblance (which is indeed considerable) to Angela Rippon, does a cunning and beautiful strip while reading the news; since the actress is Gillian Barge, previously noted in rather straitlaced roles, the effect is as great as if Angela herself had bared all. The Joint Stock company, directed by William Gaskill and Max Stafford-Clark, enjoys a marvellous holiday, and so do we.

Towards the end, Mr Keeffe introduces mayhem. I suspect that his principles were nagging him; the Jacobean Sprightlies would have been triumphant gull-catchers; Mr Keeffe has to remind himself that his are downtrodden. After an evening spent admiring their roguery, this seems like cheating. Still, I suppose he has the right to spoil his own play.

* * *

Peter Barnes's alteration of the ending of a genuine Jacobean comedy, Jonson's The Devil is an Ass (Lyttelton), is more questionable.

Jonson finished with some of his characters coming to their senses, and others behaving with positive decency. This seems to have disappointed Mr Barnes, who puts the whole cast literally at one another's throats and brings on Satan, who should know, to tell us how awful they are. Jonson wrote only one watertight malevolent morality, Volpone, set in Venice. His London plays are looser and, if only by default, more genial.

The refusal of Shakespeare's generation to be as cynical as we would like is a constant source of pain to our theatre. In The Devil's stablemate at the Lyttelton, Measure for Measure, the Duke is presented as an impish prankster concerned with nothing but enjoying his own manoeuvres. Nobody is going to believe this, but here goes: Measure for Measure is what it claims to be, a comedy. It has a happy ending. It is, what is more, a romantic comedy and the Duke is the juvenile lead. He gets the girl and deserves to. All that apart, these two Birmingham Rep productions, directed by Stuart Burge, are well worth their visitation and ours. Peter Vaughan's Fitzdottrel in The Devil has grown in stature; in foolishness, in vindictiveness, and in perverted geniality. The National's Volpone could use him. In Measure Anna Calder-Marshall and David Burke give great strength to the Angelo-Isabella relationship. Supporting playing in both is very solid.

* * *

King Lear (Aldwych), though a hell of a thing to encounter at the end of a long week, is another gift from the Midlands. Trevor Nunn's production is much tightened since Stratford; the Victorian costumes are unobtrusive and helpful. The wicked sisters (Barbara Leigh-Hunt and Meg Davies) develop plausibly from frigid propriety to sadism, and two performances (Bob Peck's Kent and Michael Pennington's Edgar) are much improved.

Hopkins must have been inspired by Lear when he wrote that "the mind, mind has mountains"; Donald Sinden frequently brings us, shivering, to their edge, lurching towards some terrible new thought. His recognition of Poor Tom, "the thing itself," is finely done; so is the robust tyranny of the beginning. All that is missing, in production and performance, is the concerted cataclysm of the end; this is not a great Lear, but it is a very good one.
