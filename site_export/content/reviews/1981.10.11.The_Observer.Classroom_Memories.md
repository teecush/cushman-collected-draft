Classroom memories

ROBERT CUSHMAN on plays by Colin Welland and Arnold Wesker.

TO judge from his programme-note Colin Welland thinks of his play Roll On Four O'Clock (Lyric, Hammersmith) as some kind of nostalgic polemic. The play is set in a northern secondary modern school some 13 years ago. Mr Welland taught in one himself, and he writes bitterly of the social divisiveness they both reflected and encouraged.

That's in the programme; in the play there are a few lines about the dead-end jobs awaiting the boys when they leave, but most of what we see - sub-military discipline, casual brutality, equally casual kindness - is common to British education at any level. I doubt if the comprehensives have changed it much. I went to a grammar school just down the road from this theatre, and much of the play struck, amusingly or uncomfortably, close to home.

The amusement outweighs the discomfort. There is a continuing thread of plot about the persecution of a possibly homosexual boy both by his contemporaries and by some of the staff, but it is not very deeply investigated and some of the characters' attitudes are inconsistent.

There seem to be only 10 people on the staff; small for a school but big for a play, and hard to keep track of if we don't have a camera to do our focusing for us. (The play was written for television.) There is a new young art-teacher. (Mr Welland taught art.) Maybe we are meant to view the action through his eyes; the end of the play suggests that, but nothing else does. His antagonists are dragged even more unceremoniously into the limelight.

Mr Welland sees the atmosphere in the staff-room as similar to that in the playground; the keynote of both is boisterous resignation. The kids are deprived of choice, and the masters mostly suspect they have made the wrong one. From this Mr Welland as author furnishes group-scenes of vivacity if not finesse, which are then brought triumphantly to life by Mr Welland as director.

The evening's highlight has Clive Swift, as senior art master, striding the floor with eyes rapturously closed, exhorting his class to get with the rhythm of clay-moulding, a rhythm that finally resolves itself into a stirring rendition of 'Dry Bones.' A companion piece has Glyn Owen's headmaster addressing us - the audience - at weekly assembly. These two players, along with Windsor Davies (woodwork), establish a pervasive air of beleaguered decency.

* * *

In Caritas (Cottesloe) Arnold Wesker takes the story of a young religious devotee, who walled herself up as an anchoress in Surrey in 1329, and moves it to Norfolk, for the dialect, and 1381, for the Peasants' Revolt. Christine Carpenter's retreat is seen as one way of grappling with a changing world; that of her former fiance, who dies with Wat Tyler, as another.

One sympathises with the Bishop of Norwich, faced with both problems at once, but seeing no special need to relate to them; and is less grateful than he must have been that all the exciting bits happen off stage.

For the first half of the play Christine is invisible behind her wall; a local crone drops by to regale her with stories of other self-imposed martyrdoms. On the other side of the stage her family doggedly grumble.

All this covers the ground; nothing in it defies belief, but nothing compels it either. Then the stage, or a section of it, revolves; and we are with Christine, who is having second thoughts. Her historical counterpart physically broke out and was put back in again, but Mr Wesker is not condescending to anything so vulgarly dramatic. His heroine stays where she is, going madder, which at least provides Patti Love with a superb opportunity to do what she does best, i.e. to be uncanny and to suggest that what the author does best is personal trauma.

Her huddled figure against the wall of her cell is as haunting as her speech, which is a desperate crash-course in Christian masochism. (Mr Wesker is of course Jewish; and his play, the dramatic equivalent of a fast, opened on Yom Kippur.) Frederick Treves as the Bishop begins suavely, but when he has to go to pieces he goes to pieces without, seemingly, much guidance from his director, John Madden. There is a Cottesloe tradition (broken only by 'American Buffalo' and 'Has Washington Legs') of doing small, lugubrious new plays. Caritas fits snugly into it.

There are, though I hate to admit it, worse things than nostalgia. The Mitford Girls (Globe) is bursting with calculated high spirits, not one of which managed to raise mine. It is the nadir of the compilation show. The only facts that seem to have struck Caryl Brahms and Ned Sherrin about the Mitford sisters are that they were upper-class, which rather argued for a fusillade of supposedly endearing West End snob jokes, and that they lived through the Twenties and Thirties, which makes them good for musical nostalgia.

Nostalgia in this context means that good old songs are performed not because they're good, but because they're old -- or because their titles are, in the authors' fevered imaginations, dramatically relevant. Thank You for the Memory, a very specific American lyric about a broken marriage, really has little to do with English nursery reminiscence.

There are also new songs; the authors' view of their characters' range of interests may be gathered from the fact that three numbers called 'Why Do People Fall in Love', 'Why Fall for Love' and 'I'll Fall in Love' are done virtually back to back and are remorselessly reprised. If you want to stage an inter-war songfest then do it, but why tie it to the Mitfords whose interests, apart from Nancy's surprising toughness as writer, lies in their bizarre political commitments, here treated as if they couldn't possibly have been serious. (Imagine a show that makes you feel sorry on behalf of Unity Mitford.)

The presumption is relentlessly coy; the girls quote at us all evening, but never do more than giggle with each other. They also play all the other female parts, which is confusing. Patricia Hodge gives a good performance, not two. There is some genetic tic to the look of things, but that somehow makes it worse.

One of the plundered classics, Cole Porter's Let's Do It, also turns up in Moliere's The Learned Ladies, as staged in Richard Wilbur's translation at (and indeed by) Pentameters, above the Three Horseshoes pub in Hampstead, by a new lot calling themselves the High Command Theatre Company.

This and other melodic interpolations, plus the removal of the play to the country-house Twenties, suggests that Moliere's bluestockings are to be reduced to Mitford status, and that the director thinks that their play too is about love. But it comes through as sex, which never did a comedy any harm, and a bright, intelligent cast bring all the characters to life.

The play comes over as better - certainly, with stronger situations and more pertinent satire - than either The Misanthrope or Tartuffe. Moliere, staged without solemnity and without camp, can make an audience freely and continually laugh; a last performance tonight.
