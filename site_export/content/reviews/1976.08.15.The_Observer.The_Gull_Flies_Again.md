The gull flies again

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

Touching down in London for the first time in at least a fortnight, The Seagull (Duke of York's) can still spring the occasional surprise. Some of these are due to Mark Woolgar's unusually forthright adaptation; it may not signify much in London in 1976, but this is the first time in my experience that the language of Chekhov's heroines has caught up with that of Eliza Doolittle. One might be hearing much of the play for the first time; a compliment which Chekhov generally attracts and for which his various translators must take great credit.

Mr Woolgar's production, which has been brought in from Derby Playhouse, is as brisk and emphatic as his adaptation, which at least makes it a refreshing contrast to the curiously flat version which Lindsay Anderson directed earlier this year for the Lyric Theatre Company. (Whatever, by the way, happened to the Lyric Theatre Company?) True, there is no Helen Mirren in St Martin's Lane and I thought at first that Georgina Hale's Nina, curly and girlish, was going to be awful; she rattled off Konstantin's play as if it were meaningless (Nina may well have been defeated by it; but surely she put up a fight; at this point she wants to please Konstantin and impress his mother.)

Later on, Miss Hale begins to display real insight: she harps nervously and obsessively on the words 'fame' and 'glamour,' clasps her hands in adoring imitation of her idol Arkadina, directly anticipates -- with her fevered complaint that her head is spinning -- the dementia of the last act. This is played with a haunting self-mockery, if without the bedrock determination that allowed Miss Mirren to make final sense of the play.

Asked in the first act to recite something from Konstantin's play, Nina refuses to oblige; her cry of 'it's so boring' amounts almost to a long-range death-warrant for its author. Gabrielle Lloyd's Masha, who made the request in a solitary bright-eyed moment, sinks back into her usual bleariness, pushed a little further towards her hopeless marriage with Medviedenko, whose long-suffering futility is here given unusually sharp definition by Dennis Denny.

Their relationship, which foreshadows and inverts (since here it is the man who is baby-besotted), the ghastly union of Andrei and Natasha in Three Sisters, emerges more strongly in each new production of The Seagull.

Coarsening through the play, Miss Lloyd still preserves the thread of her feeling for Konstantin; it is she who pricks up her ears at his off-stage piano-playing, and she who knows immediately that he has shot himself.

Completing the play's sardonic picture of youth is Richard O'Callaghan's Konstantin, the best I have seen. He whines, he snivels, he hardly grows up, but there is passion and perception in him, however ludicrous, and he freshens the speeches about finding new theatrical forms without disguising their naivete.

He is also locked into his relation with his mother; when he quotes Hamlet at her he does so with real venom, the venom of absolute dependence. This Oedipal Konstantin might have fitted well into Jonathan Miller's production a couple of years ago. (When Seagulls come, they come not single spies.)

The elders fare less well; Mr Woolgar's emphasis on the primary colours in Chekhov makes them seem garish. Denis Holmes's Shamrayev shamelessly shouts (Mr Holmes was a notable quietist with the RSC, so maybe he is compensating now) and though Sheila Ballantine's Arkadina is a ruthless and witty study of a desperately dominating woman (whose main defence against time is her wig), this may be a part which really needs a star. This is especially so when a star is actually to hand in another role. Alan Bates's Trigorin is not a selfish performance, but it hardly fits; he is acting more elaborately than anyone else (perhaps more subtly, but in this context it is difficult to tell), gesturing and stammering more. The character never quite appears.

* * *

T. Zee (Royal Court), credited to Richard O'Brien and Richard Hartley, is the successor to Mr O'Brien's Rocky Horror Show, but completely lacks its lightness of touch. It also marks a partial return, after the skill and delicacy of Peter Gill's Small Change, to the hallowed Court tradition of high-minded bungling.

The stage is so destructively cluttered that the actors have no room to do anything but sing undistinguished songs into their hand-mikes, which gets monotonous. Even when the music stops, the lyrics seem to continue, since the script is written in doggerel couplets; anyone sensitive to rhyme will find himself in pain.

Mr O'Brien has written an awful part for himself as a knock-kneed anthropologist exploring the ruins of Los Angeles, and a better one for his lady companion, Belinda Sinclair, who can and does sing. Warren Clarke as Tarzan, into whose company they fall, also has an unexpectedly winning country-and-western way; most of what goodwill is going in Nicholas Wright's production accrues to him. The action takes place on a sort of giant pin-table in a libidinous inferno, peopled by the debris of Hollywood. But this time Mr O'Brien has forgotten satire and settled for celebratory camp. Nothing happens, fortissimo.

* * *

Four To One (Young Vic) is a first play by the excellent National Theatre actor Gawn Grainger which his colleagues seem to enjoy playing. It has less to offer its audience since it is inclusively constructed, despite a final sprint, and tends to an aesthetic of group menace and a theory of violence already familiar. Only its setting -- the back room of a pub, reserved for poker players -- and the resultant atmosphere of sour camaraderie seem to derive from Mr Grainger's experience of life rather than of other plays.

It is on this run that the cast fasten; I would guess that actors spend more time in pubs than most. As a National Theatre phenomenon -- casting Derek Newark, Pip Hinton, Trevor Ray, Mark McManus and Simon Ward an untrammelled opportunity to display their prowess and keep in training -- the occasion is fairly encouraging.

* * *

The cast of Seven Girls (Open Space) work in a harder, less accessible environment which they recreate brilliantly. Lynne Monahan, drugged and unreachable, Katherine Haber, ferocious, and Linda Regan, lost and terrified, head a flock of caged Swedish nightingales, inmates of a reformatory for teenagers run on humanitarian lines which inexorably crush humanely. The play, written and directed by Carl-Johan Seth, is scrupulously fair to its keepers, and the two sides come together to create an image of a life rigid in outline and unpredictable in event. There are perhaps more characters than we can take in, and the play asks too much to focus our concentration, but go along and grab what you can.
