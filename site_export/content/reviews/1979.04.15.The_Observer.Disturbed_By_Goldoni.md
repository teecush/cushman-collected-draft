Disturbed by Goldoni

THE Glasgow Citizens' Theatre last week completed a typically even predictably adventurous season with a Goldoni piece called Country Life. The theatre will now be closed for repairs until September, though the company will be on view at the Edinburgh Festival in another Goldoni. Their refusal to repeat themselves is, I suppose, admirable, but it can also be frustrating; somebody should point a gun at them and demand a retrospective.

Country Life, for example, is far too substantial an achievement to vanish after a three-week run. It is, in fact, three plays, though crammed into one long evening, of which only the middle one has a rural setting, where one or two of the idle rich, plus several others who are idle but can only wish they were rich, fall languishingly or farcically in and out of love. Some end up mated against their wills; others achieve their desired partner, but seem set for no less disastrous matches.

The first play shows us these same characters wondering whether to spend their own hard-hoarded cash on the trip, or alternatively looking for someone who will give them the price of it; in the third they are all back in town, again trying to sort out the tangles--though Goldoni sternly refuses to give them all the help we might expect. I had thought him to be a strictly classical happy-ending man; these plays show him to have been altogether more untidy and more disturbing.

Robert David Macdonald, the translator-director, has advanced the play from Goldoni's eighteenth century to the beginning of our own. This is no help to the early scenes, which are thinly satirical; we are expected to laugh at a society which we are never permitted to see. But as the action grows lusher, the style begins to justify itself. Plangent operatic chords sound behind the scenes as self-destructive heroines surrender their lovers to rivals with a better legal or financial claim. Philip Prowse has set the entire action within a gigantic green conservatory, and the hot-house feeling suits the play's emotional climate.

Mr Prowse's sets are Glasgow's glories; this one is as striking as any, though less individual than most--one can imagine another designer coming up with it. His designs throw a strange light on the acting, which is always extravagantly young. Youthful companies generally cultivate the athletic and stentorian virtues; they come to notice with productions of, say, Shakespeare's histories. I generally begin in Glasgow by finding the playing hopelessly inadequate to the sophistication demanded of it, and then adjust as the evening proceeds and the production creates its own world. Comic performances, composed of immature and irritating mannerisms, turn out to be valuable counterweights in moments of stress; young actresses who seem hopelessly out of their depth in social comedy suddenly transfix you with their emotional candour. They also justify the peculiar style of Mr Macdonald's translation, in which academic archaism and flip modernity mingle with wedges of frank quotation, mostly Shakespearean. A more experienced and artful cast would point up the differences; this one sails blithely through them. You are never nudged into laughter. The Citizens' does not, despite some claims, represent the peak of British theatre, but it is invaluable and unique.

London critics, and London companies, are sometimes scornfully accused of never venturing north of Watford. That is a canard; it is travelling to Watford itself that we find a problem. But last week I finally made it to the Palace Theatre, for the British premiere of Lillian Hellman's 30-year-old play The Autumn Garden. I cannot say my intrepidity was rewarded. A shapeless and interminable round of domestic fidgets in a residential rooming-house on the Gulf of Mexico is given by Stephen Hollis a lackadaisical production almost in excess of its demerits. For three hours it is sleepy time down South. The drawl of course is a powerful brake on British actors; I sympathised with a couple who quietly forgot about it. The acting, some of it by well-known performers, runs a collector's gamut from the apathetic to the appalling.

* * *

A third regional theatre has brought its product to London: to the Cambridge, from Sheffield, comes Chicago. At this third remove the musical inevitably lacks both the spectacular inventiveness of Bob Fosse's Broadway staging and the refreshing spaciousness of Sheffield's open stage. Some performances, though, have ripened: Antonia Ellis now flashes a dazzlingly innocent smile as the murdering Roxie and Jenny Logan a trouper's weary grin as her cell-mate Velma, born to lose in the race for stardom.

The equation the show proposes is that crime and entertainment are one, and number after number makes this point with a relentless "Brechtian," or simply repetitious. The story, with both heroines in the same predicament and a chorus of other accused to back them up, already invites monotony; the treatment compounds it. Fred Ebb's lyrics and John Kander's music do not advance the story, they only comment on it--and it is always the same comment. Items billed as songs turn out to be exchanges of dialogue against a rhythmic background. Faced with a sequence that seems to call for sustained musicalisation--Roxie's trial, for instance--the authors back away.

This would be enough to make the show, despite the remnants of Mr Fosse's choreographic flair, a disappointment. What makes it detestable is epitomised in a number called "Razzle Dazzle." All the tricks of the showman's trade are presented for our applause--and the first night audience duly responded. Were they listening to the lyrics which mocked them for their acceptance? Well, a good joke on the audience you might think, but since razzle dazzle is in fact all the show has to offer (spiced with a cynicism as bland and automatic as the sentimentality of The Sound of Music when that was fashionable) the joke rebounds on the authors.

"Long as you keep 'em way off-balance / How can they know you got no talents," I wouldn't accuse Messrs Fosse, Kander and Ebb of being untalented (for the good and proven reason that they aren't), but all they are doing here is throwing their own self-indulgent misgivings about their craft in the audience's face, and despising them for not noticing. Throwing a few generalisations about crime and society, they have fooled the critics as well. There is not enough wit or substance in the writing to make the concert genuinely interesting. There is only flash, which the audience lapped up. Good flash, too, some of it--though one has to say, despite the pleasure of seeing an even competently staged musical arrive in London, that in this production it is not good enough. Not with all the pretensions it dangles from its spangles.

* * *

Last week, I committed a grave sin of omission in my review of the RSC's Pericles at the Other Place. I failed to mention Stephen Oliver's music, which not only contributes immensely to the atmosphere, and even to the plot, but is also historically notable as the first occasion on which a chamber Shakespeare production has come equipped with a full chamber score: part Oriental, part baroque.
