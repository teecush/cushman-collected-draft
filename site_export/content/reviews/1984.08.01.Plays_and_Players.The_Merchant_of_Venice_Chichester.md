The Merchant of Venice — Chichester

Alec Guinness plays a very dignified Shylock, and a very quiet one. It is at the opposite extreme from his Fagin, which was overflowing with extravagant Jewishness. You might say that where Fagin was an old-style orthodox performance, this Shylock is strictly reform. The character's more obnoxious traits are played down. There is nothing of the passionate killjoy in his attacks on carnivals in general and music in particular; no grotesque oscillations between bloodlust and cupidity when Tubal goads him with the tale of Jessica's elopement. He is heartbroken at the loss of his wife's turquoise; and you wonder when the balance will come. Is Guinness declining to acknowledge that the man is actually a villain?

The moment arrives when he is told that Antonio is certainly undone; he murmurs 'that's very true' RevIeWs with adamantine reflectiveness. His revenge-scheme snaps into place. From then on, he is a man possessed. It has little effect on the tenor of his voice. He remains serene and businesslike, confident in the impregnability of his case. His addresses to the Venetian court sound as unanswerable as his early meditations on Antonio. He has one moment of triumphant diablerie, feeling for the beating of his enemy's heart so as to know just where to stick the knife; but even that is done as if it were the most natural thing in the world. Defeated, he tries to salvage what he can; as each line of defence is destroyed by Portia, he falls back unflinching on the next.

His exit must be the least obviously spectacular in the play's history. This Jew knows better than to put up a useless fight. My favourite subtlety, in a performance full of them, is the pain in his eyes as he tries to persuade himself that he is glad to see the back of Launcelot Gobbo. This should be the most sympathetic of Shylocks, just as David Suchet's, full of the virulent gusto that only a Jewish actor could permit himself in the role, should have been the least. And yet I remember that when Mr Suchet was order to convert to Christianity, the whole house went into shock. Up to then one had thought as usual, a Jewish villain, okay, I'm broadminded, I know about Elizabethan prejudice, I'll accept it for an evening.

It's a comedy; maybe Shylock deserves what he gets. Then comes that crucial humiliation, whose memory one had blotted out, and it is intolerable. But when Guinness got the order of the font, some people in the first-night audience, very Elizabethanly, laughed. Now this may be a reflection on the Chichester punkers who spent the evening applauding mostly dreadful acting (with particular warmth for the mirthless Gobbo and unspeakable Prince of Arragon) and who even sabotaged Guinness's first sly entrance; it took them a moment to spot him, but they tumbled eventually and clapped him anyway. But for that last laugh he was, in a sense, asking. This is not a Gentile Jew (it is racially perfect, however modest) but it is a Christian Shylock: the Jew seen as wronged, wronging, and wrong.

The values of the play are against him; and they are perfectly articulated in the trial. This scene has a tonic effect on Patrick Garland's production; everyone in the cast suddenly starts acting, something which up till then they have left to the star and to Leslee Udwin's Jessica. Joanna McCallum, a principalboy Portia before she goes into disguise, does the mercy speech as if she and the play really meant it; and she extends it into the series of lifelines she throws to Shylock (trying to save him as much as Antonio) and the matching series of body-blows with which she destroys him. The Venetians remain horrible, but the play's morality transcends them. No wonder Shylock goes quietly.

Whether this atones for the rest of the production is dubious. It all takes place on a remarkably lazy revolve, which bestirs itself only to shift a fountain from one side of the stage to the other. We learn that Venice is a place where they keep their water-supply stage right, and Belmont is where they keep it stage-left. Belmont is also a place where three large lace table-cloths drop from above, whenever someone wants to have a go at the caskets. Chichester remains a place where a large and lopsided theatre terrorises designers, directors, and performers. David Yelland gets the better of it in a thoughtful account of Bassanio's casket-speech but nowhere else. Miss Udwin commands from her first entrance to her last.

And Guinness, who moves as gravely and lightly as he speaks, is magic as only experience allied to inspiration can make an actor. In 25 years of haunting theatres I have never before seen him in a classic. My loss, and yours, and maybe even his.
