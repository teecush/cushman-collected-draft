THEATRE/1: Robert Cushman on
Handke, Coward and convicts.

The riddle
of the lake

The legend goes, or so we are informed, that a horseman, lost on a winter night while looking for Lake Constance, unwittingly rode right over its thinly frozen surface. Arriving at a village he asked the locals the way to the lake, was told he had already crossed it, and promptly dropped dead from retrospective fright.

What has Peter Handke in his play *The Ride Across Lake Constance* (Hampstead Theatre Club) done with this extraordinarily haunting tale, beyond obliging each of his reviewers to expend a paragraph on retelling it? It serves, you will scarcely be surprised to learn, as a metaphor for the precious sustaining distance that separates our experience of life from its unbearable reality. I have to confess to some disappointment with a play which, having chosen so chilling a central image, leaves it sitting in a programme note. Nobody on the stage itself actually mentions Lake Constance (perhaps logic would demand that they dropped down dead if they did). But the evening provides a more than respectable quota of haunting moments, one of which, to my delight, does concern a lake. Alan Howard tells of how he sat beside one and noticed to his amazement that it was sparkling, just like a good lake should.

The play's main theme is, in fact, the relationship between objects (including people) and our preconceptions about them. Its major source of terror is the oppressive weight of those preconceptions: our first sight of something determines our subsequent behaviour. So when Mr Howard first comes upon the scene (which appears to be a drawing-room), accompanied by the resplendent Faith Brook and the mercilessly pretty Nicola Pagett, it is already occupied by Nigel Hawthorne and Nicky Henson. Mr Henson, having greeted the newcomers with a polite but ill-advised "What would you like?" is immediately tagged as a shop-assistant and treated as one; not surprisingly, he plays along. Mr Hawthorne tells of the ghastly day he had when a woman whom he took to be mad because she was trampling on eggshells disconcertingly proved to have good reason for her conduct. (I think Mr Hawthorne could afford to plumb murkier depths in his treatment of this speech; I have no other quarrel with his performance, and none at all with anybody else's.)

On at least one occasion the characters manage to escape from the tyranny of predetermined associations. If someone is flinching, they will not assume that he is suffering the pangs of conscience; he merely flinches. If a drawer is stuck halfway out of its chest, they will not, so to speak, look into it; they will just accept its stuckness. Not only accept, but celebrate: they sing about its stuckness, and as they sing they dance. This is their high point; retribution strikes at the end of the play when they are all scared rigid by a baby crying and Mr Henson, in what I take to be a propitiatory gesture, unsticks the drawer and pushes it firmly shut.

As social comment all this is very apt; I only jib at the implication (fostered, perhaps, less by Handke himself than by his critics) that it also has value as satire on the theatre. The revival of Noel Coward's *Design for Living* (Phoenix) provides more rewarding insights into the social and artistic connotations of the verb "to act."

Otto paints, Leo writes plays, Gilda decorates interiors; they are truly inseparable, but only discover this fact after they have tried coupling in each of the available permutations. In any other company they are outsiders; to underline this, Coward gives them Central European names while moving the action through Paris, London and New York. Each capital brings them greater material success, makes them more outrageous or, hopefully from the audience's point of view, more delightful.

Ideally, the audience should see them in double focus: as Gilda, Otto and Leo, who are insufferable, and know it, and tell each other so with murderous candour, and as the all-licensed stars of the show (at the premiere in 1933, Coward himself and the Lunts). Vanessa Redgrave, Jeremy Brett and John Stride hurl their knives and bare their wounds with great skill. Mr Stride, who has developed more steadily than any other actor I can think of, plays Leo the lionised with a silvery mane and a satisfyingly contemptuous growl. Mr Brett is maybe a little too open to fit into this jungle, but on the whole the play is mined as deeply for character values as it could ever be. In the third act Messrs Stride and Brett make a magnificent white-tie entrance like twin Fred Astaires, blending most harmoniously into the black-and-silver New York apartment.

Yet the suspicion that Leo and Otto are no more than insufferable little boys refuses to be shaken, and is powerfully reinforced by their final appearance in matching pyjamas. Even at their bohemian peak they have merely got drunk before breakfast on brandy chased by sherry. (If they had stayed sober on it, that might have been something.) They behave monstrously, but the text, which thins as the evening lengthens, cannot make them sacred; as always, Coward has trusted to his players' surpassing glamour to do that. Messrs Stride and Brett are very good actors, but so is their chief butt, Peter Bayliss. What gives them the right to patronise him?

Miss Redgrave, of course, is a different case. Hers is not the wittiest Gilda imaginable, but she radiates and intensifies and in thirties gowns shimmers. And this being a Michael Blakemore production the supporting players are given room to breathe: the solidity of Neil Wilson as a reporter, trudging through an automatic interview, and of the superb Hazel Hughes as one of Coward's disparaging domestics, is the best rebuke to the posturing of their tormentors, and contributes to the ambiguous texture of the play's fine second act.

A less intriguing triangle is tinkled to fleeting effect in Tina Brown's *Under the Bamboo Tree* (King's Head, Upper Street, N1): Carole Boyd and Olivia Mundy do spirited battle over the weary head of Ben Aris. This is half of a double bill, of which the remainder is actually called *Triangle*, but proves to be a monologue, well written (James Saunders), better acted (Michael Graham Cox) and again concerned with theatrical illusion, a subject of which you and I have had quite enough this week.

Steve Gooch's *Female Transport* (Half Moon, Alie Street, E1) has done two impossible things: he has written a believable historical play (not a documentary) and he has convincingly evoked an enclosed community of women. The enclosure is the hold of a convict ship, en route for Australia. Remember Mr Gooch's name for the future, but see his play now.
