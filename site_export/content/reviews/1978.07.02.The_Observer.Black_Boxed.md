Black boxed

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Stratford.

AT LAST new young directors seem to be emerging capable of working on the main stage at Stratford.

After Michael Bogdanov's Taming of the Shrew comes Measure for Measure from Barry Kyle, who has collaborated with his seniors on the RSC's larger stages, but has confined his previous solo work to their miniature spaces. His present production bears the marks of his confinement; there were times when I felt that all concerned would have been happy to have been transported down the road to the Other Place. It is not, physically or emotionally, an expansive production.

Christopher Morley, who designed the celebrated white box in which so many Stratford productions were set in the sixties, now returns with a black box. The final scene, played virtually on a forestage, could also use more people; the economies that have reduced the RSC's acting strength are unfortunate for those plays (Coriolanus is another) which deal with public events.

So much for strictures: in most respects Mr Kyle's is an auspicious debut. Other directors have done interesting things with this play, but he is the first in my experience to get it right. The most famous lines in Measure are the ones about "proud man, dressed in a little brief authority" and the keyword is dressed.

When Angelo first comes before us he is a Cromwellian Puritan, all neat in black jerkin; promoted to Duke (Temporary Acting), he puts on the ducal robe of justice: a long white thing, decorated with red motifs that look like licking flames. It doesn't actually change his nature, but it brings dormant qualities to life. There seems, too, to be a strange kinship between this garment and the prison uniform -- short white tunic, also splashed with red -- worn by convicted fornicators like Claudio. Isabella arrives at her convent with a suitcase, and she carefully assumes her nun's habit before going out to encounter Angelo. In the circumstances his seducer's line ("If you are a woman show it now/by putting on the destined livery") could hardly be more fitting or, for her, more shocking. The Duke, disguised as a friar, surprises himself with how easily the right words come to him. Pompey, promoted to headsman (also Temporary Acting), assumes the full black gear. And at the end of the scale, Barnardine, the play's one natural man, he who refuses to die even if it means upsetting the plots of both his Duke and his author, appears stark naked.

For most of the characters the play is a voyage of self-discovery; an unsentimental education ending, like its lighter counterpart As You Like It, in a quartet of marriages. Michael Pennington's Duke visibly improvises his schemes; neither God nor the devil (both approaches have been tried), but a witty juvenile lead whose urbanity can terrify both the Puritan Angelo and the libertine Lucio, but also enables him to forgive them.

Jonathan Pryce's Angelo is as much victim as villain: horribly shy, a theorist (probably with a first in law) wrecked by the complications of actual practice. Paola Dionisotti's Isabella is his female equivalent, as much her brother's judge as his advocate. She giggles when she tells of her mock-assignation with Angelo; it's the first time she's had to talk of such things and she seems to have stumbled on a new world.

Both John Nettles's Lucio and Richard Griffiths's Pompey are excellently communicative. Mr Griffiths is one of your deliberate fops. His pimp shines with quiet professional pride.

* * *

At the Other Place, a new playwright, Peter Whelan, gives us Captain Swing, he being a populist leader who terrified the landowners of Britain in 1830. He may or may not have existed -- and he may or may not appear in the play. A wandering wounded man is assumed by some aggrieved Sussex villagers to be the very Swing, but is never collected enough to confirm or deny it. It suits both sides to believe that he is; the people can use him as a symbol, the gentry can catch and kill him.

The odd man out is the village wheelwright, who believes in peaceful protest. "The people keep the law -- those who govern break it" is his credo, uttered at first with pride and finally -- when the troops have been sent in and he has been sent to the hulks -- with anguish. Negotiation is fine, but it don't mean a thing if it ain't got that Swing.

On this evidence, the play seems an orthodox plea for violent change. In practice it comes over differently, since so much sympathy adheres to the gradualist (especially as played by David Bradley) and so much contempt to the extremists: an Irish infiltrator who escapes with a whole skin and the village whore who discovers the crypto-Captain and whose possessiveness (she will allow no doctor near him) effectively kills him. Only the Dragoons commander (David Lyon) matches the wheelwright, since he too is allowed the benefit of his own doubts.

In setting and atmosphere the play recalls Serjeant Musgrave's Dance and it is shot through with a similar promise: verbal -- as where Swing speaks of an army raised "from under the fields of King Corn's kingdom" -- and theatrical. There is a powerful, if structurally confusing, scene in which the villagers beard the parson in his church, cut Christ down from the cross and adopt the figure as their own. Bill Alexander's production, played all over the tiny theatre, enfolds the audience; it has wrap-around excitement.
