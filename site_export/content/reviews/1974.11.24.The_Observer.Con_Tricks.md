ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'The Marquis' and 'The Beast.'

ANY heavy theatre company is liable to expend much of its energies on the caprices of inordinate ambition. (I am referring, of course, to the characters we see on the stage, not to those we may imagine behind it.) This week from the Royal Shakespeare, the people who gave you--are still giving you--the fortunes of Faustus and the machinations of Macbeth--comes a further flurry of malevolent mountebanks.

At the Aldwych we are introduced to The Marquis Keith, a character imagined, written, and originally played by Frank Wedekind, and the hero of a play which here justifies its reputation as its author's best. Keith is an adventurer, floating in from nowhere and surfacing in Munich at the turn of the century, dispensing money in order to attract it. He distributes largesse to a horde of hangers-on, while touting for support for the Magidome, a project which appears to be nothing more nor less than an arts complex.

In view of this last item, Ronald Eyre, director and co-translator, is to be congratulated on his restraint in not taking the play and shoving it bodily into the 1970s. He has, however, while still maintaining in the programme that we are in 1899, prodded it slyly forwards as far as the twenties. With Carl Davis's estringent clarinet-haunted music besieging our ears, we may even ignore all references to Munich and dream ourselves into the Berlin of everybody's favourite decadence. The vast grotesques of German society, swathed in furs, move mountainous around the thin contorted figure of the Marquis himself. Clearly Mr Eyre owes a great deal to his designer, Voytek; but he must himself take credit for the swift, chill exuberance of the evening, the best of the various good things that have been seen at the Aldwych this year, and rich in off-handed wit.

Sara Kestelman has a new comic assurance (and a new feathery voice) as Anna, Keith's mistress, destined like the wife of Citizen Kane for stardom in his custom-built opera-house and dreading it. ("What happens," she asks, "when I, for want of a better word, sing?") A lower-grade whore is played with appealing scornful relish by Patti Love, who has become, at two strokes, our most experienced Wedekind actress: she played a similar role, less happily, in the National's Spring Awakening. Not all the girls have to have fun; Keith's, for want of a better word, wife is a wraith, knowing that she will possess him for only as long as he remains an outsider, killing herself when his success seems in sight.

This only shows how little she knows her man; Keith is destined always to glitter, never to arrive. He lacks solidity, above all he lacks solid cash (huge bank-notes are the binding symbol of this production); so, thinking to batten on polite society, he finds it battening on him, squeezing him dry, finally squeezing him out. The play is actually none too explicit as to what society sees in him in the first place; the details of his intrigues are left very murky. Perhaps Wedekind trusted to his own charm to carry it (certainly a Keithian thing to do); we have Brecht's testimony that he was an actor of limited accomplishment and amazing charisma.

Ian McKellen, who plays the part now, is an actor of outstanding accomplishment and, by common consent even of his detractors, as charismatic as they come. But he is not, in the traditional sense, a charmer; as a new kind of star actor he compels attention by insistently deglamourising his roles. Keith is a cripple; Mr McKellen bends and protrudes at incredible angles, outpacing Olivier's Richard III in deformity. And here reverberations begin. Reminded of Richard III one thinks, in this Brechtian setting, of Hitler; would that be a Chaplin moustache on Mr McKellen's upper lip? Suddenly the idea of a con-man at large in twenties Germany loses its appeal.

The play, I think, loses too; we do not have to like Keith, but we should be carried along with him--we should feel his defeat. Mr McKellen distances us altogether too much. Our sympathy goes instead to Ian Richardson, who plays his doppelganger Scholz, as encased in morality as Keith is indifferent to it, trying desperately to break out and enjoy something called Life, finally and frighteningly retreating into madness. Mr Richardson offers a delicious comic performance, in the English tradition that repression is endearing. If Mr McKellen is Chaplin, he is Keaton, never cracking a smile as he contemplates the delights of an early morning bicycle ride or propositions Anna when his every instinct is to propose to her.

The balance of these two Ians is wrong, though something asserts itself in their final encounter where Mr Richardson, buttoned up tight in an immaculate pearl-grey suit, confronts Mr McKellen in a golden-brown robe that once was glamorous and now is just dishevelled; and their costumes tell everything about them.

* * *

Insouciance is much, indeed very nearly all, in the theatre; as Mr McKellen demonstrates at the final curtain. Snoo Wilson's The Beast (the RSC at The Place) is heavy with its absence, though there is enough zip in it to make one wonder what it would be like in a less portentous production. Its subject--the week's second would-be superman--is Aleister Crowley, styled "the wickedest man in the world." Why did Mr Wilson write about him? Perhaps merely because he was there; for a long time. His career as magician, diabolist and spiritual leader is ingeniously jumped through, its roots perversely and persuasively discovered in his fundamentalist background and its flowering verbosely documented in a long inverted pastoral during which, if you doze, you do so to the soothing accompaniment of high-quality writing. The effects of magic may be theatrical and its renunciation (cf. Prospero) certainly is, but its processes--here endlessly discussed--are a bore. In his downfall, which is more or less continuous, Crowley is hauntingly portrayed by Richard Pasco; and Rosemary McHale, for the first time since joining the company, does justice to her own talent as his bitterly decaying mistress.

* * *

The Young Vic, having revived several plays acknowledged to be modern classics, has discovered one of its own in John Antrobus's wartime comedy Crete and Sergeant Pepper. Denise Coffey's production is the best at this house in ages.
