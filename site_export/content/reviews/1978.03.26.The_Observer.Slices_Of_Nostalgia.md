Slices of nostalgia

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on some 'showbiz nonsense.'

IN A backward-looking week the most palatable nostalgia was served up by Ginger Rogers at the Palladium. She starts of course with a huge advantage, having an unusually large slice of the past at her command. I mean, as they say, no disrespect; Miss Rogers makes no secret of her years, and besides, better preserved Ginge you could hardly hope to find.

It is still unusual to find a director credited for a variety act but Miss Rogers lists one, and with reason. Onna White's staging is a masterpiece of tact and timing. We begin with a few clips from the Rogers-Astaire movies. As the screen is whisked away a voice, surprisingly strong, starts up in the wings on the verse of "Night and Day" and you suspect that everything is going to be all right.

What follows is illustrated, and selective, autobiography. It is not without its irritations. The narrative is glutinously written and archly delivered; you wait for some hint that the lady is sending herself up but there is never a crinkle. Miss Rogers bounds from sentence to sentence, from name to famous name, and indeed from side to side of the stage; she is very enthusiastic about her own career and the enthusiasm becomes contagious. She speaks of herself, with wistful compassion, as a frightened teenager about to play her first major role on Broadway: 1930, this was. The show only happened to be Gershwin's "Girl Crazy," and her two solo numbers were merely "Embraceable You" and "But Not for Me." She sings them, again very well. (She curiously fails to mention another newcomer in that cast who got an even bigger hand and whose name was Ethel Merman.)

So to Hollywood and the Busby Berkeley era: "Forty-Second Street" and "Gold Diggers of 1933" (the best) and producers making unscheduled appearances in rehearsal rooms ("Why, come in ... oh, Mr Zanuck") and forcing our heroine to go right ahead and sing "We're in the Money" in baby-talk. So much history. We are dazzled. And she hasn't even started with Astaire yet.

When she does get to the great partnership she dwells with suitable emphasis on the composers and lyricists involved. She goes into a Kern and Gershwin selection (Irving Berlin is reserved for special treatment later) and something surprising happened. I know these songs, really, too well; and I had expected to do no more than nod approvingly as each one washed over me. But I actually listened. Her authority is complete; for the first time I could accept her as, in her own right, a star.

The screen is lowered again for extracts from "Kitty Foyle" and "Roxie Hart," ostensibly so that we may appreciate Miss Rogers's extra-musical range, but really so that she can change. Only then does she, to any extent, dance. Her gowns actually prevent us from seeing, with any clarity, what she is doing with her feet but she can certainly glide. Though she likes to refer in tones of hushed mischief to the gamine she once was, her stage persona now is all great lady: the hardness of edge, and the craziness, that were so valuable in the flicks have gone. But the whole act is beautifully integrated.

Donald O'Connor, who precedes her, seems unsure of what the justification for his appearance is supposed to be. This is sad, for on the evidence of his films I would have supposed him to be, in strict terms, the more talented of the two. But he lacks the one great advantage which Miss Rogers exploits; he is not a legend. Just once, perhaps--doing "Make 'Em Laugh" in "Singin' in the Rain"--he attained greatness, but that routine could scarcely be reproduced on a stage, even were Mr O'Connor 25 years younger. (He used to be as dapper as Danny Kaye; he's now as fleshy as Mickey Rooney.) And then he was always stuck as hero's best friend, whether the hero was Gene Kelly or Francis the Talking Mule.

Anyway, he has to work, not just be. Where Miss Rogers cannily pretends to be breathless, he gets breathless in grisly earnest. I suspect that he could still structure an act around his singing, which was always better than that of most hoofers, but the structuring had better be done quickly.

* * *

Kismet (Shaftesbury) is generally described by both friends and foes as a great slice of Turkish Delight. All then depends on your taste-buds; it is not my favourite brand of candy but there is nothing wrong with the preparation, which is baubly and bangly though not particularly beady; I imagine that the original leading man, Alfred Drake, was a more determined charlatan-beggar-poet than John Reardon, who plays the role here. But his singing is good and round, as is that of Lorna Dallas as his daughter and Joan Diener as an irresistible (and unresisted) temptress.

There is lively choreography. Dervishing is apparently a less specialised trade than I had supposed; anyway, most of the locals are good for a whirl. The composer, as is well known, was Borodin, who didn't know what he was doing at the time; he is unlikely to write another musical but if he does I should like to hear more of his rhythm numbers and less of his ballads, "Not Since Nineveh" being far more congenial to my ear than "Stranger in Paradise."

* * *

At the Roundhouse a Dutch group, Hauser Orkater, have brought something bewilderingly entitled The Hunch. I may be paranoid but my own hunch is that the show is satirising precisely the kind of showbiz nonsense to which I have devoted so much space this week. Otherwise I wouldn't care to explain it. It contains inconsequential poems considerably delivered in English (the accent makes them sound Brooklynese), a striptease by a female impersonator alarmed to discover unwanted body-hair, and a great deal of loud, vengeful rock.

Nine of the ten actors get together at the end for a deafening drum ensemble; I remembered how Duke Ellington used urbanely to remark, after percussion solos, that "a drum was a woman" and wondered if this had any bearing on the tendency of Hauser Orkater to keep their womenfolk in what the programme refers to as "the blue object" and which actually looks like a jerry-built washing-machine with the occasional limb protruding from its port-hole. It contrasts pleasingly with the stage-floor which is made up of bright red slats. Recommended to those who like primary colours, aural and visual; others will fidget.
