Merchant of Folly

HERE we are with 'The Merchant of Venice' as a Mafia musical. 'Fire Angel' (Her Majesty's) is set in New York's Venice Bar, constructed, like so many locales in expensive British musicals, out of garish tinfoil. There is a balustrade for the Heroine to perch on, and stairs descending from it just in case (why, hello Dolly) anyone feels the urge to descend and sing a show-stopper or two.

As it turns out, the only performer to make much use of them is Gaye Brown, who plays a kind of mistress of ceremonies. Miss Brown is well known to be a trouper (she has survived 'Mardi Gras' and worse), but nobody should have to troup this much.

The show began, apparently, on the Edinburgh Festival fringe in 1974 and it is possible that, then and there, it was a pleasantly campy little frolic. It may even have been funny, though in its present form it is more humourless than most rock shows. Imagine.

Paul Bentley is credited with the book and lyrics, though where the book-writing came in I am unsure since virtually every word is sung. Mr Bentley has also written a programme-note in which he blames Ray Cooney, the impresario. He thinks, apparently, that he is thanking Mr Cooney, but his unconscious has betrayed him: It was, he makes clear, the Management -- the Mgt as they call it in 'Illuminatus!' -- which blew the show so hideously up, flattering, magnifying and (of course) amplifying its youthful self-importance to an unbearable degree.

It was the Mgt, even, who suggested updating the story, thereby opening a can of many worms. For though there is a certain logic to the Mafia setting (where else today could pounds of flesh be considered common currency?), it involves the authors in a world of which, quite plainly, they know nothing. It also involves the cast in pretending to be Americans which, in a musical, is cruel. And it compels Shylock to ask whether or not a Jew hath eyes right in the middle of New York, a city which reached a favourable consensus on the subject some time ago. (This being a moment of stress, Shakespeare's words are employed. Let us be fair; he even gets billing, probably for the first time in all his years of work on musicals.)

Shylock, here called Barach, expresses some guilt at having betrayed his brethren. This could explain why he appears at one point devoutly clad in a prayer-shawl, but minus a cap. C. T. Wilkinson brings some guts to the role and profits from the best moment in Roger Haines's score, a falsetto cry of 'I am a vulture.' There is little else of musical characterisation; the fatal flaw of all rock musicals. Shakespeare's Portia had wit and good manners; her equivalent here starts bawling soulfully at us ('My Body's Weary') before we have even been properly introduced. She was only a godfather's daughter, but she knew about mercy and rain.

Her lover, rather neatly, pawns his own body instead of a friend's to raise the million bucks necessary to enter the casket contest. At the end boy and girl go off wearing musical haloes; since he has been a gangster like any other this seems a bit sudden. The implications of the story and the milieu have not, if I may understate, been thought through. Lorenzo and Jessica are also present, beseeching the 'shining moon, bright madonna' to help us find the wonder-world of love. If they haven't found it already, why are they eloping? What kind of musical is this anyway?

The answer, unfortunately, is that it is typical: a meeting of musical amateurishness and commercial know-how that doesn't. It is woefully important, too, since Mr Cooney controls five London theatres, and since this folly will probably lose enough money to ruin the chances of future investment even in good musicals. Especially in good musicals.

* * *

This has not been Shakespeare's week. At the Olivier, John Schlesinger has directed a Julius Caesar full of bright ideas on unimportant matters. A mysterious couple of lines in the murder scene for a senator called Popilius Lena are definitively explained. The murder itself, however, is not. John Gielgud as Caesar has his moments of public grandiloquence and private petulance, but it is not enough to deposit an actor of his rank in a young company and hope the contrast will do its own work.

Caesar is more a director's problem than an actor's and this production gives no idea of what kind of ruler he was or might have become. So the conspiracy seems pointless and so, by logical process, do the conspirators. Bits of Brutus and Cassius can be seen floating by, with the actors, Brian Cox and Ronald Pickup, making frantic efforts to jump on board. One feels sorry for Mr Cox, who acts sympathetically and with more technical command than ever before, and enraged for Mr Pickup, who should have been ideal for Cassius and who has to begin the evening by wondering what to do while Caesar speaks in praise of fatness right to his famished face. (There is a theoretical defence even of this idea, but no practical vindication on the stage.)

Mark McManus as Antony does come through, a little surprisingly since he has a shrill and unprepossessing voice, which wrecks him in his private grief over Caesar's body. His public grief is another thing and he manages it by sheer cunning; the shifting emphases on 'honourable men' are very clever. It helps, too, that he is acting in a real amphitheatre. Otherwise there is a warped amusing Casca from Gawn Grainger; murky sets and costumes that may not be fatal but are certainly depressing, and an all-pervading lack of impetus.

* * *

The most respectable offering of the week was probably Hugh Whitemore's Stevie (Vaudeville), but I cannot get very enthusiastic about a play which only attains to real life when its heroine starts reciting her own poetry. Since she is Stevie Smith she has the right, but the bulk of the evening is heavy and monotonous going; it concerns Stevie's relationship with 'the lion aunt' (Mona Washbourne, most comfortable of actresses) with whom she spent most of her life. So that we will not think that we are watching just another box-set play, a corner of the stage is sawn off and left for the unfortunate Peter Eyre to stand in. He narrates, and recites such verse as the heroine herself cannot manage. From time to time he enters the action as a collective Friend; he has the best scene, complaining of the poetess's assumption that every car-owning acquaintance was ipso facto her private chauffeur. Glenda Jackson in the title role is steely and remarkably sustained.

On the whole I shall vote for the Actors' Company, though they have lost most of their identity and their chosen vehicle, Pinero's The Amazons (Wimbledon), is a nonsense, as may be gleaned from its sub-title 'A Romantic Farce.' Of course it ends up neither, and its subject--three girls brought up to be boys and regretting it when suitors arrive--had better not be considered seriously. But it gets a bright production from John David, charming acting from Cheryl Campbell, and from Simon Cadell, as an insecurely Anglicised Frenchman, rather a tour of force.
