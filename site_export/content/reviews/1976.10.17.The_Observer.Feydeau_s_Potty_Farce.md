Feydeau's potty farce

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

I APPROACHED The Frontiers of Farce (Old Vic) as one approaches frontiers generally, with trepidation. The title conceals a double-bill and a programme note. On the bill are a Feydeau and a Wedekind; they have been adapted and directed by Peter Barnes, and the note is by Barnes as well. Its tone is earnestly pugnacious, its import that farce, in the hands of a few masters, may be an instrument for scourging the bourgeoisie--or, failing their availability, the theatre audience.

In his own plays, The Ruling Class and The Bewitched, Mr Barnes has tried to use it for such an end and has tried too hard. But he has performed valuable ambassadorial work on behalf of the dead. His adaptation of Jonson's The Devil Is an Ass (now at Birmingham) is one example; his current offering is two more. Or at least, one and a half more.

The Purging is among the one-act pieces which rescued Feydeau's career. To my surprise, it justifies the claims Mr Barnes makes for it; it is hilarious, which might be expected, and it is uncompromising, which might not. In fact it restores a faith in Feydeau which a succession of swirling, over-decorated productions of his full-length plays had virtually destroyed. Michael Annals's set looks as if someone had gone over the customary art-nouveau decor with paint-stripper, leaving only the accustomed drawing-room shape intact. The dramatic structure has been similarly streamlined, though at least one familiar device remains; thirsty or desperate characters grab the nearest fluid, which invariably turns out to be a particularly disgusting medicine.

In this case, it is a laxative; Follavoine fils is constipated and Follavoine pere finds his working-day disturbed by the demands of Follavoine mere that daddy should make baby take his physic. Since the job Follavoine has on hand involves the sale of a huge consignment of chamber-pots, it will be seen that the piece possesses an unquestionable unity of tone. The flight to the bedroom is replaced by the flight to the lavatory; the Palais Royal meets the excremental vision.

The two modes unite in the performance of Leonard Rossiter, which is uninhibited in style and totally controlled in technique. He is the mercantile spirit made flesh, consumed with avarice and corseted by respectability; he and his wife (Dilys Laye in curl-papers and a terrible temper) alternate the roles of bully and victim, in a subtler version of marriage than Feydeau usually gives us; they might be prototypes of Mr and Mrs Basil Fawlty, and Mr Rossiter's rage, like a supply of supposedly unbreakable porcelain smashed to smithereens in an impromptu scientific test, is in a class with John Cleese's: inspired vaudeville which never ceases to be drama.

* * *

He goes even more picturesquely berserk in the Wedekind piece, The Singer, as a composer in the finest musical frenzy since Jimmy Durante's on the day he found the lost chord. But with this piece Mr Barnes's enthusiasm has overreached itself; the Feydeau may stand on the frontiers of farce, but Wedekind is outside the territory altogether. One or two ladies are hidden in a hotel-room and there is an unexpected death, but the substance of the piece is not action but argument: the plea of an idolised operatic tenor that art in general and his in particular is overrated by the fans, nice though it is to have the adulation and the money.

The role might benefit from a more extravagant performance than it gets from the steady and sensible John Stride, but Wedekind hammers some obvious points and Mr Barnes, who can be as uncritical of his idols as any opera-fancier, seems more than ready to indulge him. This is a piece to be collected; its predecessor (in which John Phillips is a model of affronted dignity) is to be relished.

* * *

So is The Circle, which has come from Chichester to the Haymarket. It seems, behind that chocolate-box proscenium, more conventional than before; but Maugham's involvement with the society he criticises has its own satisfying irony. This is very much a social play; there is one character whose sole function is to make a fourth at bridge.

The acting is among the best in the West End, though both the leading ladies are a shade defensive; Googie Withers is emphatically aware of her laugh-lines and Susan Hampshire switches on her smile within two seconds of her entrance. The men are unassailable: Bill Fraser's chuntering reveals all the regrets that folding flesh is heir to, Clive Francis is touchingly brash, John McCallum shows us a smugly witty raisonneur hoist with his own petard, and Martin Jarvis, bringing a beautiful style to insensitivity, is an apt son for him. Mr Jarvis plays Miss Hampshire's husband, and those who once wept at their failure to get together in The Forsyte Saga may now take heart. It would never have worked.

* * *

The Royal Shakespeare Company's detente season at the Aldwych has so far shown America (The Iceman Cometh, The Devil's Disciple) to be a more entertaining dramatic locale than Russia (The Zykovs, Ivanov). Terry Hands's production of Aleksi Arbuzov's Old World leaves the score unaltered, though it is a superior piece of staging, evoking the world of a sanatorium with great wrought-iron gates and looming models standing in for a supporting cast. These embellishments almost persuade you that something is going on, but the impression does not survive the interval.

There was an old lady from Riga; or at least an old lady from Moscow who came to an establishment in Riga and proceeded to entrap the director. Their relationship proceeds at unremarkable length; she recalls her past as a circus performer demoted to the box-office, he recalls his dead wife. At one point they dance the Charleston, and since they happen to be Peggy Ashcroft and Anthony Quayle and we are not accustomed to seeing them in such circumstances, they bring the house down.

This attack of the cutes outdoes anything in the much-scorned Same Time Next Year and the play does not offer nearly as many compensations; the sugar-content of the final scene exceeds anything on Broadway. There is nothing to quarrel with in the performances except the waste involved.

* * *

Far more worthwhile is Colin Bennett's Soon (Bush), an array of 20 or so scenes prophesying triumph or apocalypse, depending which side you are on. Which side the author is on is not clear; he appears very hung-up on hatred and even more so on mysticism. The base of the play is the successful attempts of some women prisoners to shake the walls by choral thinking. Characters are apt to express hostility not just to the present system but to all civilisation since the Greeks. A girl arguing against violence gets less sympathy than anyone else on stage, but it is notable that no-one ever gets round to countering her arguments.

At the heart of the play is one man's subversive attempt to throw money into Wimpy bars or fried chicken parlours (the mythology is confused) without taking any food in exchange. The wit of this goes beyond the general mood of starry-eyed desperation and Nick Edmett's wild-eyed exploitation of the audience's proximity makes his performance literally the most challenging I have seen.
