Cruel concert-party

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on "a terrifying theatrical experience."

I start from the assumption that any theatrical treatment of the Nazi murder of the Jews must be suspect. An Die Musik, presented by the newly reconstituted Pip Simmons Group at the ICA, is startlingly if unevenly conceived, brilliantly directed, acted with great skill and dedication, and hypnotically watchable. Apart from that, there is little to be said in its favour.

The framework is a concentration-camp concert, performed presumably to a mixed audience of prisoners and guards. An SS-man announces, curtly but politely, that the first offering will be an operetta. You could call it that.

There is no dialogue, and all the singing is done by the band, four wailing instrumentalists, whose words are generally drowned by the merciless percussive throbbing of their music. Meanwhile five actors mime the operetta's action. This, like the words, is hideous, but difficult to interpret precisely. A Jewish family meal is disrupted by the SS-man, whose truncheon, liberally applied, forces them into ever-weirder contortions. It is like watching a silent film with a screeching musical accompaniment; smiles get ghastlier, gestures jerkier, everything louder and faster. The situation is played for grotesquerie if not actually for laughs: the mother is a grinning female impersonator.

The operetta is entitled, for no obvious reason, "The Dream of Anne Frank." Presumably the real Anne, in hiding with her family in an Amsterdam garret before being discovered and sent to be killed in Belsen, often had nightmares like this; But I suspect that in using her name Pip Simmons is on to something else; the Anne Frank story is one of the few methods we have -- but a very effective one -- of sentimentalising the death-camps, and Mr Simmons will have none of it. The dream is succeeded by the reading of an extract from Anne's diary, the famous passage in which she declares her belief that, in spite of everything, people are good at heart. The production tries to show just what "everything" meant in her case and, by doing so, to disprove her.

The Anne Frank reading is the bridge-passage of An die Musik. The second part of the concert picks up from the first: one of the most dismaying moments of the operetta showed us the family forced into performing a high-kicking routine. This kind of humiliation is extended over the rest of the show; the actors and musicians are lined up and ordered sadistically through a series of exercises. There are blows and assorted other torments for those who weaken. Putting them through their paces is a second SS-guard, the first having changed costumes and identities with one of the prisoner-actors at the end of the operetta. What is meant by this transformation I cannot say; perhaps it is part of the treatment to put a prisoner into the jailer's uniform and force him to abuse his fellows, but this remains cloudy.

The commands change and the entertainment continues; the wretched musicians play Schubert, Liszt, Beethoven; two actors even more wretched are announced as "Jewish comics" and compelled to totter and wheeze through the remnants of music-hall routines. A girl playing the triangle is stripped; nothing in the evening is more piercing than the expression, desperately eager to please, on Sheila Burnett's face as her dress comes off. Before long all the others have joined her in nudity, but -- so it seems -- voluntarily and with dignity. The musicians, naked but serene, play a Beethoven dead march. Smoke fills the stage. Gas.

An Die Musik is unequivocally my most terrifying theatrical experience. It eats into the nerves; a shuffle in the seat behind and you wonder who is coming for you. There is no question here of welding the audience into a responsive unit; everybody who watches this is on his own. The ending is curiously soothing; maybe this is what catharsis means. Certainly there is enough pity and terror here (enough terror, anyway) to purge anybody's soul. Tragedy, as described in the books, is a much gentler discipline and less immediately effective.

But even while watching I rebelled. There were prisoners' orchestras in some of the camps -- a macabre decoration, if the decoration of death-camps interests you, but not a detail strong enough to bear the weight Mr Simmons puts upon it. He builds his whole conceit of a camp concert on it, and it shows the strain. Is the concert meant to absorb the whole action -- even the gassing? Is the metaphor meant to make us, as an audience, feel guilty? Where does that leave Mr Simmons, who carefully staged it for us?

There is a sense in which the camps were theatre -- to the guards who reduced the prisoners to objects in a spectacle, even to the prisoners who felt they were filling allotted roles. But that is an argument for shattering the image, not for perpetuating it. There is probably no legitimate way of dramatising this material; even novels on the subject end up between exploitative paper covers. The presence of live (or even filmed) actors emphasises the exploitation. It is degrading to watch actors being pummelled (some of them must really get hurt) while reflecting that millions went through far worse, far longer. And the scenes of explicit violence are actually less offensive than the beautifully choreographed bits -- those early high-kicks, for example, which suggest that the director has a real relish for dehumanisation.

"Lest we forget" is the usual excuse, but our memories could equally well be stirred by the recital of a few statistics. And in a few minutes, at most, of brutality make as strong a point as could be wished; to prolong it is to leave us with nothing to do except to admire the ingenuity of either the tormentors or the presenters. Besides, who needs reminding? Was it all news to the critic of Time Out, who called the show "essential stuff," or to those few of his readers who obeyed him and turned up on the second night? I suspect that the more people are told about this production, even about its merits, the less they will want to see it.

* * *

Stephen Poliakoff, the author of Heroes (Theatre Upstairs) is very young, very prolific, and very uneven. This is one of his lesser works, reordering some of the materials of Clever Soldiers and Hitting Town into this week's vision of apocalypse. He does not write well enough for the subject and his characters do not convince. Lines like "this is a mad and grotesquely lonely place" cry out to be swallowed, and David Dixon (playing Mr Poliakoff's perennial self-pitying dandy) answered the cry. Dressed in white, he was, on the first night, pallid, but Jonathan Pryce in the black corner loured fairly magnetically.
