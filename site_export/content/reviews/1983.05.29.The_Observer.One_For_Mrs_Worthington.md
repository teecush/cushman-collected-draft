One for Mrs Worthington

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Bugsy Malone'

THE self-willed senescence of the West End is frighteningly demonstrated by Bugsy Malone (Her Majesty's), which is a whole evening of freak-show. It is based on the Alan Parker film of 1976, a gangster musical with a cast of children. I can make no direct comparisons, since I stayed away from the movie; having no desire to see two of my favourite genres purged of sex and violence in the interests of cuteness.

The show is what I feared of the movie. It is actually billed as 'Bugsy Malone On Stage'; managements now are banking on the special thrill offered by letting you see again something you have already caught on a screen. There is a lot of this thinking around, and in the short term it may be commercially sound. In the long term it is suicidal.

The added gimmick of staging is, of course, subsidiary to the one carried over from the film. We have, again, a cast of kids: British kids, aged 10 to 14, playing American adults. They have plenty of enthusiasm, and some skill (especially in dancing), but hardly any discernible acting talent.

The show has a lot of dialogue: supposedly smart lines delivered by performers who can neither speak nor time them. The timing of lines is what gives a play tension, continuity and -- ultimately -- sense; and you never properly value it until you are deprived. I felt pauperised at Bugsy Malone, sapped of the ability even to follow the story.

It seemed a pointless one anyway. Bugsy Malone is a well-meaning slob who hangs around New York, being in love with a hopeful ingenue who wants to go to Hollywood and has a dream-sequence to prove it. He also befriends a budding prizefighter, but this theme, laboriously set up, then just disappears; it is merely an excuse for an energetic number in a gym.

There are two gang leaders, Fat Sam and Dandy Dan. We are meant to be on Sam's side, or at least Bugsy is, but there really seems nothing to choose between them. They fight with guns that spray custard-pies. This is very confusing: are the victims dead or not? If not, what kind of gangster-story is this?

The rationale is, of course, that it would be intolerable to see children actually shooting or actually shot; which seems a good reason for not casting them as gangsters. There is nothing satiric about this patisserie warfare; it is pure bowdlerisation. Elsewhere old jokes are used -- the hoods who repeat every word their boss says, for example -- which in an adult context could only make us groan or maybe rub our eyes. But children are trusted to make them funny by sheer ineptitude.

It could persuasively be claimed that this show exploits children, but it is more to the point that it exploits audiences. (It also, by omission, exploits adult actors. Is Equity pleased?) It is not even true to its own lousy convention: the kids do all the dancing and talking, but the singing is entrusted to half-a-dozen off-stage adults. The children, miming to their large ill-fitting voices, are reduced to marionettes.

The other grown-ups have worked rather well. Paul Williams has composed a competent bunch of songs. Michael Dolenz, surmounting what must have been fearful logistical problems, has directed spectacularly. Gillian Gregory has put her pubescent chorus line through some high-class low-down routines. (The one boy who makes an impression, Scott Sherrin, does it through hoofing.) Ralph Koltai's set is a three-decker wonder that should be kept in storage against the advent of a real show. Also working hard was a first-night audience of Mrs Worthingtons, augmented by some rejected but still hopeful little Worthingtons. They raised the roof.

* * *

Angelo Beolco was a sixteenth-century Italian actor and playwright credited with building bridges between the popular and academic theatre of his time. He was nicknamed 'Il Ruzzante,' meaning, apparently, the joker. Four of his jokes have been assembled by Shared Experience at the Lyric, Hammersmith, under the heading of The Comedy Without Title, which is what Beolco himself called the first and longest of them.

In it a Paduan peasant, Zilio, falls in love. He spends the first part of the play shyly confessing to his friends, and hearing their counsel. He then, with as much instructed bravado as he can muster, proposes; and is scornfully rejected. Only the intervention of his friend Nale wins him the girl; and the two men come to blows when Zilio realises that a menage a trois is in prospect.

He kills Nale; and we then -- for the first time -- meet the widow. She bewails her husband's prowess in the fields and in bed, swears she will never take another, and a couple of minutes later succumbs to an old admirer on the grounds that in this life we must take what we can get.

If the first, bucolic, part of the play moves slowly, this conclusion is farcically fast, the effect of the juxtaposition is to deliver us from time, to leave us with the sensation that we are merely watching the truth. It does not feel distorted, even if we know it must be. The humour -- and much of the seriousness -- is obsessively scatological; but this is offset by Mike Alfreds's production, by playing of remarkable fullness and delicacy.

'What won't you do when you're in love?' is the despairing question which runs through the shorter pieces that follow the interval. In the only one to have any previous track-record in English (under the title Ruzzante Returns from the Wars) a soldier is battered by his wife's new protector and tries to fantasise his defeat away.

In the next, a young man tries to rescue his beloved from a stock pantaloon, who disconcertingly turns out to have human feelings. These are beautifully expressed by Mr Voss, who also dominates the last play, in which hunger bites, deepest and imagination cruelest. The evening could be funnier (it lacks natural stand-up comics), but Beolco's 'gay and gentle' reputation is amply vindicated.

* * *

Another touring group, Cliffhanger, has touched down in this theatre's studio with They Came From Somewhere Else, which burlesques a science-fiction story that nobody shows signs of believing in the first place. This is the second unfunny spoof in succession at the Lyric Studio, which should make it popular with those people who like a theatre to have a recognisable policy.
