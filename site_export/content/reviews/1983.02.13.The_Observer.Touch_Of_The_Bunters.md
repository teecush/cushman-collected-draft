Touch of the Bunters

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a revival of 'Charley's Aunt,'

THOUGH he is no more than stocky, something about Griff Rhys Jones's performance of Lord Fancourt Babberley in Charley's Aunt (Lyric, Hammersmith) suggests the classic fat boy. He has a real Billy Bunter entrance, sneaking in to snaffle the other fellows' tuck -- or, this being undergraduate Oxford of the gilded days, champagne.

What keeps him going in his transvestite masquerade is vanity; one flourish of his fan, and one falsetto trill, and he realises he is very good at it. Thereafter he may grumble -- and he complains very Bunterishly about the ingratitude of the fellows for whom he is so spectacularly putting himself out -- but he perseveres, through a maze of curiosity, exhilaration and embarrassment.

At moments the surrealism of it all overcomes him; having poured tea into a gentleman's topper, he adds milk and sugar and stirs vigorously. The basic business is traditional, the additions not specially inventive, but the whole is carried by Mr Jones's frantically logical conviction; he really wants to be hung for a sheep. He is also rather touching when explaining the depths of his lovesickness. (And, what's more, he's lost his appetite.) His friends are sceptical, believing him to be a fool and love to be their own prerogative, so the play becomes a kind of revenge comedy. I, who have seen Frankie Howerd play Fanny and Babs, can hardly proclaim Mr Jones the funniest ever, but he is undoubtedly a good chap.

The production, by Peter James and Peter Wilson, is unusually solid. You may learn much from it about the petty snobberies that infected Oxford in the 1890s, if that's what takes your fancy. College gents habitually borrow money from their servant in order to tip him; David Ross is brazenly long-suffering about this, and about his sudden transformation to somebody else's butler in the last act; a symptom of both social and theatrical economy.

Brandon Thomas, who wrote the thing, took little trouble with subsidiary roles; Charley's aunt's nephew must be the most thankless character ever to insinuate himself into a title. (I mean the play's title; he doesn't become a duke.) But Mark Payton makes Jack, the prime mover, into a spoiled young intriguer, kicking the armchair in amorous frustration while declaring, 'I'm regularly nonplussed.' Mr Payton is an alert young actor who makes something of every line and plays fine period duets with bluff, broke dad (Terence Longdon) and antiphonally agreeable sweetheart (Anita Dobson).

* * *

A few weeks ago I turned the corner of Tottenham Court Road to see a coffin being loaded into the back of a small van. Even the undertakers, I decided, had been hit by the slump; but when I came up with the vehicle, it turned out to belong to Paines Plough, the touring theatre group. On Tuesday I tracked the hearse down to the Old Town Hall, Hemel Hempstead, where it played a leading role in Tony Marchant's Welcome Home.

Inside it is said to be a British soldier, killed in the Falklands. We meet five of his mates -- a corporal and four privates -- deputed to be official pall-bearers. The corporal is a hard man who says things like, 'Remember why we are here -- commit it to memory.'

Intentionally, accidentally or by happenstance the line-up recalls Serjeant Musgrave's Dance, and I started waiting for Mr Marchant to turn the tables: to reveal his characters as militant pacifists. One of them does momentarily go that way, brandishing the flag and asking what for. But it's a passing climax only; the play's instant political content is slight. The focus is not on the Falklands (Mr Marchant must trust us to make up our own minds about that), but on what makes men soldiers and what soldiering makes of them. The answer, much of the time, is bullying, verbal and physical; plus a kind of masochistic camaraderie that occasionally flowers into actual protective friendship.

Mr Marchant brings a fair amount of it off. The current white-hope playwright, he is the strongest example of a drift back to character. Our old young playwrights were either uninterested in people or contemptuous of them; they sounded dogmatic even when they weren't. That mould began to break with Stephen Poliakoff; and his juniors have cracked it open. What were thrilling ideas to the children of prosperity are ugly realities to those of the recession; and they are interested in their victims. This can result in sentimentality, but at least it starts in the right place.

In this play the more convincing the basic observation, the less are the proclamations. These characters do proclaim, usually in the wrong vocal register. When the corporal says, with sinister intent, 'We could become the nation's nurse,' he is having words put into his mouth. Even more unbelievably knowing is the weakest soldier's explanation of why pop music means so much to him. And the physical symptoms of his breakdown seem to have come out of a play.

You begin to see why naturalism is so frustrating a medium, but also why we have to keep coming back to it. Maybe a stylistic compromise is possible, but this production, though potentially well acted, has yet to find it. Nor has the play's argument been properly aligned with its action. The show visits York and Lancaster this week, and arrives at the Theatre Upstairs in March.

* * *

Secret Gardens (ICA) is sort of trancey. Composed of dance, music, bits of speech and fractions of song, it begins hypnotic, and ends tedious. It is based on the Edwardian children's classic of almost the same title and on the life of Nancy Cunard, these being taken as feminine poles of security and danger. I only know this from having read previews; none of it is mentioned in the programme or, to me, apparent in the show. (I haven't read the book, but so what?)

I suspect the obscurity is due less to arrogance than to absent-mindedness; they never dreamed we'd never know. All I got were some pleasing visuals (the verbals were very banal), the pleasure coming partly from the dance, but chiefly from the art (credited, like the direction, to Tim Albery, Antony McDonald, Geraldine Pilgrim and Ian Spink), a deep louvred chamber in which anything would look good.

At Pentameters, in Hampstead, there is by contrast a very basic staging of a fascinating text: Red Magic by Ghelderode (1898-1962, of Belgium). Mixing in the traditional characters of miser, misanthrope and alchemist, it builds up a marvellous nexus of sex, money and death: a man so covetous he even preserves his wife's virginity as a prize before losing to it a cozener who says he wants to get to Muscovy. (Haven't I heard something like that before?) The play goes way beyond practical joking, though, into real cruelty and obsession. Jeremy Blake acts best, though his range seems limited to various kinds of scorn, but this is a playwright who should be rescued from the devout, preferably by the National Theatre.

* * *

Caryl Churchill's Top Girls, reviewed here last year, are back at the Royal Court after success in New York; and unless something unfathomable has happened to them en route, they are the best play in London.
