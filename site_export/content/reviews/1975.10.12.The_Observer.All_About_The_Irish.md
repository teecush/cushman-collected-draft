All about the Irish

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN at the Dublin Festival.

THE critic who has been properly chastened by Trevor Griffiths's `Comedians' is likely to feel nervous for several weeks about his response to racial stereotypes: particularly, in view of Mr Griffiths's preoccupation, Irish racial stereotypes. The Dublin Theatre Festival, it turns out, is no place to escape from them; here, however, when the Irish stock is boiling over, the polite phrase to reach for is not exploitation, but national consciousness.

At the Gaiety, for example, Hugh Leonard offers three one-act plays collectively entitled Irishmen. Imagine that on the bill in any other capital. Had Joyce not got in first, it might have been `Dubliners'; all the plays take place in the city, indeed in a single street, in the course of a single evening. And if Barbra Streisand, or her songwriters, had not staked their claim, Mr Leonard might simply have settled for `People.' For a street, as Granada television has spent years instructing us, is a universal thing and Mr Leonard's is not, in its essence, hopelessly remote from other streets.

Mr Leonard's position in Dublin rivals, for popularity and productivity, Alan Ayckbourn's in London or Neil Simon's in New York, but technically his range is greater than either of them. A bit of pathos, a bit of melodrama, a bit of farce, make up a pleasing, teasing mixture; if you tickle us, do we not bleed?

The pathos is served first: a late night drunk returns home to find `the house talking to itself' and proceeds to follow its example. On the whole we are his beneficiaries, especially as the monologue is played by the stalwart Godfrey Quigley. An exquisitely appropriate name that: a portmanteau of quick and wriggly. He is here extremely quigly, executing elaborate diagonals across the debris of his life and his living-room, but never relaxing into self-indulgence. With the fun and the pain goes an unsentimental evaluation of the man's plight and his own responsibility for it. His wife has left him, and so may his job; one can see the reason for both predicaments without quite losing sympathy for the victim. The playlet is over-long, but it has the most consistent writing of the three.

The best individual flashes, however, come in the melodrama. A young bourgeois, identified by his possessions (`a car that's fast and a wife that looks fast'), falls foul of two embittered activists; he is hated, and he suffers, less as an enemy than as a blundering neutral. The early dialogue, with the situation still mysterious, is charged with solid dread: dangerous banter on the surface and deadly grudge beneath. The later passages are less original, recalling `The Birthday Party' as the aggressors turn interrogators in the antiphonal style that is supposed to denote uncompromising realism. (Actually I have never heard anyone -- or any two -- speak antiphonally in real life, and I doubt if it could be done without a great deal of practice, even in Ireland.) This play comes nearest of the three to an honest justification of the overall title. Its concerns, bleakly realised, are native and organic -- notably in Donal McCann's chilling performance of a gunman.

In the farce, local colour is pasted on. A husband gets the chance to deceive his wife because she has gone on a pilgrimage; when she makes the expected unexpected return she mistakes his girl friend for a leprechaun. That sort of thing. There is no very good reason for the wife's gullibility, but in order to get his machinery moving Mr Leonard needs to keep most of his characters stupid and this blunts his comedy. Farce is a struggle between a man's wits and a malevolently logical fate. Fate has it too easy here; despite all that a bustling Mr Quigley can do for the husband, it is too obvious that he hasn't got a prayer.

Mr McCann also makes a solicitous return as a fey friend of Mr Quigley. I have never seen Irish camp before, and I laughed a great deal. But again my conscience, new moulded by Griffiths, troubled me; for assuredly Mr McCann is playing a homosexual stereotype, and the offence is not lessened but increased by having him deny that he is gay before milking hell out of the assumption that he is. And a similar, larger duplicity informs the whole piece. If Irish puritanism were as rampant as the plot assumes, then Mr Leonard's play would not be put on.

* * *

Probably there are subtleties I missed; certainly I felt completely out of touch with Thomas Murphy's The Sanctuary Lamp at the Abbey. An English dramatist who took loss of religious faith for his subject would need to work at it; he could not assume an interested audience. Even in Dublin Mr Murphy has been too confident in the demands he makes. All his dramatic skill goes on setting up the situation -- on getting Harry, a down and out with an unstamped card, into a church as keeper of the eponymous flame and general janitor.

But turning Harry loose in the midst of religion does him no good nor Mr Murphy either; the symbols sap their will to act and tempt them to the making of interminable speeches. The man who ran off with Harry's wife turns up, gets into the pulpit and talks longer still; I took him to be a satirical anti-Christ ranged, probably with his author's approval, against the presence in the lamp. But I could not feel that Mr Murphy had activated the conflict, or that the Abbey production, offering turbulence without excitement (in other words dullness), helped any.

* * *

And if this, the Irish National Theatre, is disappointing, what can be said of the Gate, the temple of Hilton Edwards and Micheal Mac Liammoir? In the days of its glory its likeness to a village hall was presumably forgotten the moment the curtains parted. But the amateurishness of its festival offering is extraordinary. It is called The Real Charlotte, but it is strictly apple sauce.
