Poet on a hot tin roof

ROBERT CUSHMAN,
our Theatre Critic,
looks back on the life
and work of Tennessee
Williams, who died in
New York last week.

Tennessee Williams died at the age of 71. You have to go steadily back in time to find his landmarks.

1961: The Night of the Iguana, his last generally successful play. 1955: Cat on a Hot Tin Roof, the last to be greeted as the work of a modern master. 1945: A Streetcar Named Desire, his most famous play. 1944: The Glass Menagerie, his first hit. These last two the ones on which his reputation currently rests, the ones that get revived.

So for nearly 40 years his career went downhill: for the last 25, pretty steeply. And yet his reputation never really faded. He might be forgotten for lengthy periods but when remembered he was never discounted. He was ritually ranked with Arthur Miller as one of America's two best living playwrights, the two of them, with O'Neill, as the three best ever. Of their juniors, only Edward Albee looked briefly like a contender, and that was very brief.

Veneration of Williams was not just a patriotic American custom. For the 10 years after the war he and Miller were the dominant dramatists in the English language, the inspiration of our own new wave in the Fifties. They coincided with a great period in American acting and direction. Stanley Kowalski in Streetcar was Marlon Brando's last and greatest stage role; Elia Kazan directed both that play and Cat. Kazan's contribution is evident in the published scripts; they are immensely atmospheric plays, delighting in the stagecraft that summons up the Deep Williams South. The pre-war American playwrights had been gaunt realists, as Miller was. Williams was sensuous, a post-austerity dramatist. That was part of his early appeal in Britain.

Everything about him was picturesque. Born in Columbus, Mississippi, he spent his early years in genteel poverty in St Louis. College was interrupted by a Dickensian period in a shoe factory, culminating in a breakdown. Before his first success, he was a waiter in New Orleans and New York. That success was the autobiographical Glass Menagerie, in which the narrator, Tom (Tennessee's real name was Tom) recalls his dreambound mother, his shy, crippled sister, and the stolid gentleman caller who raises and then crushes their romantic hopes. The young heroine's cherished glass animals were part of Williams's and his sister's own past. They stand for the bright, vulnerable creatures who go through all his plays, subjected to increasingly harsh pressures.

The belle of them all, of course, is Blanche DuBois, the heroine of Streetcar, who comes to her sister's New Orleans apartment, echoing to the sounds of jazz from the streets, weakly but valiantly upholding the flag of art, reeling from marriage to a homosexual, toying with the men who come her way until Stanley, her brother-in-law, ups and rapes her.

When Streetcar was revived in London in 1974, it seemed as full of quotations as Hamlet: "It's Della Robbia blue," "Sometimes there's God so quickly," "I have always depended on the kindness of strangers" -- the last a wonderfully moving plea as Blanche surrenders herself to the men from the mental hospital. Williams had a feeling for degradation which at this time was wholly compassionate. It may be that Streetcar drained him of what he had to say; though he arranged it more ambitiously, or at least more allegorically, in Camino Real, a flawed fantasy that preaches idealism while revelling in the gutter: a Spanish-American gutter (Williams was always a Hispanophile) that must be an irresistible lure to directors.

Streetcar was first presented in London in 1949 by a non-profit company exempt from entertainment tax; there was much fuss about the taxpayer's money being used (or at least rebated) for immoral purposes. Ludicrous as that now seems, Williams's later plays began to look like parodies of the sex-obsessed dramas he had been accused of writing.

Cat on a Hot Tin Roof, powerful in its day, now reeks of Southern Gothic and also of a homosexual writer's self-conscious attempts to grapple dramatically with heterosexual themes. These became increasingly strident, just as the titles -- Orpheus Descending, Sweet Bird of Youth, The Seven Descents of Myrtle -- became increasingly flowery. A sort of sadism crept in, the cruelty being piled on in direct ratio to the helplessness of the victims. It got more than Jacobean; there was cannibalism in Suddenly Last Summer, a poet dismembered by dogs in Orpheus.

Williams had always had talents for melodrama and farce; some of his later work became unconsciously farcical as the melodrama jumped out of control. But there were still surprising spurts of genuine humour. The Night of the Iguana was a notably benign work, and Vieux Carre, his last to be seen in London, revisited the scene of Streetcar in rambling autumnal mood.

In person Williams was an unpredictable but enthusiastic laugher; I once sat in front of him when he almost brought a performance at the Royal Court (of someone else's play) to a roaring standstill. He was also a fully paid-up paranoid. Kenneth Tynan has left an affectionate portrait of Williams preparing to meet Ernest Hemingway ("they tell me that Mr Hemingway usually kicks people like me in the crotch") and William Goldman wrote of him passing from the particular ("the reviewers don't like me") to the general ("I was not a well-liked kid"). Working, he said, was his escape, and he never let up.

The RSC wanted to do Sweet Bird last year, but say they were gazumped by Elizabeth Taylor. Either way we should see it soon. It was once dismissed as over-heated but our harsh climate is surprisingly favourable to hot-house art. We could be in for a Williams renaissance.
