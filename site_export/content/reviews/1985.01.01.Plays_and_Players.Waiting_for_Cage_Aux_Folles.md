Waiting for Cage Aux Folles

There were far fewer musicals produced in London in 1984 than we have been led to believe. I counted seven major shows, plus a couple of marginal entries (semi-musicals, almost-musicals, maybe-musicals) at the National. Considering the number of theatres still left alive in the West End, that is not a very great number. There were very few significant items on the fringe. Let me dispose of the marginals first, though that seems an unkind term for Animal Farm, the best evening to be had on the South Bank in 1984 (appropriately) and the best thing Peter Hall has done in years. It did no more than put Orwell clearly and skilfully on stage, but the obvious technical difficulties provided built-in suspense, and the voice was one we needed to hear.

The show-trial scenes were as chilling an enactment of police-state justice as I remember. The musical element - Richard Peaslee's tunes and Adrian Mitchell's lyrics - was probably the least impressive thing about it, but the songs were still a needed lubricant, raising the tempo and temperature of the novel to that of the stage. Only once did the score go glaringly wrong: in the provision of a big song for Barrie Rutter as Napoleon, the porcine Stalin. It tried to explain the character by referring back to the early biography of his role-model. But Orwell here is not interested in psychology, and the show was elsewhere faithful enough to him for this one departure to Jar.

Music and lyrics too, were among the weaker points of Tom Stoppard's Rough Crossing. What do I mean, weaker? Isn't the whole thing weak? (The world 'abysmal' has been used in my hearing). No, it isn't. I am a sucker for Stoppard jokes and for the spirit that keeps him making them. They are a lot more use to the theatre than anybody's sermon: more worthwhile, for example, than The Real Thing, and the first act of Rough Crossing, with John Standing exhibiting his impeccable lounglizardry as a Hungarian Coward and the underrated Niall Buggy fluttering in hypochondriac attendance and Michael Kitchen's uncannily assured delivery plonking his words down in marvellous counterpoint to his wavering sea-legs (in dry dock, yet), is as cheerful an offering as London offers.

In Molnar's original some tongue-in-cheek fun is had with the mechanics of exposition; Mr Stoppard expands and improves it immensely. The toast, in which Mr Kitchen obligingly unloads all the details of the plot, is a masterpiece of cheek, in both writing and acting. The performance style, indeed, is far surer throughout than in this show's predecessor, On the Razzle. I even enjoyed the running gag that enables Mr Kitchen to drink every glass of the Pernod that he is supposed to be serving to Mr Standing. I confess that I could feel the plot and the point of the original drifting away as the jokes accumulated, to be finally lost from sight in the second act.

Where this really founders, though, is on Mr Stoppard's attempt to burlesque the impossibly tangled plot of an inter-war musical. I am not convinced that this is a form he knows very much about; and anyway he falls foul of basic audience psychology. Given a story, any audience will try to follow it. Mr Stoppard is usually kinder to his public; and his good nature is the main attraction of Rough Crossing. It even works musically in the first-half closer 'Where Do We Go From Here' (based, presumably, on the Gershwin' 'Things Are Looking Up').

The finale, though, looks like the nadir of the subsidised companies' attempts to cash in on the musical: a desperate attempt to send us out happy, presuming on a goodwill that has long since dissipated. Probably the most genuinely musical aspect of the entertainment is Carl Toms's ocean-liner set: it should be held in storage, against a National revival of Anything Goes. I suppose the polar opposite of Rough Crossing, which would do anything for a joke, was The Hired Man which disdained them (eschewed them, as Bette Midler used triumphantly to pronounce it) altogether. With the possible exception of Man of La Mancha it was the most humourless musical in memory; and I don't quite see what it had to be so miserable about.

(Poverty? Mining disasters? The First World War? A load of laughs. Everyone knows that.) Altogether to its credit was Howard Goodall's music. Rich and rousing, owing nothing to Broadway and little to pop, it affirmed that a genuinely British musical might after all be a possibility. But Mr Goodall's lyrics were banal in the love-passages and awkward throughout (an especial attraction of the choral numbers was that the music tended to drown out the words) and Melvyn Bragg's book, based on Melvyn Bragg's book, as chaotic as his script for Mardi Gras a few years back. Its opening number promised, at some length, the story of an agricultural labourer who then spent most of his time down the pit or in the trenches.

Its indeterminate love story had nothing to do with its social history, beyond the fact that they both happened to the same people. The war introduced for chronological rather than dramatic reasons; it happens because it happened. It seems, too, typical of the show's high-mindedness that the musically impressive war-number was faded out, giving a frustrated audience no chance to applaud. Nor was the constant use of a split-level set very happy; more than half the action (including all the best numbers) got pushed into the middle distance. What was most infuriating was the failure to individualise the characters: a matter partly of acting (only Julia Hill as the heroine really communicated) but mostly of writing.

The show obviously felt deeply for them, but it gave us no chance to share its emotion. Just what constitutes a revival in 1984 is difficult to estimate. New shows are steeped in nostalgia, and where exactly do you place 42nd Street? Perhaps this is why The Boy Friend fell flat; we do not, at this moment, need a meticulous pastiche of the 20s seen through the eyes of the 50s; it hardly makes a change. That, at any rate is my theory, even though Sandy Wilson's score remains indestructible. It needed to be, in this heavily camped production, one totally lacking in the essential innocence. To compound it all, Anna Quayle was off sick at the performance I saw.

This was frustrating; having often seen Miss Quayle salvage bad material I was eager to see how she would cope with good. On Your Toes was a more respectable enterprise but not a really satisfying one. Rodgers and Hart wrote it a great score, or a least a great collection of songs; most of them (even such a triumph of bittersweet as 'Glad to Be Unhappy') go for nothing theatrically because they are not keyed into the book, an insubstantial but also plodding affair that lacks even the fizzing silliness of an Oh Kay or Whoopee. (And why, I want to know, does the villain who plans a murder get off scot-free — even marrying the leading lady at the end, while the gangster he hires is dragged off to gaol?) What remains is the dancing, and - in London - the performance of Natalia Makarova and Tim Flavin.

The first balletspoof, 'The Princess Zenobia', is a damp squib; Slaughter on Tenth Avenue' still has impact. The title number - the real heart of the show and a knock-out on Broadway with its sustained friendly battle between ballet and tap - just refuses in London to take off. This may be because all the best dancers had been nabbed by 42nd Street. The tapping here really is exciting, and Clare Leach in the Ruby Keeler role - the aspirant showgirl - gives the piece a heart it never had in New York. This it needs, for this, unlike The Boy Friend that JANUARY 1985 Sandy Wilson actually wrote, is calculated camp: plundering the old conventions but showing no real affection of understanding for them.

I invite you to consider 'Lullaby of Broadway', here borrowed from Gold Diggers of 1935 (which doesn't matter) to provide the key number. To begin with, James Laurenson, snappily though he acts the role of the director, cannot really sing it (and the part exists for that song). After him, the whole cast arrive on the stage, begging Miss Leach to come back to the theatre and save the show. They've chosen an odd song to do it with. 'Lullaby of Broadway' is about New York night-life: the hookers, the sugar-daddies, the daffydills who entertain at Angelo's and Maxie's... the one aspect of Broadway it does not mention is the theatre. The show obviously believes (and, sadly, it may well be right) that nobody listens to lyrics; we are expected to hear, and respond to, the word 'Broadway' and forget about context.

Nor are we really meant to share the emotion; we are supposed to regard it as absurd: as so much generalised razz-ma-tazz that we cannot believe in but may still think adorably ridiculous. "Think' bellows the director of musical comedy, the two most beautiful words in the language'. Nobody who really cared about musicals (which entails keeping them in perspective) would ever say, think or believe such a thing. And you state the case for the musical by doing it, not by orating over it. You can sit at Drury Lane and sense the trouble that 42nd Street itself must have been in out of town. The film, within its conventions, is a rather realistic piece about the tensions of doing a show; all this has gone out the window in favour of more and more numbers.

Some of them, especially at the start, are stunning, but they end up as overkill; the title-number, the one we've all been waiting for, is a particular disappointment. And yet... there is more fun in 42nd Street than in most current shows, and Margaret Courtenay's performance, bulldozing laughs out of a recalcitrant script, is something Broadway never had. For me the two best revivals were Little Me - brilliant score, funny book, all right production - and West Side Story: a treat to see Jerome Robbins's dances back in stage convention and stage scale; the realism of film murdered them. Some of the book may be forced, some of the score overblown, but it still gets you by the throat.

A first-rate Tony and Maria, though Anita and Riff went for nothing very much. Which leaves Starlight Express. I was away when it opened, and if it were not for this article I would probably still not have caught up with it. I had somehow got the impression that cast-changes would be immaterial: that technology was all and personality nothing. I had been lied to. Jeff Shankley, Lon Satton, and Michael Stanniforth are all terrific, and the energy-level, nine months into the run, would not disgrace New York. As far as I can gather the show has been attacked as whimsical, sentimental, relying on spectacle at the expense of story and character - by critics who bypassed exactly the same flaws in Cats.

It does perhaps repeat the Cats structure too exactly for comfort, right up (or down) to the heavenly intervention in favour of the most despised member of the pack. It is also true that the warmth, the sheer likeableness, of Trevor Nunn's direction, (that reached its apogee in Nicholas Nickleby), may be softening into a formula, to be sprayed on like Showbiz Warmth. But the anthropomorphism is more successful here than in Cats. The real weakness there was in Eliot's verse. He hardly bothered to make his practical pussies feline; he drew human types, artitrarily fitted out with fur and whiskers, and there is something self-defeating about putting actors into cat-gear and catmovements to impersonate them.

At least the trains in Starlight Express are described as such, and put through a story that has something to do with locomotion. Richard Stilgoe's lyrics are neat, sometimes even funny; he is only defeated by Andrew Lloyd Webber's customary refusal to allow dialogue. It may make him feel operatic, but it inolves his lyricists in writing awful doggerel in the name of recitative. The music has plenty of drive and - especially in its blues and boogie moods — more conviction than any Webber score since you-name-it. The intended big ballad is as watery as ever but at least it has had the grace not to become a hit. The unequivocal failure is the racesequences; repetitive, difficult to follow, and has anybody known a theatrical production to make successful use of video screens?

It is a cop-out: simply not what you go to the theatre for. But the adventure playground aspect is fun, and John Napier's costumes an all-out triumph: gold not only in themselves but in the whirling contribution that they make, especially when deployed en masse, to Arlene Phillips's choreography. The predictability of the story - you know who will win and who will get whom from the outset - drags the show down rather. As a fulfilling experience it hardly ranks with Guys and Dolls or Gypsy or Carousel (bravely and movingly revived at the Royal Exchange) or, in better days, The Boy Friend. But there is juice in it: something to keep us warm while we ready ourselves for La Cage Aux Folles and wonder when or whether someone will take a chance on the better shows in the recent Broadway crop: Baby or Sunday in the Park with George or — most stylish of the nostalgia trips — My One and Only.

And God knows, if skating's your fancy it's better than The Rink.
