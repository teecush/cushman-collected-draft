Fighting off sharks

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

JOHN McGRATH's Fish in the Sea--presented by the 7:84 company at the Half Moon, Aldgate East, prior to a national tour, its first stop Lancaster--is an unclassifiable piece. It suggests a disturbing novel brought to the stage in the style of a dramatised documentary.

Like most stage documentaries it is a kind of musical revue. It is traditional enough in form: its first half has a title song, and for this number to be the highlight of the evening. It is a mixture of defiance and foreboding. The title, the feelings of a father and son-in-law, both involved in the workers' occupation of a Liverpool engineering factory; the foreboding in the three girls of the family. All of them are fish in the sea; the point at issue is whether they will manage to escape or defeat the sharks.

Song is a great crystalliser. It can concentrate meaning without debasing it, and the musical and dramatic impact of this one is tremendous. For it can also produce dangerous simplifications. Mr McGrath's convictions are militant but he is sceptical about the chances of being realised. The title-song expresses this division, but theatres and audiences being what they are, it is the optimistic rhythms that are uppermost in us at the interval.

When we return there is less music. The first half has contained some buoyant rock numbers which the band can carry even when the actors falter. In the second half Mr McGrath makes greater use of traditional tunes, Marching Through Georgia to bellow distrust of bosses and Two Lovely Black Eyes to cartoon a trio of Labour MPs. The songs are fun, but they are also a bit perfunctory. The heart of the play is now elsewhere.

The Labour delegation are the only caricatured figures in the piece; otherwise Mr McGrath solves the political playwright's eternal problem of how to treat his adversaries simply by excluding them. The real tensions are within the family, one of the three sisters marries a shop-floor activist, another is courted by a Marxist intellectual and emotionally besieged by an Ulster Protestant gunman. In this last character are concentrated reserves of bitterness that have less to do with sectarian struggles than with Mr McGrath's feelings over the wastage of working-class anger. Andy, the gunman, is one character who never sings.

He is Scots, not Irish, in origin; we are on the same ground with him as in Hector MacMillan's The Sash. That was a small, funny naturalistic play that ruptured itself whenever it reached out. This is a multi-layered chronicle, funnier still and almost an epic.

The qualification is necessary mainly because of the size of the Half Moon. Pam Brighton's production and Bill Dudley's designs are endlessly inventive at moving the action and the scene-changes along, but they have not been able to provide the framing devices that might make us glory in the form rather than merely accept it. As it is there are more strands of action than can be comfortably encompassed and at least one character--an atheistic, sentimental Welshman--whose inclusion seems, to put it benevolently, over-generous.

The play lasts three and a quarter hours and by its close acting, production and text were beginning to show signs of wear; only the audience still looked invigorated. Most of the performances are excellent: my favourite, for pace and geniality, is John Judd, an open-faced comedian with barbed wire on his tongue.

7:84 is a company whose minor technical lapses can be forgiven: it is not selling slickness, but it does not try to pass off muffed moves or mistimed lines as part of its charm.

* * *

This is the state to which, farther east at the Theatre Royal, Stratford, Theatre Workshop has now been reduced. Superficially Maxwell Shaw's production of Labiche's An Italian Straw Hat is smoother than some of its recent shows. But even its passing facilities are disastrous, destroying the sense of danger basic to this as to all classic farces.

Mr Shaw has at least given the play an agreeable spine: as Fadinard, the distraught bridegroom, traverses Paris in search of a straw hat with poppies (no substitute accepted), his in-laws follow him in eight horse-drawn cabs and it is pleasant to see them mime this progress, high-stepping across the stage. But Sylvester McCoy lacks all precision as Fadinard, and the male supporting acts range from a good imitation of Eric Morecambe to a terrible one of Bill Fraser. As ever at this theatre, the girls are attractive, physically and vocally; Melody Kaye is as valuable as Valerie Walsh is melodious.

* * *

Eastward still, but south of the river, lies Greenwich, whose platform stage affords a fairer haven for drawing-room sets than any proscenium arch: the acres of red plush in which Bernard Culshaw has framed The Picture of Dorian Gray are richly imposing, so long as they are left alone. When the stage hands or the lighting technicians begin to play tricks with them, to effect a change of scene, the results are clumsy. John Osborne's adaptation of the Wilde novella is faithful even unto death; he has invented no theatrical equivalent for the original narrative method of revealing Dorian's guilty secret to us, so the second half of the play should prove incomprehensible to many people. But it includes much entertaining material, as well heard or read, and really makes a better play than many of Wilde's own. Unfortunately Anne Blake is the only member of the cast who knows how to weight an epigram and she has very little to say.
