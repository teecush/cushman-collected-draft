Of Mice and Men

When I read the book Of Mice and Men I thought it had the most speakable dialogue of any novel I had ever encountered. I knew that it had been turned into a play, and I could imagine the words just falling off the page into the actors' mouths. A wiser man had apparently had the same reaction; George S. Kaufman (described, almost insultingly, in the Mermaid programme as "an eminent director and writer of the time" had said to Steinbeck "it drops almost naturally into play form." Perhaps the lack of sweat is what made Kaufman, that voracious collaborator, take no official credit for his work on this one. Steinbeck seems to have been a natural playwright.

It is not just the arranges itselt races a it a tibe plot dialogue that plays easily; the guidly, around the stage. I could spot only two flaws. First, the minor characters are developed at greater length in the first half of the play than can well be supported in the second. But that is almost a virtue; it makes every role richly playable, a point on which the present production gratefully seizes. The second is that the catastrophe - Lennie's accidental killing of Curley's wife - happens rather hurriedly. She dies before she has fully come alive: the penalty of being a woman is what is, in its heart, an all-male play. For those who don't know, Of Mice and Men is set in California in the late thirties.

George and Lennie are itinerant workers, George small and reasonably bright, Lennie huge and retarded. They love each other, and they are unique among the roaming farm-hands of the West in that they have each other. They also have a dream: of saving their money and buying their own place. (The point of this is that they will be able to take the day off if a circus or a ball-game is in town; they sound more than a little like Tom Sawyer and Huck Finn.) George keeps Lennie in line through threats, cajolements, and repeated stories that Lennie likes to hear, Lennie loves to pet anything small and soft - mice, rabbits, puppies and he invariably pets them to death.

He also likes to caress women's soft dresses, and this has got him in trouble; the two are on the run from their last job when we Lou Hirsch plays George, the exasperated companion to the dopey Lennie (Clive Mantle): another well received production from the Nuffield, Southampton, this time in the hands of Geoff Bullen meet them. At their next they fall foul of Curley, the boss's son, a vicious bantam who likes provoking fights and who picks on Lennie, the gentle and - usually unresisting giant. This is smoothed over but it leaves Curley's frustrated and seemingly flirtatious wife, of whom he is violently jealous, disgusted and ready to go. But she gets into conversation with Lennie and her proximity turns him, fatally, on.

All right. The title. There is not much about mice, as rodents, after the first scene, but there is a lot about best-laid plans ganging agley. George and Lennie have their vision, for the first time, within reach; a disabled farm-hand Candy, fearful of being put out to grass, offers to come in with them and donate his savings. (A third possible flaw: Candy does go on a bit.) Lennie's act snuffs out that dream as suddenly and irrevocably as it snuffs out Curley's wife. Callous, and even misogynist, it may be, but we feel the death of the dream more acutely than we feel the death of the woman. The play's achievement is to make us feel it, physically: to want, desperately, to turn the clock back.

All that is left for George is to kill Lennie before Curley's lynch mob gets him. And then back, this time in solitude and without hope, to the fearful life of a migrant worker in the Depression. Not perhaps as fearful in the play as in reality; the life has been tenderised (the language certainly has and Slim, the kindly foreman, is maybe too good to be true. But the back-breaking barleypicking, and the aimlessness, and the fear ('can't ever tell whether you're talking to a nice guy or the Boss') and the lure of the cat-house that leaves a guy as poor as when he started work, are all vividly there. And, like most plays depicting an enclosed life, this is a treat for actors.

Clive Mantle, being nearly seven foot, has apparently made something of a career out of playing Lennie, and has evolved a vocal and physical language that puts him, as he should be, in a world of his own, its boundaries derived by bafflement (turning dangerously to terror) and desire to please. Lou Hirsch as George is may too hyper, too emphatic, but he gets infuriated protectiveness very well liam Roberts, drawling and solid as Slim, and Christopher Ryan, spluttering and febrile as Curley, are positive and negative poles for the currents that flow through the bunkhouse; Francis Drake (fairly endearing as Candy), Peter Banks and Ian Tyler go with it; Barry J. Gordon has him a stylish moment or two as the Boss who may even be a nice guy; and Calvin Simpson is splendidly grouchy as the black stableman, an unsympathetic victim of discrimination.

Susan Penhali- Sean Cavanagh's evocative and watery setting suggested an element of Mark Twain Susan Penhaligon as the floosie whose death at the clumsy hands of Clive Mantle, the benevolent giant, is the undoing of Steinbeck's itinerant duo gon is, like her character, initially rather out of it playing against the grain as a walking smudge of lipstick; when the lady is allowed some feelings, she develops. Geoff Bullen directs firmly and unhurriedly (this play could quite easily slip out of control); Sean Cavanagh has designed a variegated set that trucks back and forth over a stretch of river that looks real and poisonous enough to have been pumped in from the Thames on the other side of the stage.

Of Mice and Men, we are told, won the 1938 Critics' Circle prize over the heads of Golden Boy and Our Town. It deserved to beat the Odets, ambitious and tormented though we have this year seen that to be. Steinbeck, working out of the mainstream, was writing better common-man drama than those striving to do it in New York. As for the Wilder, it would be nice to have a chance to do our own judging. I don't promise to like it but I promise to attend.
