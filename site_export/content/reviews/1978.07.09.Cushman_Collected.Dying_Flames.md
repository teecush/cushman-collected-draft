Dying flames

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Stratford.

ALL THE characters in The Lady's Not for Burning (Old Vic) speak alike. That was noted when the play was first done just after the war. Christopher Fry admitted the point, but made light of it. After all, he said, people in real life all talk alike as well.

That is as true as it is disarming. But it does not follow that we all think or feel alike. Drama would be impossible if we did and dialogue, however uniform it may be, ought to clue us in to the rifts beneath. But the characters in The Lady seem to have no inner lives; all they have to offer are words, many of them now devalued by time.

I had hoped to be exhilarated by verbal ingenuity, if not actually by poetry. In the event I was fatigued by archness. Poetry concentrates, but Mr Fry's verse only decorates. Sometimes it even obscures. It is as if the conceits of Love's Labour's Lost were allowed five acts to themselves with no messenger of death to sober them up.

Death indeed hangs over Mr Fry's plot -- a war-shocked medieval soldier wants to be hanged, a suspected witch does not want to be burned -- but it makes no difference to what we actually hear. They rattle on regardless. They fall in love, of course, and are spared. "I love you," says Thomas the soldier, "but the world's not changed." A finely balanced conclusion which fails to resonate because "the world" has been kept firmly offstage.

Thomas, gazing into the wings, sums it up as "all nitwittery": a mincing phrase for the horrors of 1400 either more or less exactly, and even more so for those from which the world had just emerged when the play was written. Derek Jacobi and Eileen Atkins thread their lines well, but there is no romance between them, and Mr Jacobi seems too lightweight to be suicidal.

George Baker's production plods, offering single sparks instead of explosions, but it gives the actors room to work and the famous funny lines remain potent; in fact, they keep the evening afloat. "You always think you can do things better than your mother," says Brenda Bruce, cross but patient, to a fractious son who claims to have been reborn; and brings down the house.

Miss Bruce's timing and her unfussed professionalism are something of a revelation; Robert Eddison could hardly be that now, but his wispy Chaplain is perfect, again. Michael Thomas is promisingly good-humoured as a subsidiary lover, and Ronnie Stevens is a gleeful late arrival, bearing the welcome gifts of drunkenness, prose, and even the odd four-letter word. Has Mr Fry been rewriting, or was he an early unsung battler against the censor? The programme, piquantly enough, champions Mr Fry against the purveyors of "building-site dialogue."

* * *

I have my own objections to obscenity in the theatre, particularly in the West End. In A Family (Haymarket) a young girl tells her grandfather to fuck off, and the first-night audience fell about at the naughtiness of it all. It is the only such lapse in Ronald Harwood's curious play; the rest is literary, not only in style but in structure. The characters have their bearings only in the author's head; the climax is a flashback. You wonder why they couldn't have dragged out the skeleton in the first place.

The title is the play: one to which we can all, if you like, relate but which we resist because it has been done so often before. Mr Harwood's clan, besides, are marooned between archetype and special case -- and also, in Casper Wrede's nebulous production, between Jew and Gentile. It has besides a cast of solitaries: Paul Scofield, Harry Andrews, Irene Handl et al are, like most stars, natural orphans. Only Trevor Peacock looks attached and he, as it happens, is meant to be a foundling, connected only by marriage.

There is building-site dialogue to spare in Savage Amusement (Warehouse) and much of it could be spared. It is true that indiscriminate obscenity limits human potential; it certainly limits dramatic potential, since every time a row brews up the dialogue becomes predictable. To be fair, Peter Flannery's characters are meant to be limited -- they have in fact little choice -- but the distance between them and their author often gets blurred; convenience writing takes over.

These are blots on a compelling evening. We are in Manchester in 1982. The chalked slogan that reached our newspapers last week -- "Hulme is a prison" -- are out of date. It is now part wilderness, part concentration camp -- one of the areas abandoned by an authoritarian government which has brought back the birch, overhauled the social security regulations and staffed its offices with disembodied voices.

It is depressingly convincing, but Mr Flannery adds a dimension: we focus on a quartet of squatters, of whom one is an anthropology student, drawing unflattering comparisons between civilisation and savagery. Meanwhile we see a home-grown savage emerging: an 18-year-old scavenger blind to all except the most immediate loyalties, not so much learning survival as embodying it.

Played with fierce vacancy by David Threlfall, he is a powerful social and dramatic fact. Around him a debate rages between a drop-out who admires him and a pragmatist who deplores him; the author tries to be fair, but cannot help indulging the first and despising the second. There are awkward explanatory speeches and stageworn relationships. But the structure is deceptively cunning, the territory new and John Caird's production grimly sympathetic.

The old Manchester school is well represented at Greenwich by a revival of Stanley Houghton's classic Hindle Wakes, in which the mill-owner's son gets a working girl into trouble and she takes all breaths away by refusing to marry him. Robert Kidd's production is uneven, with one character misconceived, another miscast and Frances Viner smiling too readily as the heroine. But John Gill and Anne Dyson make her parents a fine couple of sanctimonious graspers, Tom Chadbon skilfully navigates the shallows of her intended and Mark Dignam gets much, though not all, of his father's doggedness. The odd suggestion of The Fosdyke Saga ("don't forget the old saying -- there's three generations from clogs to clogs") is rather charming.

* * *

In The Merchant of Venice (Other Place, Stratford), Patrick Stewart's Shylock, an unemphatic and unaccented businessman, is the most convincing I have seen. In the trial scene he follows the play's comic logic and cringes. Silently he begs mercy from Antonio; nods assent when commanded to become a Christian. I don't know if John Barton intended this to be as distressing as I found it; perhaps his production, like this year's Shrew, is concerned to expose the play's unpleasantness.

The whole Shylock menage is authoritatively handled with a silkily tormenting Tubal (Raymond Westwell) and a silk-hatted one-man band of a Launcelot Gobbo (Hilton McRae) -- like Harpo Marx given voice. Marjorie Bland's Portia reacts beautifully to her suitors from a distance, but on closer acquaintance freezes over; John Nettles, however, effectively conquers his accustomed sneer as Bassanio.
