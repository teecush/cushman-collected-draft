Musical musings

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Bar Mitzvah Boy.

AS I WAS saying, television plays, however meritorious, usually seem thin when translated to the theatre. One obvious way of thickening them, though, is to turn them into musicals; make them, that is to say, into wholly different animals. Let us consider Bar Mitzvah Boy (Her Majesty's).

The man who professes, when musicalising a play, to be doing it with the utmost respect for the original should be mistrusted. If you feel that way about a play you should leave it alone. Really you need the instincts of a vandal, prepared to change not only the shape of your source but its values as well.

These are just principles, understand; it is difficult to be that cavalier in practice, especially if your point of departure is something as estimable as Jack Rosenthal's teleplay. Since Mr Rosenthal has himself written the book of the musical the problems must have been peculiarly delicate: should he have remained true to himself and who, in this new state of affairs, was himself anyway?

Bar Mitzvah Boy has actually turned out a thoroughly professional musical (it doesn't look like an accident, which, since it is a predominantly British show, makes a pleasant change), but it is difficult to watch it without feeling a bit torn: half willing the author to be ruthless, half regretting the changes he actually has made.

The story remains the same: a 13-year-old Jewish boy runs out of the confirmation ceremony that is supposed to turn him into a man because the exemplars of manhood he has before him--his father, grandfather, and incipient brother-in-law--seem very poor advertisements for the product. His family are aghast; not only has he desecrated a religious ceremony, he has also (and, it seems, far more importantly) forced them to cancel the extremely expensive dinner-dance that was to follow. The officiating rabbi finds a Biblical text that enables them to have their celebrations (and the show, incidentally, its finale). Young Eliot makes his bar mitzvah speech; he has threatened--and here, I think, we depart from the original--to unmask the failings of his elders but he refrains, on the grounds that "it is less important to know the truth about people than to understand it": an unexceptionable sentiment, but a disappointing substitute for the bitter-sweet observation that Mr Rosenthal once kept going until the final credits.

I am breaking my own rule, blaming him for infidelity. But I have to, since he has not made enough changes to put such considerations out of court. Much of his dialogue, if not identical to what was televised, is at any rate as witty and as accurate. But there are considerable foreshortenings of character. Only Eliot's father, among his male elders, is given much scope: a testy taxi-driver forever complaining of the crippling expenses into which he has been inveigled. Grandfather has only one brief, rambling scene and an energetic song-and-dance ("The Harolds of This World") is no real substitute for the gentle but ruthless expose to which Eliot's sister's boyfriend, an unturning worm, was initially subjected.

So we do not feel the long-term reasons for the boy's flight--though the moment itself is tersely and tellingly presented with some of his relatives' more fatuous remarks superimposed on the boy's mind as they go through their assigned portions of the synagogue service. The musical in fact makes one point about Eliot's alienation more trenchantly than the play: that he is involved in a religious ceremony whose significance to everyone else seems purely social. Being a musical, though, it seems constrained to give excessive emphasis to the Jewish mother joke, which was far funnier when it was played down.

As a story musical, hewing near to an established dramatic line, the show suffers from some familiar drawbacks; the songs may sometimes intensify the situation, but they rarely propel them any farther than has already been managed by dialogue. There are a couple of marvellous moments in the score; get them when a character comes on and, immediately and without warning, sings; that does move things along. Jule Styne still writes better brassy music than anyone else around, and a pair of initially sticky ballads are rescued by cunningly bright reprises.

The other Americans involved are Martin Charnin, the highly efficient director, and the choreographer Peter Gennaro, whose first act knees-up (the one sequence in which the show unquestionably justifies itself) may be the most invigorating use ever made of a British chorus. The principals, too, are ours. I especially liked Harry Towb's cabbie, alternately shamefaced and acerbic, and the unnervingly confident Eliot of Barry Angel. In sum: there was probably no crying need to musicalise Bar Mitzvah Boy, but as the new Jewish proverb has it, if we tried living on necessities we'd probably starve.

* * *

Jesse and the Bandit Queen (Roundhouse Downstairs) is an off-Broadway success by David Freeman. Jesse is James; the bandit queen is Belle Starr, his female counterpart. The two of them play out their lives and deaths for us, occasionally stepping into each other's roles and even clothing. (There are psychological reasons for this, too fatiguing to explain.) They have their moments, but I wish they knew more people; it would have been pleasant had someone else dropped in from time to time. Annabel Leventon is certainly a belle, and very likely a star.
