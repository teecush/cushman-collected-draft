The call of the sea

SHE WAS only a lighthouse-keeper's daughter, but her passions were way off the beam.

Ellida, the heroine of Ibsen's The Lady From The Sea (Royal Exchange, Manchester), has a marine complex, presumably inherited, that caused her during a youth spent in the frozen north to fall in love with a sailor. He vanished, with a murder charge hanging over him, and she became the second wife of a doctor from the south.

Here she lives, loving her husband, but unable to communicate with him or with his two resentful daughters. Besides, the sea keeps calling her: most audibly in Michael Elliott's production, where the cry of the breakers and cries of (I am assured) whales and dolphins come at you from all sides in the highest of theatrical fidelity. They sound real enough for a dream and they are complemented by Laurie Dennett's set which is largely composed of water.

Dr Wangel lives by a fjord and the action here takes place on a series of moveable jetties. Stage-hands wade in between the acts to shift them around; one even planted a tree which obscures an important conversation in the succeeding scene. But it is soon uprooted, and everything else is clear: this is the most visually satisfying production yet staged at this theatre.

It is also by far the best acted. It is a paradox that this play, which would seem to have the most tenuous and unlikely plot of any Ibsen, has also the greatest feeling for the everyday life of a household. The affairs of the doctor's daughters are set, in almost Chekhovian counterpoint, to those of their stepmother. Ellida believes that in marrying she may have sacrificed her freedom for the sake of an illusory security; Bolette, the elder girl, is shown doing the same in agreeing to marry her old tutor, who is himself merely transferring his affections from Ellida herself. Meanwhile young Hilde (the future predator of The Master Builder) amuses herself in teasing a sculptor who looks to the hopeless but ennobling longing he must surely inspire in every young woman he meets.

Against these illusions are set the claims of a perfectly real lover, Ellida rejects him - to summarise brutally - only after the doctor has set her free to make her own decision. She then subsides, quietly hymning the joys of responsibility. That seems glib when you read it but Vanessa Redgrave and Graham Crowden make it not only credible, but moving.

Mr Crowden, who starts with the advantage of being in his angular way an exceptionally endearing actor, visibly grapples with his own possessiveness, thereby generating a powerful electricity of his own, quite enough to counterbalance the seafarer's magic. Miss Redgrave can make you share her belief in any number of impossible things; happiness, bewilderment, loneliness, loss, and happiness again suffuse her every inch. (Her height, given the intensity with which she works, may be her greatest asset.) She gives an amazingly unstrained performance.

The play belongs to her; she has played Ellida in New York, and years ago in London she played Bolette. The memory of that performance lends resonance to the parallel between the characters in the present production. Sherrie Hewson's Bolette, initially a modest lump, grows steadily in strength and definition through the evening, as does Lynsey Baxter's Hilde, the only character actually to get her feet wet. She trails them in the water while effectively seducing the sculptor, who is amusingly played by Christopher Good.

There should be other bouquets for John Franklyn Robbins's tutor, Michael Byrne's stranger and for Ronald Herdman as a choric Jack-of-all-trades, who dissolves play and production in a single call. Seventeen years ago Miss Redgrave and Mr Elliott gave us the As You Like It of a generation; their joint return to form could hardly be happier.

* * *

On the strength of Gemini, still running on Broadway, I would rate Albert Innaurato the best of the new transatlantic playwrights. Hampstead, the Royal Court and the Cottesloe should be knifing one another for the rights. Meanwhile the Bush has sent us Benno Blimpie, an earlier play which, like Gemini, shows us a world of Italian immigrants with a sensitive and fat boy at its centre, but is earlier, shorter, more schematic and more self-indulgently ugly. Benno Blimpie has the soul of an artist, but the shape and the appetite of the Michelin man. And with no rosetted restaurant available in the district, he has to gorge himself on junk food.

Discarded wrappers and empty cartons lie in heaps around him as he looks back on his life: arrested by his mother, neglected by his father, bullied and insulted by the other kids - especially by a 13-year-old nymph, who seduces and robs his grandfather. Watching this last experience 26 years later, he intervenes until he is assaulted by a gang of youths in a playground. Realising that the world has no other use for his body, and none at all for his mind, he sets out to eat himself to death.

It is a limited view of the world, but Mr Innaurato can impose it for the duration; his writing commands wit as well as grotesquerie and the two are balanced here in Lynda Marchal's swashbuckling production. Michael Poole's groping, pathetic old man; Robbie Coltrane, pain and indignation battling across his face, does a sympathetic job on Benno.

An Evening With Dave Allen (Vaudeville) is time well spent with a modest but masterly comedian. I'm not too sure what comics get up to in Ireland, but over here Mr Allen seems to belong less to the tradition of the English standup gag man than to that of the loose American conversationalist: homely Will Rogers crossed with liberal Mort Sahl. Not incendiary Lenny Bruce, though: one conceivably anti-gay joke aside, Mr Allen, who is in favour of tolerance and against bigotry (sexual, religious, political), says little that today's West End is likely to find controversial.

I was tempted to write him down at first as better than his material, but this would hardly do since he rolls his own. Sometimes he pursues a subject too far and too mechanically, but one or two of his riffs - for example his plain man's gloss on the story of Noah - are inspired. He is very likeable, which has got to be the intention.

Lumiere and Son are ahead of most performance art groups in that they can actually perform. All five of them can move and talk with conviction. The severe limitations of their medium are thus exposed more clearly than usual. They can present an issue - in this case the constrictions of a guilt-ridden social order - in pretty pictorial terms, but they cannot explore it.

Lacking faith in words, they patronise their characters, who are made to scan like inarticulate slobs, no worse than the patronage of much conventional drama, but it is to me more tedious. They too are likeable, but I am not sure that that is the intention. Their show is called Nightfall and it can be found at the Theatre Upstairs.
