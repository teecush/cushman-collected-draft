Turning up at Tony-time

NEW YORK THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN reports on his recent visit to Broadway.

LONDON no longer has a definable theatre season, but Broadway still likes things to be official and that's why Tonys were born.

The Tony (Antoinette Perry Award) is Broadway's Oscar, presented in droves in mid-April to reward the best endeavours of the previous twelvemonth. The visiting critic arriving, as I fortuitously did, at Tony-time is thus provided with a handy official guide to what he should be seeing. (Shows presented off-Broadway--one step down the economic scale--or off-off-Broadway--where the unions are charitable and wages minimal--are not eligible.)

It has been, by most accounts, a dim season for musicals; the acknowledged best, Candide, was a revival and therefore ineligible for an award of its own. There were prizes, though, for several of its participants, including confusingly enough, its author--Hugh Wheeler having provided a new libretto to replace the one which was generally held to have sunk the show back in 1956.

Even Mr Wheeler's version is liable to sag; nor, despite the presence of many good voices, is full justice done to Leonard Bernstein's celebrated score. Mr Bernstein actually wrote a mock-opera, in a variety of parodied styles, and this is a form of sophistication for which Harold Prince's production has very little time. He has opted for calculated innocence, accepted that Voltaire's fable is too repetitive to be conventionally dramatic, and cunningly embraced the picaresque. Suspense consists not in wondering what is going to happen next, but where. There is a choice of half a dozen stages, situated all over the theatre; the audience sits in the middle. The method will be familiar to anyone who has seen the Grand Magic Circus, but Candide is both more expert and more fun. Mark Baker, guileless and curly-headed, is a model Candide and the music is still glorious.

When the Broadway musical is not battering its audiences with, or into, insensibility, Mr Prince's presence can generally be assumed. His production of Stephen Sondheim's A Little Night Music is now into its second year and, despite some signs of wear, was the best thing I saw in New York. Based on the Ingmar Bergman film Smiles of a Summer Night, it makes a conscious (sometimes a self-conscious) bid for high comedy tinged with farce, and is thus unusually dependent on its cast. On Broadway I was moved and enchanted by the performances of Glynis Johns and Hermione Gingold and by the dizzying splendour of Sondheim's lyrics, matched, on this occasion, by his tunes. A London version is due in the autumn.

Neither of these shows offers much scope to the choreographer, though what there is has been well seized by Patricia Birch. She and her dancers are the best reason for seeing Over Here, an attempted musical epic of the home front in World War II. It is, a little bewilderingly, a cosy epic, since it features the two surviving Andrews Sisters, chatting up their audiences with the matronly aplomb of an American Elsie and Doris Waters.

The Sisters still sound as they used to 25 years ago when there were three of them, which is some kind of achievement, and it is pleasant to watch the patrons enjoying them. (The American audience is one of the nicest things about the American theatre: kindly, earnest, homogeneous and recognisable. Nobody knows who goes to the theatre in London.) It would be easy to be offended (as some New Yorkers have been) by the show's general silliness (and even more by its attempts at intelligence), but an Englishman cannot help admiring its efficiency. It comes from the same producers as Grease (its rock 'n' roll equivalent, now the longest-running show on Broadway) and has a similar frenetic appeal.

Ulysses in Nighttown (originally seen off-Broadway and indeed in London in the late fifties) is not musical, but it is a similar kind of star vehicle, being an excuse for Zero Mostel to do his Leopold Bloom routine; sentences from Ulysses are read aloud and he (often hilariously) mimes to them. Mr Mostel's look of appalled distaste when instructed that Bloom "kisses the bedsores of a palsied veteran" is a sight, but it illustrates the extent to which he is commenting on the text rather than acting it.

With his button eyes, short and bristly moustache and battery of chins, Mr Mostel certainly looks like Bloom and he transmits a furious worship of Joyce, but he sounds like nothing on earth; certainly not on Irish earth. Burgess Meredith's production is decently imaginative, but the occasion hardly amounts to more than an animated literary recital. Molly Bloom's monologue, as delivered by the nude and supine Fionnula Flanagan, is, aurally and visually, the highlight of the evening; but it is out of context, only thrown in because it could hardly be left out.

The house rose (literally) to both play and star. But I became progressively appalled by what New York would accept in the way of seriousness (and a little surprised by what they would not accept: Tom Stoppard's Jumpers opened to apathy and bewilderment during my stay). Broadway's pride is a lethargic production of O'Neill's A Moon for the Misbegotten, not itself the snappiest of plays, but possibly possessed of a warmth which was here stamped into the ground in the name--presumably--of truth. Ten years ago it was claimed that such solemnity had very nearly murdered American acting, and the charge still holds.

In truth I saw no production of a native play as good as the current London version of A Streetcar Named Desire; directed--with an English cast--by the American Edwin Sherin. Obviously, though, Mr Sherin is good at transatlantic transplants; his was the Broadway production of John Hopkins's Find Your Way Home with the magnetic performance (at once controlled and overflowing) by Michael Moriarty to which I referred last week. But even in this cast there was some poor acting. Once the musicals are left behind, Broadway's performance standards can dip surprisingly.

So can off-Broadway's, though a lugubriously sentimental piece called The Sea Horse, much lauded by the Press, and offered by a prestigious group called the Circle Repertory, was well played throughout. It had a cast of two.

Off-off-Broadway seemed largely given over to revivals, though it can take first-night credit for an entertaining double-bill--Bad Habits by Terrence McNally--which has now made the full Broadway pilgrimage: off-off to off to on. I caught it off, and found it--at any rate its first half, which was, enragingly, the less well acted--a well-drawn cartoon of hung-up middle-class America, who of course filled the theatre and reacted with marvellous masochistic sportsmanship. The air was full--if you can imagine it--of rueful guffaws.
