Victims of parental war

ROBERT CUSHMAN on fringe plays in London, an Anouilh premiere in Bristol and 'The Party' on tour.

JOHNNIE QUARRELL'S Stakeout at the Half Moon is a play about baby-bashing. The statistics of the subject are frightening: 4,000 cases each year is the number quoted in the programme, and the implication is that it is these figures that have driven the author to write his play.

The implication is borne out by what we see on stage; there are in fact two characters but essentially this is a play about statistics. Mr Quarrell's embattled parents exist only as mechanisms for destroying one child, and conceiving another plainly destined for the same end. One or two traces of individuality they must be allowed in order to keep the play going, but these are arbitrary and unconvincing. The play is one half of a double bill, its companion piece being Homeworker by the young German playwright Franz Xaver Kroetz, whose Stallerhof made so deep an impression at the Bush a few months ago. The new play bears some superficial resemblances to Stakeout; it also depicts infanticide against a starkly underprivileged background. Like Mr Quarrell, Kroetz quotes statistics in his programme note (the English play seems, indeed, to have been specifically designed as a counterpart to the German one), but Mr Quarrell has fastened precisely on what is least valuable in Kroetz's work. Homeworker, whatever its inspiration may have been, is a play about people.

It has a real quirky shape. The title refers to a husband, debarred by illness from outside employment. His wife is periodically, joylessly unfaithful; she gets pregnant. At his suggestion she attempts to abort herself with a knitting needle. She fails; and when the baby is born it has a couple of lumps on its head, a deformity on which the husband--to the confusion of his own two children--continually and bitterly harps. She leaves him; eventually she returns but by this time the baby, left in his care, is dead.

Kroetz's characters, like Mr Quarrell's, live on the edge of desolation; the difference is that the German writer never quite pushes them over. Always there is some saving humanity; the play is bleak, but it is not mechanical. I will admit, though, that the memory of Stallerhof rather than anything apparent at the Half Moon persuaded me of this.

The production is considerably more crippled than any of its characters. The play, written in short scenes, constantly changing its locale, is here approached with a hopeless listless literalism. Long pauses for scene changes murder the dramatic rhythm--or would if any had been achieved within the episodes themselves. Much of Kroetz's drama is unspoken; in this production it is merely absent.

As damaging as anything else is the decision to abandon the play's German setting so as to give it an immediate local relevance. This surely is community theatre gone berserk; the idea that people can only respond to actions that are supposedly taking place on their own doorstep is destructive to the idea of drama, which is supposed to widen audiences' horizons, and patronising to the spectators themselves. The implicit assumption is that the plays are absolutely representative of local working-class life. That is insulting if false and self-defeating if true; anyone not aroused to protest by direct experience of such a situation is unlikely to be much affected by seeing it re-enacted on a stage. What actually happens, of course, is that the intended audience stays well away from the play, which ends up performed for us middle-class voyeurs. Even we, however, deserve a better deal.

* * *

Stephen Poliakoff is a 21-year-old writer who has written a stage-worthy play. That will do for compliments. Clever Soldiers (Hampstead Theatre Club) tells of a young man's progress from public school to Oxford and thence into the trenches of the First World War. It is a progress of disillusion. Young Teddy loses all faith in the values he has been taught but finds that they have gone too deep in him to permit any real change. He is fated to command and his men to obey; the only way out is death, which obstinately avoids him.

Intellectually the play is persuasive enough; structurally it is neat; its characters, like those of Stakeout, are all too obviously tailored to fit the thesis. Teddy is powerfully portrayed by Simon Ward in his best young subaltern manner, but his every action seems dictated. Perhaps the worlds he moves through are--from previous plays, films and books--just too familiar to us. We know all about World War I--at least we know as much as Mr Poliakoff is able to tell us. We know too the legend of pre-cataclysmic Oxford, divided between rampageous bloods and nervously epigrammatic aesthetes.

These last are represented by Michael Feast, splendidly tic-ridden, but required to bear too heavy a load of degeneracy. Opposed to him is Teddy's socialist tutor, and the two spend much of the play battling for the hero's affections and, by implication, for his soul. But the implications are difficult to swallow because the actual relationships are underwritten; there are huge gaps in the development. This is even more the case with the one girl in Teddy's life, an emergent liberated woman whom Sheila Ruskin plays--perhaps of necessity--on one barking note. Teddy ends by rejecting everybody, and this at least is painfully believable.

* * *

Trevor Griffiths's The Party looks considerably more persuasive in its National Theatre mobile production than it did last year at the Old Vic. The living room in which its collection of radical intellectuals assemble during the 1968 Paris uprisings now looks as if it might truly belong to a TV producer rather than a movie mogul. Several minor characters have been eliminated and those who remain have been fleshed out a bit, each being allowed some kind of response to the success ethic which the producer shamefacedly embodies, and which the Trotskyist Tagg attacks in the play's longest speech.

In its original venue half the play's energies seemed devoted to providing a bourgeois audience with an enforced lesson in political philosophy. The present production, touring mainly on a university circuit, more narrowly examines the psychology of the amateur revolutionary. The main conflict is no longer between Tagg and his LSE opponent, whose revisionist credo, delivered with such sparkle by Denis Quilley at the Vic, has been considerably damped down and seems in fact much longer than is justified by its significance to the argument. It is easily squelched by Fulton Mackay's Tagg, lacking Olivier's bravura, but substituting an unassuming intensity which establishes him almost unassailably at the play's moral centre.

Almost but not quite, since he comes under withering fire from the play's Thersites-figure, materially the most successful of them all. Jack Shepherd in this role lacks Frank Finlay's comic finesse (most actors do) but makes up for it in violence. It remains a weakness in the play that these two are permitted no real confrontation; their opposition is dramatised only by their effect on the producer and this is still bewilderingly inconclusive. (Intellectual ambivalence may be a sophisticated virtue, but here spills over into insecure dramaturgy.) Hugh Thomas nevertheless makes more of his focal role than I would have thought possible, aided by direction from David Hare which makes up in intelligence and clarity for what is lost in atmosphere.

* * *

I saw The Party in the studio theatre of the Bristol Old Vic, whose main house had on the previous night scooped the world premiere of Jean Anouilh's The Arrest, which title--or so I like to think--derives from Shakespeare. The Arrest is the strict one imposed by the fell sergeant Death, here promoted to the rank of superintendent and played unerringly by Michael Rothwell, a Bristol fixture for several years and among the most calmly skilful actors in the land. His victim is portrayed--simultaneously--by Alan Dobie (ageing and embittered), John Hurt (youthful and vulnerable), and a small boy (a small boy).

This is one of those plays about time--or rather (like all such from Priestley to Pinter) a play about memory; as ingenious and enjoyable as you would expect from Anouilh and not overburdened with significance. Applause for most of the cast, among whom Marilyn Taylerson and Charlotte Cornwell are particularly striking, for Alexander McPherson's spectacular sets, and for Val May's impeccably neat production.
