Scooping up Africa

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Stoppard's latest play.

IT can sometimes be difficult to persuade people of the unbroken continuity of Tom Stoppard's work; and he doesn't help either, dividing his stuff into the real heavies and the "nuts and-bolts" comedies.

His new one, Night and Day (Phoenix), has affinities to that second workmanlike category (it is, just as a story, much the tensest play he has written), but its size and its ambitions place it with Jumpers and Travesties. And yet it takes off from a moment in the impenitently lightweight Dirty Linen: the one in which the delectable Mrs Gotobed advises her roomful of lovers not to worry what the Press might say about them. After all, she maintains, the readers don't really care, and the journalists are mainly concerned to impress themselves and each other.

Ruth, the heroine of Night and Day, puts the same case, more emotively. She is the wife of a senior mining official in the imaginary African country of Kambawe, a State with--depending on your point of view--a secession or a rebellion or a liberation problem. From the Sunday Globe in Britain come three journalists to cover the story: a photographer and two reporters. One of the reporters, Jacob Milne, is actually a young freelance who has got lucky and secured an exclusive interview with the rebel leader. This does not please his counterpart Dick Wagner, a hard-bitten Australian. (That is very likely how he would describe himself, but I must admit that the cliche rolled quite effortlessly off my typewriter, which seems to prove some of Mr Stoppard's points for him.)

Dick is a strong union man; Jacob isn't, having resigned over a strike at his former paper, the Grimsby Messenger. Dick informs London of this, demands that Jacob be taken off the story. His demand is refused, but it sets off a labour dispute, and next Sunday the Globe fails to appear, which is not quite what Dick wanted, since this time he has the scoop. It doesn't matter much to Jacob anyway; he's dead, shot in--for want of a better word--action.

It is this last event that sets off Ruth's tirade. It's all bloody ego ... as far as I'm concerned Jake died for the product. She, as it happens, was some way to being in love with him, though it is Wagner whom--just once in London--she had bedded; at the final disillusioned curtain, she looks like repeating the performance. "A lady," she had said earlier, "might go to bed with a chap once ... but twice ... a lady might think she'd been taken for a tart." (The structural ironies in this play are fewer than in most of Mr Stoppard's but they are crushers.)

Anyway, and shades of The Front Page, both men are less enamoured of her than of their jobs, symbolised in a very practical manner by her husband's shining Telex machine. I am not sure exactly what Mr Stoppard intends us to make of Ruth; he gives her more than he has given any other woman. She has charm, wit, beauty, music, some passion, much self-mockery; alone in the cast, she has a direct line to the audience.

She is played with an abundance of all the qualities mentioned above, by Diana Rigg, whose personal blend of sad secrecy with devastating attack has never been more highly charged. But somewhere her roles of commentator and protagonist have got tangled up. It would make sense if her problems were the same as the journalists' and she just happened to see them more clearly, but that isn't how it comes out.

She gets a lot of the "civilised-values" speeches, siding with Jacob against Wagner over the journalists' closed-shop. This seems loaded. Jacob is given an attractive boy-scout performance by Peter Machin and Miss Rigg is Miss Rigg; against them John Thaw's Wagner, forceful though he is (over-forceful for the first half-hour, in which his accent seems to consume most of his attention), has no chance. And the argument is trivialised; the reduction of workers' solidarity to protective squabbles over differentials is a sadder and more complex thing than the play admits; so is the newspaper industry itself.

When the play widens to consider the whole question of a free Press, its value, its abuse and its psychology, it achieves kaleidoscopic brilliance: from particular painful barbs ("rubbish journalism is produced by people doing their best work") to sweeping proclamations. And even Ruth's last outburst has its coda: when the denimed cameraman (played by William Marlowe with, well, photographic accuracy) admits her case, but adds to it the rider that reportage is some small insurance against tyranny. "Information is light." And he should know about light.

You can set against this the blunt honesty of Olu Jacob's African president, or the precise amount of Press freedom that he, a despot but not a madman, can tolerate. The amount of imaginative (as opposed to intellectual) sympathy in this portrayal is something new for Mr Stoppard. The jokes are disarmingly good as ever. (He even gets away with having Wagner called Strauss by a composer joke, but a composer called Richard joke.) It is an elating evening.

* * *

The Glasgow Citizens' company have always been heedless of immortality, but it is cavalier, even by their standards, to have jettisoned their version of The Spanish Tragedy after a mere eight performances. As expected they got Kyd's primal shocker (the foundation-stone of the Elizabethan theatre) exhilaratingly right. Despite some allegations, they did not even muck around with the text very much; the alleged additions were mostly of contemporary origin and helped to make the story clearer. (Some reviewers have strange ideas about the plays of this period; one chastised Terry Hands for introducing a ghost into The Changeling. That house was haunted long before the RSC reopened it.) Death comes easy in The Spanish Tragedy, most staggeringly in the final scene: a play within a play where the deaths are real and the victims expire to enthusiastic audience applause. At Glasgow it seemed inevitable.

A Trick to Catch the Old One (Theatre Clwyd, Mold) usually seems arbitrary. This is another Middleton, this time a city comedy of cheating and more cheating. In Scotland they played in modern dress and one gave it hardly a second thought; in Wales they did the same, and seemed to be trying to prove something. Michael Hucks's production lacked comic gusto; its rival usurers were tame figures.

The production only came to life when most serious; when the play's peculiar male morality carelessly turned a courtesan to a victim, or when an extraneous miser (a Dickensian figure) expired in a haze of greed and avarice, quietly mourned by his housekeeper, to whom Ilona Linthwaite gave remarkable depth in very little space.

A good week for transfers: the joyous Gloo Joo to the Criterion; DHL, the Lawrence show praised by Victoria Radin at Edinburgh, to the New End; and to the Prince of Wales, prior to Broadway, Alan Ayckbourn's Bedroom Farce, which may be my favourite play of the past five years: not necessarily the best, though I can think of none which achieves its aims more completely, or in which every word works more efficiently.

Three and a half marriages are laid hilariously, sharply, but quite unpatronisingly, bare. The missing half is the distrait Susannah, who has now defeated three actresses: Delia Lindsay finds truth in her, but not much humour, perhaps because Mr Ayckbourn forgot to leave her any. With her mate Trevor, however (of whom I was also inclined to complain on first acquaintance), he has done wonders, and Stephen Moore--appealing with his whole length to every interlocuter, even when the conversation is being conducted by telephone--does even more.
