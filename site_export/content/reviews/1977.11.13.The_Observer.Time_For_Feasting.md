Time for feasting

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Paris and London.

THERE are those, and I fear they even include some theatre critics, who go to Paris to eat rather than to see plays. But happily we can do both.

As theatrical tourists we don't have to contend with first-night timetables and even if we did I suspect that the Parisian sense of priorities would make it easy for us to dine before. The rule in Paris theatre remains unaltered: everything starts late.

The centrepiece of this year's World Theatre Festival is Moscow's Taganka Theatre, whose arrival has occasioned a new brand of delaying tactic: the queue for simultaneous translation equipment. In Paris, of course, you don't queue; you mill. And money alone cannot hire you the gift of tongues; you hand over your identity card or, if you are foreign and easily intimidated by officialdom ahead of you and the shoving crowd behind, your passport. You have, of course, to get them back, under similar conditions, at the end.

Paris at the moment makes the Anglo-Saxon feel at home. Record-shop displays and Metro graffiti alike proclaim the supremacy of the Sex Pistols. And British playwrights rule (really one might be in New York). Bedroom Farce is there already, Equus approaches the home stretch after its customary lengthy gallop, and four chicks called Steffy, Pomme, Jane et Vivi are nobody but our own Dusa, Fish, Stas and Vi.

The stage-struck Briton, wondering why he left home, can anyway catch up with the generation of French actresses on whose names he grew up: Madeleine Renaud, Edwige Feuillère, Maria Casarès, Suzanne Flon are all on view. Mlle Flon's vehicle, shared with Dominique Blanchar (who is marvellous) and Françoise Lugagne, is Les Dames du Jeudi by Loleh Bellon, a striking example of boulevard theatre at its finest and one of the few new French plays of interest.

It is in a way the middle-aged equivalent of Dusa, Fish: three women, whose means stop just short of being comfortable, forgather every Thursday to bitch and reminisce over their sexual and political pasts. These are re-enacted without change of set or costume.

French companies visiting London, mainly in classics, have accustomed us to rhetorical virtuosity, but have given little hint of the naturalistic gifts, tender and fluent, on show here. Mlle Blanchar's role--a woman exploited by a wastrel son--recapitulates the theme of Marguerite Duras's Days in the Trees and makes it dramatic.

As for the classics: the Comédie Française had three Molières in the bill during my visit and apparently was doing very nicely with them. At all events, I couldn't get a ticket. Elsewhere there are three Ibsens; I would guess, without having seen it, that the Biothéâtre-Opéra's Master Builder is very good.

My evidence is a production by the same group (and the same young director, Pierre Sala) of a piece even crazier in its austerity, Claudel's La Jeune Fille Violaine, a fable of sin and miracle set, with some audacity, at the end of the last century, the author's own time. It has the rigour, if not the control, of Racine: and something of the same bemusing exhilaration--so out of step as to seem avant-garde. The company is, appropriately, a fiercely disciplined team of vocal gymnasts.

Before returning to the Muscovites, I should mention an American team playing cheek-by-jowl with the Paris Festival, though not a part of it. Guided by Elisabeth Swados (who was on Peter Brook's African trip, and provided the music for Andrei Serban's Greek tragedy productions), they offered Nightclub Cantata, full of the expected skills in song and full, too, of the portentous cuteness that passes for poetry off-Broadway.

If they were hung-up, the Russians were refreshingly simple. That, I suppose, is another cultural cliché, especially as applied to what is reputed to be Moscow's most adventurous company. But the Taganka (founded 1964) showed us, in its version of Gorki's The Mother, what could be done with a large stage (they performed in the huge Palais de Chaillot), great resources of money and manpower, and a tradition of strong, jovial, realistic acting.

The director, Iouri Lioubimov, goes in for stabbing lights, choreographic staging, and massed choral effects. There are times when his production sounds like Boris Godunov and looks like Fiddler on the Roof. It is everything that was once called arty; but it is done with great visual assurance and--thanks to the cast--with unlimited warmth. That may not have figured in the director's scheme; he is lucky to be able to take it for granted. It makes the strongest contrast with the Brechtian version of the same work which was so rapturously received in London a few years ago.

Does anyone remember the scene in which Brecht's Mother, pausing for a moment from her work of making the revolution apparently single-handed, told the mothers of her opponents that they had no right to grieve for their sons since they had fallen on the wrong side? Even that might have been supportable--it might have chimed with our masochistic notions of political toughness--in a play less concerned to wring every sentimental ounce out of the idea of motherhood.

There is nothing quite as bad in The Days of the Commune (Aldwych), but little good either. I found it difficult as the evening unfolded to apportion blame between author, director and actors, but by the end was too dispirited to care. I think, though, that Howard Davies has applied a disembodied idea of "Brechtianism" that works for almost any playwright but Brecht; certainly not for this late play that needs all the realistic help it can get.

It is set here in a kind of puritan limbo; no hint of Paris in 1871 or of anywhere else very much. (The set is this year's Shakespearean fixture. It worked for Ibsen, not for Brecht. Draw your own conclusions.) The play is slanted so heavily in favour of the Communards that my own sympathy with them quite disappeared. Most of the second act is devoted to the question of whether to preserve revolutionary purity and perish, or turn repressive and survive.

These are big questions but not, divorced from particular personalities, very subtle ones; or, after five minutes, very compelling. They are briefly enlivened by the cutting rhetoric of Ian McDiarmid, as the corresponding street-scenes about the gallant barricaders are fitfully rescued by the shy clowning of Mike Gwilym.
