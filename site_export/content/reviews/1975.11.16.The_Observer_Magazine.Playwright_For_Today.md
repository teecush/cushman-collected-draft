Playwright for today

HOWARD BRENTON, a tall, broad, and gentle man of 32, is the playwright of his generation. He may or may not be the best of them (I think he is) but he typifies them. This 'generation' (a word frequently on Brenton's own lips) is, at least in terms of its playwrights, a broad one. It stretches from Stephen Poliakoff, in his early twenties, to Trevor Griffiths, pushing 40. Besides their individual plays, these writers have often worked together. Brenton and David Hare's joint work 'Brassneck', seen at Nottingham two years ago, was as big a success as either has had. As far as Brenton is concerned the works of his peers are all-important, the only plays he can read with interest. 'I'm a bit of a Philistine,' he says. 'For me one play of David Hare's is worth more than the whole of Shaw.'

Brenton was the first playwright of his group to be commissioned by Peter Hall's new regime at the National Theatre, and the fruits of that commission, 'Weapons of Happiness', will open at the Lyttelton Theatre next June. His playwriting career began, disastrously, however, in 1965, in his last term as a Cambridge undergraduate. What he now calls 'a hopeless, huge, soggy, violent, joyless allegory' drove its audience out of the theatre in large numbers. To compensate he wrote another play, handling the same themes at half the length in a vein of 'savage, absurdist, black comedy. I discovered that I could tell jokes. The bedrock of my work is still, in a way, a delight in scabrous humour.

'Why scabrous? I've always felt like something of a bum; maybe that's the position a writer finds himself in. Believing nothing that's told you, moving on from theatre to theatre. So the sense of fun is a delinquent one.'

That 'believing nothing that's told you' may explain why Brenton, though a political writer, is not doctrinaire. He is a socialist, but he worries about not being affiliated to a specific party. Again, he describes his politicisation in terms of generational solidarity. In 1969 he visited Paris where he 'met many people who were survivors -- barely survivors -- of what had happened in '68. I began to think of political things for the first time. The sense of loss was enormous; something had been attempted by my generation and it had been smashed.'

Even before this Brenton had been preoccupied with questions of law and authority. Policemen and criminals recur in his plays. His first important play, 'Revenge', staged at the Royal Court's Theatre Upstairs in 1969, concentrated on one of each, played by the same actor. It was a sour, uproarious farce, elegantly written, elegance being a matter not of decoration but of efficiency: the ability to make words carry the maximum weight with the minimum effort. A one-act play, 'Christie in Love', probing the legend of the most celebrated of post-war murderers, moved more darkly over a similar area.

'Christie' was presented by the pioneer touring group Portable Theatre. Writing for them Brenton had to pare everything down; once a florid writer he was now charged and laconic. His clipped, disciplined sentences can give an extraordinary impression of pent-up fury.

In 1973 Brenton completed a year as resident dramatist at the Royal Court (an institution with which he has had, and maintains, an intense, amused love-hate relationship) with 'Magnificence'. The concluding scene showed a young revolutionary trying to find a rationale for throwing a bomb at a Tory Minister and finding himself trapped in a vortex of conflicting emotions. Involved rather than committed, it was the most honestly complex scene yet to appear in New Left drama.

Last year saw his 'Churchill Play' staged at Nottingham; this too may be done at the National. Again it is ambivalent since, whatever Brenton's private view of Churchill, as a playwright, 'I wanted to give him his full voice and passion -- horrible though I think it was'. He reckons that his plays are getting 'more real and more loving of people'. This may be due to the aging process though he takes the depressing view that 'most playwrights blow up by the time they're 35'. Or it could owe something to his marriage; it is from that event, five years ago, that he dates his ability to write about women. 'I was a fearfully sexist playwright before I met Jane; the women in my plays were lovely but fearsome creatures.'

He and his wife now own a house in Camberwell, have one child, live on what might be called a steady income. He reckons he's been able to make a living as a playwright since 1969. 'Sometimes you can go abroad, ride everywhere in taxis; sometimes you can't sell anything and the rats are coming up through the drain to get you.'

Robert Cushman
