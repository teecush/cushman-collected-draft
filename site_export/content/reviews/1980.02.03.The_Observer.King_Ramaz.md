King Ramaz

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the Rustaveli Company, Brecht, 'Oklahoma!' and 'Beecham.'

THE Georgian Richard III, widely proclaimed as earth-shattering at last year's Edinburgh Festival, has arrived at the Roundhouse and has predictably proved to be merely very exciting.

The Rustaveli Company's most exotic adornment, to our eyes, is the bull-like force of its players combined, in the case of Ramaz Chkhikvadze's Richard, with a harsh wit. He is not a self-pitying Richard, though, since the production has his destroyer Richmond dogging his footsteps from the first, he might have some excuse for concern. He is not even particularly crippled, though he displays an impressively withered arm when the plot calls for it. He is a burly figure in a grey Napoleonic greatcoat and high black boots. His voice is mournful, with an ache in it. He seems driven neither by ambition nor villainy; he is a conscious agent of doom.

The second most spectacular actor is Avto Makharadze, a gash-mouthed player who supplies a joke-archbishop and a choric jester as appendages to a dying, suppurating Edward IV, a sick toad. There are interesting performances too among the courtiers: Buckingham is trim and gentlemanly, a chill time-server from the first. The council-scenes have the deadly, conscienceless efficiency for which British productions used to strive in the Sixties when Jan Kott was God.

But those productions were sleek and picturesque. The peculiar interest of these Georgian plottings is that they take place in an atmosphere of decay. Costumes are mixed modern, props are few and dilapidated. This too is a style familiar to us--more perhaps from other visitors than from native productions--but it is unusual to see it combined with political clarity. The pervasive mockery is as much aural as visual: a fiddler pops up to accompany the wooing of Lady Anne. There are occasional bursts of rock-music to accompany the more violent turns of action; other moments are underlined by a rottingly sweet Palm Court tune that seems always about to turn into the Pineapple Song from Cabaret.

Richard and Richmond fight their last duel with their heads popping out of a map of England; the kingdom is otherwise represented by a dirty Union Jack. Shakespeare is telescoped, so that Richard briefs the murderers as Clarence tells his dream; this kind of juxtaposition still seems to me the most fruitful form of Shakespearean distortion. But in fact very little damage is done to the values of the play, and its language of course was forfeit anyway.

Doubts occur when Robert Sturua's production fails to work in its own terms. Queen Margaret as an avenging chorus stage-managing the other characters is a logical extension of her role in the original; the idea falters because she is not very strikingly played. And of course the last third of the play is suffused in irony (hands up, incidentally, anyone who was not relieved at the despatch of the entire Bosworth sequence in 10 minutes), but the smirking clown who personifies fate and retribution is kitsch in any language.

Other arms reach out to me, other eyes smile tenderly, but Georgia remains on my mind.

* * *

The RSC production of The Caucasian Chalk Circle (Warehouse) fails to clear the play's notorious first hurdle: the actors playing peasants debating the proper fate of their valley seem dutiful, but unconvinced. Clive Merrison, as the presiding expert, sounds notably relieved when the play-within-the-play starts up and he inquires nervously as to its length; here at last is a subject he can identify with.

There were times when I shared his apprehension (Brecht liked his audiences to be physically comfortable, and there's small chance of that here), but a mostly excellent company proceed, under John Caird's direction, to give a good relaxed account of the play. Doubling becomes its own pleasure; one particularly looks forward to seeing Kenny Ireland's dim squaddie act or Charlotte Cornwell, flintily correct behind her mask, as the governor's wife. Kenneth Colley is a sharp narrator; Roger Sloman leads the soldiers whose barometrically shifting loyalties are possibly the richest stratum of the play: human nature seen face to ugly face.

Of the two leads Jane Carr's Grusha has a cockney directness that wins many laughs and even, at the end, the odd tear; it only seems out of place in the doggedly convoluted dialogue with which she confronts her lover. Alun Armstrong's Azdak by contrast is northern-urban: too lightweight to carry the philosopher side of the role (I remember Leo McKern on television becoming, orientally contemplative), but professionally adept in the clowning. The judicial topsy-turviness is a delight and he charts a remarkably clear course through the tugs of conscience that eventually determine Grusha's fate and his own. Stephen Oliver's melodious score adds considerably to a notably undogmatic evening.

* * *

It has been a musical week. Oklahoma! (Haymarket, Leicester) is in a sense beyond criticism. One would be hard put to judge the merits of these songs; they are a part of life. Still, at least one of Rodgers's tunes (the waltz, Out of My Dreams) is a masterpiece in or out of the theatre, and none is ineffective. Hammerstein's lyrics seem, to a late believer, perfect.

What gives the show theatrical body is the theme behind the love story. A cowboy is united with a farmhouse heiress, as the rivalry that breaks out in the square-dance brawl ("the farmer and the cowman should be friends") gives way to the irresistible affirmation of the title-song. Performance of this has become stereotyped; the waving wheat can sure smell sweet, but do all arms still have to sweep up every time it's mentioned?

There is still something tentative about the production (which begins its national tour this week at the New Theatre, Oxford): is Curly telling himself that it's a beautiful mornin', or Aunt Eller (good old Madge Ryan) who's on stage with him, or the audience? John Diedrich, though he ripens considerably later on, seems uncertain. Laurey, the heroine, is very English, the ballet-dancers' lumbering (though the bucolic numbers are fun) and though Alfred Molina acts, sings and dances well, he cannot explain, despite some Steigerish inflections, why Jud Fry is supposed to be so instantly dislikeable.

Not all the anecdotes assembled by Caryl Brahms and Ned Sherrin can fend off a growing suspicion that Beecham (Apollo) was a pill, appealing to the same English philistinism he professed to deride. I also doubt if his verbal timing was as good as Timothy West's; I sometimes wondered if anybody's ever was. This immaculate performance dusts Paul Scofield's Salieri as the best piece of stand-up comedy on the London stage. Two moments at the end are very touching: the conductor's statement at a children's concert that Mozart wrote the ensuing piece "when he was about your age, sir" (there is no catch in the voice, but suddenly gravity descends) and the sight of his hands fluttering professionally as the lights finally go down.
