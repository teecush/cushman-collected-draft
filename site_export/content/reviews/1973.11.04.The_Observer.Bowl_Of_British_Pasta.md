I discovered last week that it is possible to enjoy oneself immensely, but not quite whole-heartedly. Eduardo de Filippo's *Saturday, Sunday, Monday* is the latest of the National Theatre's forays into an alien comic style. Paris (*A Flea in Her Ear*) and Chicago (*The Front Page*) have both fallen to this company, but Naples, as might have been expected, put up a tougher resistance. The terrain is less familiar and not even the recruitment of an Italian to direct the operation could solve all the problems.

Franco Zeffirelli is, by instinct and practice, a carnival director; he is apt to sign his work (as he does here) with a flamboyantly irresistible curtain-call, and he can imbue British players with an extrovert spirit which a British audience will immediately dub Italian. But Eduardo de Filippo, as several fine critics have testified and as we could see for ourselves when he brought his company to the Aldwych last year, is the soberest of comedians. He has a gift for simply existing on a stage, with minimum effort and maximum intensity, which no British actor can match. (Alan Webb, I think, comes nearest.) His plays may be Neapolitan family comedies, but they are not soap-operas; they have an acrid tang to them and I call to witness not only my memories of the black-market satire in which London saw him, but assorted comments in the Old Vic programme concerning the present piece.

It treats of a weekend in which a marriage and a family come near to breaking-point. The husband, a shopkeeper preoccupied with business, accuses his wife of infidelity; as Sunday fades into Monday he is made to realise that her supposed coolness is the reflection and the result of his own. The pressures of work leave him only one day a week into which to cram his private life and, not unnaturally, it cracks under the strain. This obsession with work runs through all the men in the family: from grandfather, nostalgic for the hat-trade and twisting every piece of headgear within reach into strange but doubtless therapeutic forms, to his favourite grandson, the proud and cocky proprietor of his own clothes store. The theme is explicitly stated in the play, but it seems bald and unsupported in a production which works far better as a celebration of life than as a critique.

Much of the celebration is centred on food, which is rendered as convincing in sight and smell as could be desired. A ragout is prepared, calamares are anatomised, onions are chopped (we were a hungry, lachrymose bunch in the front stalls); Sunday lunch is magnificently served, and it is only logical that the marital squabble should finally hinge on the disputed merits of a dish of macaroni alla Siciliana. Joan Plowright is the rightful queen of this kitchen and the fine, frayed duel she fights with Frank Finlay gives the evening its substance.

Mr Finlay, in de Filippo's own role, functions as the punchbag to his volatile friends and neighbours; there are many good jokes in this adaptation (by Keith Waterhouse and Willis Hall), but none better or more apposite than Mr Finlay's rebuke to an emotive compatriot: "You're spitting in my soup." Some of the wives he crosses are exceptionally live: Laurence Olivier, of course, plunging in and out of his own world as granddad; Nicholas Clay as the unquenchable infant capitalist; Denis Quilley, who looks continually on the brink of song; and, the choicest of all, Anna Carteret as an urchin-cropped maid, downtrodden, tearful and strangely resilient. Perhaps she glories in the possession of such a brother as Desmond McNamara, the ultimate skinhead, whose particular foible (baring his pate to all-comers as if it were a challenge) is integrated into the action by the hand of a master dramatist.

If this review has turned into a parade of National Theatre favourites it is merely reflecting the production; there are perilous moments at which the main interest lies in anticipating the effect of Italian vowels upon the next familiar voice. Weak performances here induce a kind of vertigo, and much of the enclosed family feeling has disappeared through the open spaces of Zeffirelli's inexplicable set. What survives, and places the show among the finest as well as the funniest in London, is a humanity and generosity comparable to that radiated by de Filippo's own troupe.

Our native family drama, David Storey's *The Farm* (now at the Mayfair) is pinched and jaded by comparison, despite its incidental humours and well-managed scene a faire; while the new American musical, *Pippin* (Her Majesty's) is merely loathsome. It does not lack talent. Tony Walton's sets, amusing in themselves, bring out the best in Bob Fosse's direction. A bolt of green silk billowing down from the flies, a double file of spotlights to simulate a curtain, or a talking head upon a battlefield, stimulate Mr Fosse to magical feats. But the show, having got its mileage out of magic, in the end professes to discard it; having gained its effects by split-second professionalism it makes a great parade of improvisation, also rehearsed to the last split second. The script sneers at the appeal of lovable stage children, brings one on (complete with animal), and exploits him, then sneers again for luck. The music and lyrics, as full of sunshine as a cereal commercial, refute the canard that after his score for *Godspell*, Stephen Schwartz had nowhere to go but up. As Pippin (mythical son of a pantomime Charlemagne, but bearing a strong resemblance to the timeless, witless hero of an Anthony Newley show) Paul Jones is likeable, but inexpert; Northern J. Calloway as an omnipresent evil genius is precisely the reverse. The authors have things to say about War, Revolution and Sex; none of it explains their choice of a mock-medieval setting.

Two plates of goulash: Molnar's *The Wolf*, over which I enthused at Oxford, has come to the Apollo; while a modern Hungarian play, Istvan Orkeny's *Catsplay*, serves at Greenwich as a vehicle for Elisabeth Bergner who, within a limited range, is an enchantress without flaw. She is a mistress of direct address, a necessary attribute for this play, in which she bends all ears to the tale of her one great love. She is, in fact, one of those suicidally dependent heroines from whom, it is to be hoped, Women's Lib may one day deliver us. Close of play finds her on all fours, miaowing, while Margaret Rawlings, hitherto immobilised and stately, unbends as far as is physically possible in a wheelchair and confesses that she has wet her knickers. This has been acclaimed as a great comic moment. Maybe I'm hard to please.

I could even resist Michael Elliott's largely impeccable staging of T. S. Eliot's *The Family Reunion* (69 Company, Manchester). The chorus of uncles and aunts were convincing in isolation and impressive in unison; the Eumenides, supposedly unstageable, were truly terrifying. Mr Elliott, greatly aided by his designer, Laurie Dennett, and the arena stage of his inviting temporary playhouse in the Royal Exchange, proved the play to be theatrical. What he could not do, despite the surly passion of Edward Fox's country-house Orestes, was to pierce the mists of its narrative of guilt once removed and render it dramatic.
