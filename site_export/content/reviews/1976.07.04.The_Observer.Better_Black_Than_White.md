PETER SHAFFER'S Black Comedy, revived at the Shaw, is the theatrical equivalent of the Marx Brothers' stateroom scene in A Night at the Opera: a single mechanical joke indefinitely prolonged and becoming more irresistible every second. Mr Shaffer is a conscientious builder and both his blueprint and his construction are, on this occasion, immaculate. Black Comedy is probably the only classic farce written in England since the war.

To the initial premise - pitch darkness to the characters is brilliant light to the audience, and vice versa, and all stations in between - is added farce's essential staple: the hero to whom all this could not have happened at a worse moment.

Young Brindsley is faced with a power cut, a roomful of sculpture waiting to be viewed and hopefully bought by a deaf millionaire, the debbiest of fiancees to be kept sweet, her military martinet father to be impressed, expensive furniture borrowed to this end from the flat across the passage without the knowledge of its fanatically possessive possessor, the sudden return of said owner necessitating the return of the furniture before the darkness ends and he finds out, and the irruption of Brindsley's ex-bird, to whom most of this, especially the fiancee part, comes as a surprise. Mr Shaffer's deployment of these varied forces is masterly.

Paul Giovanni's production, which is more notable for spurts of energy than for overall rhythm, will not altogether satisfy those who remember John Dexter's original; though it is of course doubtful whether anyone who saw that production will ever be really pleased by anything else ever again. Celia Bannerman as Clea is a bit too self-conscious about her charming candour to lay the ghost of Maggie Smith, though Gemma Craven's deb is daintily determined enough to take on the shade of Louise Purnell, whose delighted trill of "Sexipegs!" still echoes down the years. Peter Machin as Brin keeps the engine running, but his chirpy demeanour has more to do with the manner thought proper to a light comedian of the sixties than with the reactions of this person in this play. But nobody is actually bad; and both the play and its characters emerge as the most credible and engaging Mr Shaffer has created.

Apart from Black Comedy, which is only half an evening, this has been the direst theatrical week my fairly young remembrance can furnish. The rot started early with Mr Shaffer's curtain-raiser, White Liars, professedly re-written since its first London appearance. The most interesting addition, in view of the long line of male attachments in Mr Shaffer's plays, is the explicit acknowledgment that this one really is homosexual. The most valuable subtraction is the disembodied voice with which the fortune-telling Baroness, who governs the play, was wont to hold interminable conversations.

The theme remains the same. All three characters are living protective lies: the Baroness is no Baroness, the Northern prole pop-singer is middle class and southern, the manager who claims to have created him is an emotional parasite. The play was planned to complement Black Comedy (which contains a helpful line - helpful since otherwise we wouldn't have noticed - about a darkened room acting as a lie-detector) and it also of course resembles The Iceman Cometh. Mr Shaffer seems more optimistic than O'Neill about man's capacity to survive the death of his illusions, which is cheering, but perhaps a dramatist needs to treat this theme at four hours' length if he is to quell our desire to know what else might be new. The play suffers, like many of Mr Shaffer's, from its failure to find a believable idiom, especially for its young: Timothy Dalton as the singer is wet, but he is not helped by his author or by Mr Giovanni, who lowers the lights on him and leaves him to soliloquise.

* * *

Rainer Werner Fassbinder's reputation is doubtless exaggerated, like all reputations, but I was enough affected by it to expect more from The Bitter Tears of Petra von Kant (Theatre at New End) than a boudoir weepie. Petra is a rich fashion designer spurned by her lover, who happens to be a girl; diligently I searched for irony, but found only stifling indulgence. (It was a stifling night and the New End is not air-conditioned; the heat-wave may asphyxiate the fringe.) I suppose the acting may have been intended ironically; Delphine Seyrig's English is very clear, but she gets the inflections wrong - as, with even deadlier effect, does everybody else in the cast except for Angela Pleasence, who is smoulderingly silent and for whom bully; seeing as those who speak have to say "certainly I can well understand your bitterness" or even, unanswerably I suppose, "he stank of man as only men can stink." The production by Robert Walker is not good.

* * *

The only fitting phrase for Hanratty In Hell was coined many years ago, at the opposite climatic extreme, by W. C. Fields: "Tain't a fit night out for man nor beast." It is Charles Marowitz's last production at the Open Space, before moving on to other premises and to a programme that will abandon a policy of plays in favour of unspecified company work. This is a promise that Mr Marowitz keeps making, but maybe this time he means it; his treatment of texts is, in general, poundingly inept, but Brian Carr's piece deserves the worst he can do to it and may be meant as a valediction.

Mr Carr assumes Hanratty to have been wrongly convicted of the A6 murder and begins with some laboured joking about his status as an innocent in hell. We then shift, unexplained, back to earth and back in time for a reconstruction of the police investigation, with the coppers humorously renamed Kray, with methods to match. I am prepared to believe the worst of anyone, but I want more than just assertion; so apparently do Ronald Lewis as the super and Ken Parry as Lord Longford (another gas), since they experienced great difficulty in getting Mr Carr's words out of their mouths. Valerie Storie is also portrayed; jumping out of her wheelchair with joy when she gets the wrong man sent down. Whatever else may be in dispute, nobody has ever questioned that Miss Storie was raped and was crippled; this does not make her a saint, but it makes one reluctant to laugh at her just for kicks. The play's choice of culprit is Peter Alphon, who repeatedly confessed and withdrew his confession. It ends up telling us where he now lives, presumably so that the lynch-mobs will know where to go.
