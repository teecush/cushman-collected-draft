Claudius the good

IN CHOOSING the Emperor Claudius as the protagonist of a pair of historical novels Robert Graves did something brilliantly perverse. The Roman historians have left little evidence of anything particularly interesting about Claudius. The chronicles make him out the least colourful of the Caesars; considering how lurid the others were this may be to Claudius's credit. It takes a novelist as clever as Graves to see the usefulness of a historical nonentity like Claudius--a blank sheet of paper on which the writer can scrawl whatever he likes. Graves's inscriptions have proved ineffaceable.

Doing Roman history at school I found myself glaring at the textbooks with exasperation, even resentment. How could they have got everything so wrong? If only they'd read I, Claudius they would have known what the people and events were really like. They would have realised that Claudius was no weakling but a canny and compassionate man, biding his time and trying to stay alive. And why should the professors worry about Claudius's contemporaries? It's all there in his memoirs, so meticulously ghosted by Graves.

The historical Claudius may have seemed recalcitrant material for a novel, but just try turning the fictional Claudius into drama. The film never got made. A West End theatre version raced aimlessly and incomprehensibly through random bits of the action. The television treatment takes its time and is enjoyable but it has not avoided the obvious stumbling-blocks. I doubt whether anyone--including the creators themselves--ever expected it to.

When they get to the second book, Claudius the God, the situation may be different, for that will show us Claudius as emperor, an actor as well as an observer. But as I write we are still on I, Claudius and there is no way--at least none has been found--of preventing that title from seeming a misnomer. For the action seems to concern everyone except Claudius himself. He claims the titular position not through his deeds but through his words, uttered many years after the events of which they speak.

This reminiscing Claudius is the only real character in the book; the others, including the narrator's younger self, are sketches. Their lives are narrated with earnest irony--and it is the irony far more than the lives (or, even, the deaths) that registers.

Television has settled for introducing each episode with a glimpse of old Claudius at work on his history (or at least wondering where he left the manuscript) and then more or less abandoning him--Claudius Interruptus. The bulk of the story is left to stand, not as a record of his impressions, but as history and drama in its own right. And here enormous difficulties arise, for Graves's novel provides, by design, a partial view of history and is almost indifferent to drama; though it is certainly full of events--battles, murders, conspiracies and sexual adventures ranging from simple adulteries to complex orgies--that could be described as dramatic.

Keeping the book's irreverence and informality

A reviewer on Radio 4's Kaleidoscope declared that Jack Pulman, the adaptor, could hardly have used Graves's dialogue since actors would have found it unspeakable. He proceeded to quote a line or two that should have given no ordinarily competent actor any trouble at all. The real problem with the novel's dialogue, from a dramatist's point of view, is merely that there is so little of it. The first half of the book contains only one conversation of any length: a philosophical discussion on the duties of the historian. When the characters are doing things--important things, even lurid things--they keep their mouths shut.

Mr Pulman has accordingly had to invent a great deal; sometimes--as in the episode of Piso's trial for the murder of Germanicus--extracting 50 minutes of screen time from two or three pages of narrative. In his invention he has obviously kept in mind the book's reputation for irreverence and informality, but his back-slapping joviality is an equivalent for the dry-biscuit wit of the novel. The actors have been faced with much the same problem; in reading we can accept Augustus described simply as "an overgrown boy". But, when Brian Blessed comes before us with the same interpretation, and we are asked actually to look, listen and accept him as the first Roman Emperor, the whole thing becomes a charade.

Siân Phillips as Livia: an artist in murder

That said, I found the Blessed Augustus endearing and at the approach of death (after which he became the Divine Augustus) honestly touching, like a puzzled teddy-bear. The performer who has done most to make the character her own (and it seems to me the actress's achievement rather than the adaptor's) is Siân Phillips as the imperial multiple murderess Livia. She is a creamier, wittier fiend than the book suggests, an artist in murder rather than a conscientious craftswoman; but Miss Phillips's handling of Mr Pulman's fairly shameless gags (one borrowed, presumably by augury, from Mae West) and her taunting of Derek Jacobi's stammering Clau-Clau-Claudius, made one disinclined to quibble. Jacobi has so far turned in two winning impersonations of the young and the old Claudius; it will be interesting to see them meet in the middle. And I liked the playing of the young Caligula as a malevolent Christopher Robin. But all Claudius's relations are becoming difficult to distinguish before they are assassinated. I wonder how those who haven't read the book are faring with the complexities of the family relationships.

Herbert Wise's direction has been excellent. The absence of film is a positive virtue. Its presence would suggest authenticity--and that would never do. The serial is fun but, unlike the books, it will never replace history.

Robert Cushman is drama critic of the Observer.
