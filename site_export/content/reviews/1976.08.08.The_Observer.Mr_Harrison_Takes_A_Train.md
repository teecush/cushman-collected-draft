Mr. Harrison takes a train

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

The day after seeing M Perrichon's Travels (Chichester) I snatched a copy of The Selective Ego and was rewarded by the discovery that James Agate had been presented with a copy of this very play when about to board a train for Southampton, en route for New York. He did not, apparently, say much in the way of thank you, being mindful of M. Perrichon's odious habit of making speeches on railway stations, something about which Mme Perrichon had to speak to him severely.

Fate having made me a present of this information, I thought it only fair to pass it on. Anyway, it raises two points of interest. One: the incident occurred in 1937 when Labiche's comedy, written in 1860, was apparently a quotable classic. It was not, however, given an English production until last Tuesday. Two: now that it has reached us, the railway station seems almost its main reason for existence.

The director, Patrick Garland, and his designer, Eileen Diss, have expended enormous effort on re-creating for us the Gare de Lyon, where M. Perrichon's travels begin. Upstage a facade is projected; downstage arches are erected; smoke voluminously billows at the moment of departure; and Bill Fraser, our theatre's prime representative of bilious uniformed officialdom, slowly and resentfully combines the roles of porter and ticket-salesman, thereby occasioning factotum jokes which Labiche never invented but should have.

Mr Fraser's presence, together with that of a brass band to play us in (was the Gare de Lyon really such a fun-palace?), recalls the National Theatre's version of that other picaresque comedy The Captain of Kopenick. Mr Garland's production suffers in the comparison, since the play belies its title; having made such a fuss over dispatching Perrichon from Paris it follows him to Switzerland (land of merry alpenhorns and gemutlich hoteliers) for one scene only, then gets him home and belatedly settles into situation farce.

There is a continuous plot-line, concerning the efforts of two fairly indistinguishable young men to win Perrichon's daughter, but it hardly matters. What does matter, and might unify the evening, is Perrichon himself, a successful coach-builder who, as one of his employees cantankerously remarks, can ride in his own coach, to such a pass has French society now come. Perrichon, in other words, is a type of the emergent bourgeois, and at Chichester he is played by Rex Harrison, an actor who represents the bourgeoisie after a comfortable century in the saddle.

When buying his ticket for Switzerland, Mr Harrison tells us that he has never travelled before; this is an obvious lie, and Labiche's text compels him to go on fibbing for most of the evening. Perrichon is continually beset by hazards that scare the life out of him, but that Mr Harrison could shrug his way through; as a result the actor goes through the evening looking thoroughly ill-at-ease, but not the right kind of ill-at-ease.

In other roles, Mr Harrison has presented an ironist's complacency; Perrichon's complacency, however, is that of the buffoon, leading him into the solemn verbosity that alerted Agate. If it is his vice, it is also his armour, and it is linked to a meanness that gives the play some distinction as satire. Misadventuring on the Matterhorn, he is rescued from a crevasse by one of his daughter's suitors; a moment later he himself performs a similar service for the other. He much prefers the man he saved to the man who saved him; in gratitude, as in all else, he would rather be a creditor than a debtor.

Maybe this joke could sustain a whole play, but since neither Mr Garland (whose invention runs dangerously low as the evening proceeds) nor Mr Harrison gives it an honest try it is impossible to say. Mr Harrison does have a very touching few moments at the end, when Perrichon is told the truth about himself. Clive Francis, who was good in The Circle a couple of weeks ago, and Andrew Sachs do spirited work as the rivals; and Tony Robinson, suggesting Bob Cratchit gone mouldy a few years after Scrooge's reclamation, is fine as the captious clerk.

* * *

Little birds are bathing
Crocodiles in cream
Like a happy dream.
Like, but not so lasting:
Crocodiles when fasting
Are not all they seem.

That squib of Lewis Carroll's gives a tone as well as a title to Crocodiles In Cream (Mermaid), a one-man show devised by David Horlock, who directs it, and Michael Rothwell, who performs it. Carroll's verse and his fiction are subtly joined to his life and letters (I mean his correspondence) to reveal a man for whom existence was a perpetual jol, every Snark a Boojum.

Insufficient allowance is made, perhaps, for the success of his comic mask; when Mr Rothwell goes into extracts from Alice or recites 'Hiawatha's Photographing' ('From his shoulder Hiawatha/Took the camera of rosewood/Made of sliding, folding rosewood'), he eliminates the breeziness and concentrates on the melancholy. This makes him, of course, a magnificent Mock Turtle, a source of truly beautiful soup; and he holds the stage impressively throughout, with scarcely a moment's boredom.

Carroll cuts a more interesting figure than does the Roman satirist Juvenal in another solo turn, Juvenalia, which has just ended a run at the Bush, but may return; since the actor concerned is named Callow, the possibilities for punning seem limitless. Simon C. delivers the material in the style of a nightclub comic; in one of the dingier boites on the Appian Way, the kind you might drop into for compensation after a gladiatorial show with a low mortality rate. His flashing teeth and glasses, and his transfixingly chatty manner, suggest a Ronnie Corbett grown physically upwards and outwards and dyspeptically in all directions.

He comes out of it very well, better than his author. Juvenal's invective at the state of things in Rome (flattery, nymphomania and atrocious cooking) is pungent all right and if you want to think it topical you may; but it is very generalised. I never believed in the people to whom the satires were addressed (I always used to have difficulty with this particular convention when faced with Latin poetry at school) and I detected no specific concern. Juvenal is always compared with Swift, but his narcissistic vituperation runs the icy passion of the Modest Proposal a very poor second.
