Distant laughter

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on new comedies by Ayckbourn, Frayn and Nichols, plus more O'Neill at the National.

THREE OF our four funniest playwrights had new plays on view in a week less riotously amusing than might have been expected.

Alan Ayckbourn won. His Stephen Joseph Theatre in the Round in Scarborough is one of the most important houses in the country, and his Suburban Strains is his most accomplished piece of staging to date. Two concentric revolves have been installed, the actors generally standing in the inner circle while necessary furnishings travel to them on the outer.

Suburban Strains is a musical, though one in which the songs are the least important part; Mr Ayckbourn's lyrics are well placed and technically sound, but they lack spring, while Paul Todd's music is rather tinkly. But the form itself has released something in the playwright. It takes any clinical curse off his accustomed games with time and space. And though his subject-matter remains much the same as before - loneliness and casual cruelty - the approach has opened up. He has tended before to show us relationships so far gone in decay that the participants are too tired to shift or too blind to notice. Here we see the rot starting, the characters struggling. There are even love-scenes.

The central figure is Caroline, a schoolteacher who flees from marriage with an actor, who messes up both her flat and her life, to an affair with a doctor who tries tyrannically to tidy them up. The treatment is generally chronological, but with leaps; moments from the first relationship are frequently recalled, to be juxtaposed with the second. On the outskirts are grimly cheerful dinner-parties and pep-talks from Caroline's appallingly optimistic newsagent dad, backed - in the happiest musical stroke of the evening - by a trio of OAP customers (including one who regularly thumbs through the girlie mags before settling for the Radio Times).

The story is too drawn out (and it was surely a mistake to have the doctor's estranged wife warn Caroline what he would do to her, rather than letting her and us discover for ourselves), but both the dialogue and the observation are unflagging. Lavinia Bertram is sweet and spirited as Caroline, and Alison Skilbeck - a regular Scarborough ornament - delivers, punctually, a varied quartet of Mr Ayckbourn's saddest sacks.

* * *

A government inspector arrives at the end of Michael Frayn's Liberty Hall (Greenwich), and the title may also put you in mind of She Stoops to Conquer, which is among other things a comedy of class-and-role-reversal. Liberty Hall ends with a positive riot of this, a Feast of Misrule: pure comedy. That's the theory, anyway.

The reversal that underlies all the others is based on an historical fancy; we are asked to assume that the 1917 Revolution happened not in Russia but in Britain. Twenty years on, Balmoral Castle is a holiday-home for State-approved writers. Four are in residence; their names are Warwick Deeping, Enid Blyton, Godfrey Winn and Hugh Walpole. Walpole, unpopular with the others for having grabbed the only warm room, expires and must be replaced so that he may be interviewed by a visiting representative of the Tsarist Press.

The entire Balmoral staff - consisting of a butler named McNab - is pressed into impersonating him. Rikki Fulton plays both Walpole and McNab, and the substitution, when as far as we can see the dead body is still on stage with its legs protruding from a cabin-trunk, is wondrously well managed in Alan Dosser's production. McNab, glazed, drunken, and blinking behind Walpole's thick glasses, proceeds to take full advantage of his new position; and Mr Fulton, with much assistance from his author, takes full advantage of McNab.

The conventions of stage disguise are wittily spoofed as the new Hugh relates just what he said to his butlering self at breakfast that morning. This middle section of the play is golden, but the sequel is forced and the preamble protracted. The mechanism hangs heavy; it is difficult to believe in science-fiction when it already hasn't happened.

Besides, the three supporting writers become positively irritating, so little is done with them; and it is far from clear why they participate in the charade, since they all dislike the Balmoral Warden, whose face they are being asked to save. He too is kept around with nothing much to do until the end; at which point George Cole does his considerable best with what is, by now, mechanical writing. Oliver Cotton has a stylish go at the Russian, and Jill Meager a bright one at his conscientious young escort from the Board of Trade.

* * *

Peter Nichols's Born in the Gardens (Globe) is, as its author wryly acknowledges en route, a state-of-England play. When done last year at the Bristol Old Vic it was also a state-of-Bristol play, and this was the aspect on which the local audience seized most enthusiastically and infectiously. For its London transfer the piece has understandably been trimmed of many of these references, but the unfortunate result has been to leave both play and audience in limbo.

This is the one in which brother Hedley, a Labour backbencher, and sister Queenie, now a Californian journalist, return to persuade other brother Mo and mother Maud out of their shared rut. Mo and Maud live in a Tudor mansion; she talks to the television and about the Michaelwave oven, he to his cat and about jazz. Their plight is compared to that of Alfred, Bristol Zoo's home-grown gorilla, who died happily in captivity.

I am less sure than everyone else seems to be that Mr Nichols approves of his characters' obstinate desire to be left to a similar fate; Hedley, the welfare statesman, facing similar defeats at home and at large ('offer them heaven on earth and they throw it back in your face') seems to me a character drawn with both sympathy and wit. But the conclusion is long foregone and this (certainly at a second viewing) weighs the play down; it has to subsist on Maud's eccentricities, which become mere running gags. Beryl Reid runs them.

* * *

For all they are worth, I thought her extravagant in Bristol, but I was probably wrong. Anyway she's perfect now. Barry Foster, in an unusual role for him, and Peter Bowles, in a usual one, are, as before, excellent.

The last time I saw The Iceman Cometh (which he doth again soon) I felt this was the underside of Runyon's underworld: bare bums. Hughie, the latest and briefest instalment in the Cottesloe's run-up to that marathon pub-crawl, brings the comparison more firmly home. Hughie is a deceased hotel night-clerk; Erie Smith - small-time gambler, small-time everything - pours out his heart to Hughie's taciturn successor, boasting not just about his past triumphs (illusory, we assume), but about his past boasts. He patronised Hughie, his one-time audience, but obviously was in piteous need of him: so much becomes rapidly, subtly clear.

This is a rich little piece, full of atmosphere, if lacking as always in verbal zing. ('When I say she was plain I'm giving her all the breaks,' the best O'Neill can do, places him pretty low in the ranks of New York humourists.) Stacy Keach's Erie cannot be faulted except for its carefulness; you expect a visiting American star to be a bigger spender. Hayden Griffin has designed a marvellously dispirited hotel lobby; Howard Goorney's mousy clerk looks like part of the furniture. He seems less at home when he opens his mouth, but that doesn't happen often.

More male camaraderie, much louder, in David Williamson's The Club (Hampstead), which is about backroom intrigues in Australian football. There is a funny but irrelevant pot-smoking scene. Ron Haddrick, as the unwary smoker, gives a performance of some distinction; the best you can say of the other bullies is that they're forceful.
