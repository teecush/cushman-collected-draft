IF THE author of The Wood Demon (Lyceum, Edinburgh) will just step along to my room, I shall explain to him how he may reassemble the elements of his immoderately exhilarating play into not just one masterpiece, as he might expect, but four. I shall do this (the author, one Chekhov, being safely dead) with the aid of an invaluable critical tool known as Hindsight.

In this Actors' Company production The Wood Demon, previously regarded only as a rough draft for Uncle Vanya, stands revealed as the great Chekhovian seed-bed. It foreshadows The Seagull in its alphabetical progression of broken hearts (A loves B who actually prefers C who is languishing for D); the luncheon party at which these emotional tangles are revealed to us looks like a trial run for the first act of Three Sisters. As for Dr Kruschov, the wood demon himself, his passion for forest preservation inaugurates the symbolic pattern that was to be completed with the laying of the first axe to that cherry orchard in which all Russia was prefigured.

But the play also looks backward, to the one-act farces on which Chekhov's playwriting skills were originally honed. The blustering soldier Theodore (who afterwards merged with the wood demon to produce Astrov, the hard-drinking medico-ecologist of Vanya) is a descendant of the tactless wooers of The Bear and The Proposal. There is nobody in the mature plays anything like as funny--or so I thought as I watched Robin Ellis, striking classical poses in bushy beard and blanket, and radically extending my view both of his range and of Chekhov's.

If the comedy in this play is more broadly conceived than in Vanya, the tragedy is more compressed, but also more direct. Where Uncle Vanya, at the climax of his desperation, tries unsuccessfully to shoot the Professor who has exploited him, his prototype here, Uncle George, tries successfully to shoot himself. Vanya spends the last act of his play in a hideous awareness of futility; George spends it in his grave, virtually forgotten by those who survived him. The play moves into a vein of romantic comedy; two elusive couples are hurriedly brought together at the final curtain. In David Giles's production these pairings are uproarious and slightly unreal, like Moliere's happy endings. Suddenly we remember that George is dead, and that the Professor's wife, Helen, who in this play makes a half-hearted attempt to escape her husband, has gone with docility back to her fate. Kruschov's diatribes (which included a generous measure of self-hatred) against the surrounding second-rateness ring ironically when we see him abruptly dismissed to happiness.

The irony is, of course, refined by our memories of the later play, which we can hardly expunge. They are actually encouraged by the quality of this production, the first Chekhov I have seen to approach the National Theatre Vanya of a decade ago. To watch the shooting scene in that production, or the reconciliation of Helen and Sonya, was like assisting at the realisation of a myth. In The Wood Demon the same episodes are played out almost word for word, but the diffuseness of the surrounding action prevents them from having a comparable effect.

However, if this dramatic powerhouse, which fuelled Chekhov for the rest of his career, has too much energy for its own immediate purposes, that is hardly a fault at which to repine--not, at any rate, with this cast. Tenniel Evans's hollering Uncle, Ian McKellen's mercurial forest-freak, and Robert Eddison's unreachable Professor constitute, with Mr Ellis's grandiloquent Cossack, as formidable a male quartet as I remember; John Woodvine, suave on the sidelines, comes in a good fifth. The girls are less impressive, but the sharp corners of action and dialogue (strongly emphasised in Ronald Hingley's translation) are negotiated with dashing adroitness. The craft of Mr Giles's direction is impeccable, but it is the performances that register.

Our National Theatre recently announced the engagement of half a dozen associate directors, with barely a word of who is to do the acting. The attitude this represents is now the theatrical norm; with this production, a year after their inception, the Actors' Company acquire the authority to supply a necessary corrective.

The other main Festival offering this week has been a version of Buchner's Woyzeck, offered matinees only in the Assembly Hall by the Young Lyceum Company, the junior branch of the Edinburgh Rep. Since Buchner himself died at 23, play and players might seem excellently matched, but Woyzeck is in spirit the oldest young play ever penned, and its ashen disillusion is beyond these actors' reach. As individuals, that is; when they function as anonymous members of a unit under the Romanian director, Radu Penciulescu, they can be numbingly impressive.

Early on, Mr Penciulescu has them lie down all over the stage, the field of the living dead. The only two actors left upright step among them, swapping images of gloom. As their conversation ends, drums sound: the prostrate cast beat an accelerating tattoo on the floor. For this effect, I felt I could forgive much; but as the afternoon ground on I began to repent of my charity. "Not so fast, Woyzeck," says the tyrant Captain at the start of the play, but he seems to have dictated the pacing throughout.

Buchner leads naturally (as he did historically) to early Brecht. Drums in the Night, poor B.B.'s second play, has been staged at the Traverse, whence it will shortly transfer to the Hampstead Theatre Club. I recommend it, though not without misgivings. Roland Rees's production seems imbued initially with the playful spirit which Brecht demanded but rarely obtains and which, if anything, intensifies his savagery. That savagery was still nihilistically indiscriminate when he wrote Drums; only when he revised it in East Berlin after the war did he point it in an approved political direction. But Brecht's second thoughts are not in the same league as Chekhov's. C. P. Taylor has adapted the play by following in the master's revisionist footsteps and then imprinting a few of his own, but thinking to sharpen the play's claws, he has actually pared them. There are residual ironies in the text which the relentlessly partisan production refuses to countenance; the acting meanwhile grows increasingly strident.

Dear old Uncle George attributed his downfall to his sense of irony; the contemporary theatre seems in little danger of succumbing to the same disease. Michael Almaz's Chicago Sunday School for Anarchists (Poole Theatre, lunchtime) is a possible exception; I assume Mr Almaz intends his schoolmistress with her dreams of dynamite (a left-wing Jean Brodie, or so it seems in Edinburgh) to share the responsibility for disaster with the fascist cop who opposes her.

The Poole and the Traverse are Edinburgh fixtures. The transient bulk of the Festival fringe is suffering this year from a bad attack of the Christopher Robins; everybody has gone down with Alice. Dodging all the plays by, from or about Lewis Carroll wound up with the Brontes. For the second time this year they failed to hold me past the interval, but Richard Crane's Thunder (Bradford University Drama Group), though fearfully overlong, has some good jokes and this time I forsook the sisterhood, if without regret, then also without malice.
