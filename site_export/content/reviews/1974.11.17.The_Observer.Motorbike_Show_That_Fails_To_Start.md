Motorbike show that fails to start

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

With the rock musical virtually defunct, it seems gratuitously unkind of the Royal Court to offer us the Tokyo Kid Brothers in The City. This entertainment might reasonably be described as outlandish; it is surprising, at first blush, to hear Westernised pop belted at us in Japanese accents. But that first blush is the only one; it is for the most part an extremely pallid evening.

Accents apart, we have heard it all before. Come to think of it we have heard it all before, accents included. As far back as South Pacific we were thrilling to the titillating sound of the Oriental lisp. There was even a twenties hit called Nagasaki, whose lyrics ("Hot ginger and dynamite, There's nothing but that at night") sound kind of uncomfortable now. I have to report that the most appealing moment in the present show is provided by a Tokyo Kid Sister shyly confiding, "I think it would be fun to rip off a motorbike." How you say?

To be fair, the show is not always as ingenuous as that. The Tokyo Kid Brothers proclaim themselves to be the children of a Japanese culture fostered by American occupation; they listen to rock, eat Kentucky Fried Chicken, ride motorbikes. That last is most important; the show is subtitled "a motorcycle musical." It could conceivably be the aim of the Brothers - and even more of the Royal Court - to offer us an oblique but devastating view of our own values by showing us the effect they have had on alien lives.

Approached from this angle, however, the show is simply confusing. Its attitude to fried chicken is indeed somewhat caustic; it is remarkable how swiftly Colonel Sanders has come to be accepted as shorthand for imperialism. But rock and motorbikes are equally products of a commercial civilisation, and these The City celebrates.

Actually it is nearly crushed between them; what with the backing-group giving out upstage and Hondas and Suzukis lying around downstage, the cast have very little room to manoeuvre. For much of the time they are occupied performing characterless dance routines (as nearly static as choreography could be) to equally anonymous music, and performing them so stiffly that I could almost have sworn they were English.

The first of these numbers - an opening celebration by a motorbiking commune living just outside Tokyo - is interrupted by a couple of baton-swinging cops (the villains; they later double as the Kentucky chicken merchants) determined to put the kids in their place; I thought I heard the word "punks." (Half the dialogue is in Japanese, half in English.) The echoes of West Side Story are quite deafening, but in its later stages the show takes on a closer resemblance to Hair.

The kinship is particularly noticeable in the thinness - amounting nearly to invisibility - of the story-line and in the self-regarding sentimentality of the general conception. The land which the gang occupy is destined to be the site of a housing complex, an object which the bikers, opposed to all forms of mechanisation (with one rather obvious exception), dismiss as a "computer city." They want to protest; and one of them does so by riding his machine full-tilt against a newly erected barricade. He kills himself in the process, which is very sad since not 20 minutes before he has palpitatingly found Love.

A guard delivers a long and tearful monologue disclaiming responsibility; we must not assume, just because he built a barrier that he wanted young heroes to go dashing themselves against it. I believed him, but his extreme volubility lost him my sympathy. There is a great deal of violence in the show, and the Court may conceivably be hoping to siphon off some of the Kung Fu audience. Those who think fifth-rate musicals are redeemed by social comment, faraway places and strange-sounding names should also have a good time.

* * *

I cannot think whom to recommend to see The Highwayman (Roundhouse), despite its undoubted historic interest. This is the first English production of Schiller's Die Rauber, first published in 1781 and a seminal Romantic work. It concerns two brothers, one of whom, naturally base, contrives that his virtuous sibling shall be disinherited, said brother thereupon taking philosophically (everything in this play is done philosophically) to a life of crime. He proves in fact a singularly benevolent bandit, but Remorse weighs him down just the same.

The brothers never appear together; they may therefore be played by the same actor, in this case Derren Nesbitt, who manages a dual performance of staggering dullness. He is slightly more bearable as the good brother; the bad one is a cripple and his affliction elicits from Mr Nesbitt an unconscionable amount of plodding and grunting. He also has a nasty habit of nudging people with his hump.

Here, however, I must cease berating the actor and turn to the director. Hovhannes I. Pilikian's production is unbelievably graceless. Backs are slapped, fists clenched, elbows rubbed, laps fondled; the director appears to know no other way of establishing relationships between characters and he makes his actors look ridiculous. They are not safe even when dead; ramp-shaped traps open to receive them, and it is a pretty sight to see a performer in his final throes carefully rolling himself into the required position.

Mr Pilikian, who gave us such a rollicking production of Oedipus at Chichester, may possibly be out to prove that Schiller, like Sophocles, was a farceur under the skin (he mutters something in a programme note about the Theatre of the Absurd), but, like so many directors who reach about comedy, he lacks any recognisable sense of humour; and in this play, where melodrama (some of it rather stirring) is liable to topple into burlesque even without prodding, such a lack is fatal.

The leading actress, continually defending her virtue, is given a terrible time; not least by the translator, who requires her to say "I feel as strong as a horse" after niftily disarming her persecutor. Other characters litter the stage by tossing down books or papers in moments of passion. It is altogether an untidy occasion.
