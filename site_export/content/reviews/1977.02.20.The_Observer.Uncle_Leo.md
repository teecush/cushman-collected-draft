Uncle Leo

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Chekhov in Manchester and 'Spokesong.'

ON the subject of Uncle Vanya I make no claim to be trustworthy. The great production of 1963 -- the Olivier-Redgrave-Plowright- and everyone-else version -- is generally agreed to have been definitive, and it caught me at an age too impressionable for me to entertain the possibility of its ever being equalled. I am therefore predisposed to underrate any new production; but I am also liable to make considerable allowances. Having seen the play so intensely lived, I still carry some of its electricity with me, and plug in my memories whenever other interpreters' batteries are running low. I may be disappointed, but I am never likely to be bored.

Michael Elliott's production at the Royal Exchange, Manchester, runs true to form. Observant and intelligent but uninvolving, it never made me laugh and so could hardly expect to make me cry. But if it is not really funny, it is sometimes amusing, and if not moving, it is often, at any rate, touching -- largely due to Leo McKern's rendition of the title role.

The evening is particularly strong on silent suffering; this is a play in which characters continually and painfully blunder into the truth. They learn from the lips of sympathetically disposed third persons that life holds no consolation for them. Here their faces fall in time with their hopes, and with barometric precision. With all this clarity of detail the impact of the whole is surprisingly fuzzy.

The play emerges as a pattern of masculine self-pity and feminine endurance. Even Elena's beautiful indolence is a form of stoicism, a device for keeping at bay the embarrassment of human contact. And Eleanor Bron is unexpectedly successful at delineating this defensiveness without patronising it. Only her breakdown, when Vanya's shooting-spree forces her to take notice, fails to convince; she is not a good weeper. Joanna David is a suitably spartan Sonya, though the girls' brief reconciliation, like much else, is only half what it might be (here, more than anywhere else, my memories obtrude).

The elder ladies, Susan Richards and Peggy Thorpe-Bates, offer contrasting and instructive examples of the healing powers of routine.

While they take disappointment for granted, the men cry out against it: even Michael Feast's Waffles, whose moment of recognition (when he overhears himself described as a sponger) is among the most affecting in the play. The monstrous Professor is allowed by Alfred Burke a measure of pity; his plight as he grows old is recognisably a variant on that of Mr McKern's Vanya and Albert Finney's Astrov -- though three growling actors in one production is a lot.

If, however, Vanya and Astrov are brothers under the skin, it helps if the skins are recognisably of the same vintage and Mr Finney is handicapped by his partner's visible 15 years' start. There is great subtlety in this Astrov, particularly in his bushily disfiguring moustache and in the hands which clutch obstinately at the vodka bottle while Sonya lectures him on his drunkenness. There is remarkably little elasticity; his drunken outbursts are compelling but not his ecological passion, and most crucially he fails to plumb the depths of disillusion that should bind him to Vanya at the end.

Even Mr McKern reaches the limits of his range about here; he can present disappointment most poignantly, but he cannot, as Redgrave could, universalise failure. There is a toughness in him not finally to be gainsaid. But he draws a fine straight line between his discovery of Elena in Astrov's embrace, and his explosion when he learns that the professor is about to sell him down the Volga.

Desolation, nothing left to lose, builds into near farcical defiance, and in his stricken face you can see the fuse detonating (at least I could; I doubt if those sitting opposite got as much out of the back of his subtly heaving shoulders). It is good, too, to see a Vanya who has run not merely to seed but to fat, and fine to hear one who gives such value to the man's irony. Perhaps the best thing is the sheer pleasure of seeing him on the stage again. Now how about Falstaff?

* * *

And so to the latest play in the Ulster Cycle. When Stewart Parker's Spokesong opened at the King's Head last year, I read the rapturous reviews with interest, since I remembered it from the Dublin Festival as a play of rueful, agreeable moments, all of them in the first Act. The second had sunk under a story line at once complicated and inconclusive.

Now it has transferred to the Vaudeville, I still feel puzzled. The charm remains; Jimmy Kennedy's music and Mr Parker's lyrics are well-matched. The argument of the play is, to put it very crudely, that bicycles are the opposite of terror. If everybody in Belfast rode a bike, this being apparently Dunlop's own city, there would be no more war.

The author is honest enough to admit that this is not going to happen and that his hero's bike-shop will be blown up like everyone else's, but even the first proposition is excessively winsome. He weaves it in and out, though; the play is well conceived. But it is not well constructed, and the flashbacks across two generations to the Golden Age of Dunlop add weight only in the strictly arithmetical sense.

There are, to agree with everyone, two extremely winning performances. Annabel Leventon shines with common sense as Daisy Bell (the best song is not original). As her part-time mechanic and full-time wooer, Niall Buggy begins with disconcerting coyness, but the arrival of his kid brother, who is even smilier, seems to cure him of that and he rides in a straight commanding line thereafter. Thanks for the Buggy ride; I'll take a rain-check on the cycling.
