Inside the shell of technique

ROBERT CUSHMAN
interviews Ronald Pickup

AT 29, AND AFTER five years' service as the National Theatre's most consistently interesting young actor, Ronald Pickup is to play Oedipus--the Sophoclean one--at the National's new junior school, the Young Vic. He follows "Stomp," which is an indication of a catholic repertory if you like, though both, I suppose, could be described as a sort of atavistic. The presence as Jocasta of Hairy Annabel Leventon may strengthen the link as well as adding a final ingredient to the confusion of ages that must always surround this play. No wonder he had a complex.

National Theatre productions usually rehearse for about two months; Pickup has been at this one for only three and a half weeks, and a month ago he thought he was playing Creon. He is not an obvious choice for either role, and one pauses, in the middle of patting the Young Vic on the back for their enterprising casting, to reflect on how few obvious Pickup parts there are anyway. In a company in which most actors are known quantities, or quickly become so, he has remained remarkably open.

He has some definite, if paradoxical, advantages. His voice is conventionally pleasant, and his appearance, in his own words, "tall and thin and fairly angular." Everybody's idea of a sensitive young actor, in fact. Perhaps the closest he has come to being type-cast was when he took over from Edward Petherbridge (similar in shape, but sardonically individual in utterance) as Guildenstern in Tom Stoppard's long-running Shakespearean jollity. This part being by definition a "tabula rasa," the result should have been blank on blank and very boring. Actually, though, the part lost something in funniness, it gained in what he called "hopelessness." "You have a personality you draw on for every part; without it you're a shell of technical tricks."

Something unique

With Guildenstern one knew, finally, that there was something unique within the shell, even if its precise identity remained as mysterious as ever. Maybe he should play Kafka; certainly he could play Hamlet (Shakespeare's, not Stoppard's, which confusingly enough he has already done) but, then so could anybody, so we might not be much wiser.

Most of his career has been at the National or the Royal Court. "I've been spoiled; I've not known what it was to be out of work." He comes on as fairly equable, even when interviewed in a lunch-break three days away from Oedipus. One index is that he has stayed in the Waterloo Road while so many other bright young talents have moved on disconsolate. Of course he's had the breaks, but between them he has managed to play small parts without looking obtrusively unselfish.

"It is difficult, too, to think of any actor who could have brought such clarity to Rosalind in the all-male As You Like It." This was the only time I have ever been made to believe that Shakespeare's or anyone else's lines really could be made to speak for themselves. This, considering all the other possibilities inherent in the situation, was a triumph of tact.

The obverse of this, too infrequently seen at the National, is a great comic appetite for the mania of self-absorption. I remember him at the Court in a Jacobean farce capering with gleeful solemnity at one side of the stage while another man prepared to impregnate his wife. His lunatically malevolent Don John in Zeffirelli's "Much Ado About Nothing" was a wrong-headed conception carried out with magnificent abandon, a composite of every mad Mediterranean villain ever beheld.

Outrageous things

Another don, Armado in "Love's Labour's Lost" proved initially beyond him. "I started working too early from a physical picture of him," and the portrait which emerged was grave and a little thin. At Olivier's instigation, he added a Spanish accent and the performance flowered. Olivier "dared me to do outrageous things. There's nothing very subtle for example about 'Juno and the Paycock.' My character, Johnny Boyle, exists in a state of permanent paralytic fright. The temptation is to play it down. Olivier pushed me right over the cliff." And this kind of pushing I think he needs.

He received another kind of impetus from Peter Brook, who put him through "ten weeks of tortured work" in the Senecan "Oedipus," making him realise that "when you're totally committed to an idea it breeds relaxation and confidence." His was the account of Oedipus's blinding delivered with a staccato intensity behind which there echoed the crunch of technical barriers falling to the ground.

Now he has to enact the agony he depicted. The circumstances are different of course. Sophocles is a more compassionate dramatist than Seneca, and where Peter Brook scored the story as "a barbaric mass" Roland Joffe, the Young Vic's inordinately youthful director, is aiming for a more recognisable humanity.

The approach is reflected in the choice of translation, the tried, trusted and surprisingly homely W. B. Yeats version. "Homely" was my own diffident description; the new Oedipus briskly informed me that when he read it first he had found it "banal." He added though that further investigation had revealed it to be "urgent and immediate," and apt for this "short, sharp whirlwind of a play."

As for whether he's yet ready for Oedipus--well, when are you the right age for this "marvellous big child who has reached an extraordinary maturity without achieving self-knowledge? If I'm not going to dare now, I might as well pack up. The older you get, the harder it is to begin taking risks."
