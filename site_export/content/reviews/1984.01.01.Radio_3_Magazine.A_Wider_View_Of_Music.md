A Wider View of Music

"There is no such thing as 'serious music' and 'light music' - there is only good music and bad music." Where does this leave the musical? Robert Cushman is in no doubt.

The premise underlying Book Music and Lyrics is that the music of musicals is worth listening to. That may sound obvious; but when our first series was reviewed on Critics' Forum we were informed that such material might be just about acceptable in a late-night slot on Radio Two, but had no place on Radio Three, unless backed up by large slabs of sociological analysis.

This really puzzled me. Either music is worth hearing or it isn't; and if it isn't I don't see that its case can be improved by any number of spoken programme notes. A Richard Rodgers song needs no more apology than a Franz Schubert song. Knowledge cannot create enjoyment, though it can enhance it. That enhancement is part of what these programmes are about. Each individual edition has a theme, and most of them have an argument. But then these are a practical necessity anyway. The modern musical (which, in practice, means mainly the American musical) started gathering momentum around 1920, and since then it has accumulated a huge recorded literature. Choosing from it is a daunting task, and you need some manufactured support just to get through the undergrowth. With a theme I can mitigate the agonies of selection; if I leave out something I love, I can feel that it is, somehow, not my fault. Besides, finding what fits and what doesn't is a fascination to me, and, I hope, to listeners.

One criticism levelled at the first programmes was that I was making distinctions between different lyricists and composers that could only be appreciated by specialists. This is probably true; but then part of our aim is to turn listeners into specialists; to make them pleasurably aware of the differences between one talented and intelligent creator and another. This seems to me the kind of knowledge that is worth having: more so than historical background ("this song was written in the Depression; it is therefore about money") though I enjoy filling that in as well. If I had to defend the presence on Radio Three of a series about musicals I would do so by analogy with jazz. Both are forms with popular roots that still have some contact with everyday pop music. There are jazz standards and show songs that everyone knows, but there are many more that have passed into obscurity and that need explaining. Apart from anything else, the songs in musicals written since 1945 are tightly connected to the plots of the shows and need to have their dramatic context supplied. Even a song as familiar as People Will Say We're in Love gains in interest when you know that it is sung in Oklahoma! by a couple of Western lovers, too proud and shy to admit their feelings. It also helps if you realise that Oscar Hammerstein had spent his life trying to write conversational lyrics that fitted the characters singing them. This - since the work itself is excellent - is the kind of exegesis that belongs on a serious music station; and deserves, I self-interestedly think, as regular exposure as is granted to jazz. Charlie Parker was a fine artist, but there is no way that he was any more important than Irving Berlin (who, if radical chic appeals to you, started life every bit as poor and disadvantaged).

We have some self-imposed rules. We are not Songs from the Shows or Your Hundred Best Tunes; we steer off the best-known songs and we try, whenever possible, to use original cast recordings. These however have only been systematically issued since the 1940s; when dealing with shows written earlier we look either for individual records by original performers or for the completest possible version by an outsider. (This usually means records issued on small labels within the last 20 years.) From these sources we have attempted to create our own re-creations of little-known shows. It is surprising how solid an apparently flimsy number can sound when fitted into its context, even if that context is a frivolous one.

The first programme of the new series is entirely made up of cast recordings which give it, I think, an exciting theatrical flavour. The subject is Devils, Villains and Anti-Heroes. The thesis is that the musical theatre reached maturity partly through treating its rogues and scoundrels as more than plot mechanisms. Brecht and Weill's Macheath (The Threepenny Opera) was the long-distance inspiration; Rodgers and Hart's Pal Joey, Rodgers and Hammerstein's Jud Fry (Oklahoma!) were pioneering examples; Stephen Sondheim's Sweeney Todd, a mass murderer, is the ultimate. But Sweeney is also a figure we identify with; he is the anti-hero as, in a much lighter vein, was the agent con-man of the British show Expresso Bongo (a rare opportunity to hear Paul Scofield in song: Cockney song at that.) The devil-figures, the tempters, are something else, and wonderful insinuating musical subjects; their lineage stretches back to Gershwin's Sportin' Life (Porgy and Bess) and goes forward to the epicene M.C. of Cabaret whose "wilkommen, bienvenue, welcome" takes us back in its milieu and its orchestration to Brecht and Weill.

Programme Two is about Rodgers and Hart, the most fascinating in their checks and balances of all composer-lyricist teams. It concentrates on two periods of their work: the twenties, represented by the yacht-set comedy Heads Up, and the forties, which they inaugurated with Too Many Girls, one of the many musicals dealing with collegiate football. From it came one of the most charming of all love-songs, I Didn't Know What Time It Was. We don't always eschew the familiar, though the recording we have chosen is an unstandardised one, that does more justice than most to the development of Hart's thought-line. It's sung by an actor, Anthony Perkins.
