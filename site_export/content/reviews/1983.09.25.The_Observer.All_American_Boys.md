All-American boys

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

NOW listen. This is important, this is vital information, classified, and there is no second, I said will you listen, please, there is no second chance. Right. A lead is a guy who might just want to buy a piece of land in Florida, and a sit is when you sit down with the guy and you sell it to him, I mean you sell his ass off. And the board is the sales figures. It's a picture of how you sell. And you better be up on that board, I mean right up there. Because number one on the board, numero uno, gets a Cadillac; and number two gets a bunch of steak knives; and number three and number four get fired. Number five? What do you talk, number five? There is no number five. This is a small operation.

Now you better get all this, and get it good. Because, without it, you go to see David Mamet's Glengarry Glen Ross at the Cottesloe and you are nowhere. You walk into this theatre and you see a curtain for Christ's sake. And you think a curtain? In 1983? Where have they been? And what have they got to hide?

Only the best play in London. I will stop this. It was meant to be an approximation (only an approximation, because, hell, this is a family newspaper) of Mr Mamet's Chicago dialogue, and I can hardly hope to reproduce the intoxicating mixture of evasions, pleadings, browbeatings, stonewalling and spiel: the fantastic ear for emphasis and repetition and the interrupting of people who weren't actually saying anything. Nobody alive writes better American.

The curtains part on Hayden Griffin's munificent impression of a stiflingly plush Chinese restaurant, precise and atmospheric to the last table-top bottle of soy sauce. Banquettes and flowering lampshades are in a tasteful shade of blood-red. This is a tank for a breed of nervous piranha fish to swim or flounder in; politic, heavy, sanguinary men.

We meet them two by two. Levene, a pudgy ex-champ salesman, tries to bribe Williamson, the young office manager, to give him better leads. Moss, even pudgier and oily, discusses with Aaronow, who is shabby and rattled, the possibility of stealing the leads for their own use or for sale to a rival firm. (Aaronow is rash enough to use the word 'robbery': Moss looks at him like he never heard it before.) Finally we see Roma, the current high-flyer, courting a client with worthless tracts of real estate called things like Glengarry or Glen Ross. I know, I too went into the theatre expecting a contest between two rival brands of malt whisky.

Act Two takes us to the burgled office, grey but at least daylit. Mr Mamet, who in 'American Buffalo' had a character advise caution 'in case, God forbid, something inevitable should happen,' this time kicks off with the startling observation, 'People used to say that there are numbers of such magnitude that multiplying them by two made no difference.' A cop is around to grill the staff, provoking a hilarious mixture of fear and contempt. The result of the investigation is surprising.

Meanwhile we have watched Roma getting high on the job, Levene even higher on an unexpected triumph, and the four - who never quarrel among themselves - turning on Williamson, the despised desk person who has power over them all. 'American Buffalo,' three men in a junk-shop, was as closely wrought as 'The Caretaker'; this one is as smoothly incarnadined as 'The Birthday Party' or 'The Homecoming,' with the difference that we are told who the people are and what is at stake (and, strangely enough, that doesn't diminish the suspense one bit). Here at last, carving characters and conflicts out of language, is a play with real muscle: here, after all the pieces we have half-heartedly approved because they mentioned 'important' issues as if mentioning were the same as dealing with. 'Glengarry Glen Ross' mentions nothing, but in its depiction of a driven, conscienceless world it implies a great deal.

Bill Bryden's boys do good by it, though one sequence that should be an explosion of embarrassment is underdone and a very tricky downbeat ending merely tails off. I imagine too that American actors would find richer Jewish and Italian rhythms in the dialogue. Jack Shepherd, who plays Roma, always goes high-pitched when he goes Transatlantic, but he is highly watchable, as is James Grant's dour Aaronow.

Derek Newark's sweating glad-handedness is matched by Trevor Ray's fleshy impassivity: two fat studies. John Tams is an implacable detective, and Tony Haygarth's customer is amazing. He comes to the office to welsh on his deal because his wife has told him to; and he ends up apologising for not letting himself be cheated. He obviously feels that he has broken a masculine bond. In the fewest words author and actor tell us everything about the man's marriage, his fears and dreams, his vulnerability to sales-talk, and his wife's cooking.

* * *

More Americana. Sam Shepard's The Tooth of Crime is revived at the Bush with a black cast, and with many of the musical references, though not the music, changed to match. The play has not dated, but it hasn't improved any either; and though Charlie Hanson's production is less portentous than the Open Space one x years ago (I missed the intermediate versions), it grinds to a slow finish. Mr Shepard's work, as I learnt from seeing his own production of his latest and best, 'Fool for Love,' benefits from a light touch.

I take it we are all opposed to racial stereotypes: one we could lose is the notion that black English actors are better than white English actors at playing Americans. The only one here in easy command is Victor Romero Evans as Hoss, the bizarre amalgam of rock-star/ganglord/dragstrip racer whose musical/criminal/sporting - and add sexual - empire is won from him in a musical duel by the young pretender, Crow. Crow does, in Chris Tummings's performance, take intermittent wing but nobody else has much style, and this play is all style. I grant that nobody else could have written it, but the language is plastered over just two situations: Hoss waits for Crow, Hoss meets Crow. In 'Glengarry Glen Ross,' or come to that, in 'Guys and Dolls,' the argot serves the action; here it merely serves itself. I'd call it camp (at its lowest, in one performance); or another deadweight rock musical.

* * *

Having strutted off with 'You're a Good Man, Charlie Brown' Snoopy (Duchess) gets a 'Peanuts' musical under his own name. In Teddy Kempner's performance, which is white-sweatered and two-legged, he thoroughly deserves it. Keeping a properly ineffable distance from the surrounding humans except when he needs them, he is a fascinating hybrid of satirist and satirised, erupting into a top-hat-and-bone routine when elected head beagle but subsiding under the epitaph 'He isn't much of a dog but after all who is.' The cartoon is actually more pungent on the stage than on the page: Charlie Brown more mercilessly sat-upon, Lucy (who surely begat Miss Piggy) more implacably emasculating, Linus more convulsively neurotic. Only Woodstock is too cute for words; and indeed doesn't have any.

There is a very cunning blend of sting and honey in Hal Hackady's lyrics, which make the major uplift song into a showpiece, and showstopping, laundry-list number. The resourceful score, starting from ragtime and working forwards, is by Larry Grossman, who earned all our gratitude as musical consultant for the Muppets, and is one of a youngish Broadway generation (along with Craig Carnelia, David Shire, Robert Waldman, and others equally unknown) who persist in writing actual music for musicals. Best musical. Best play. Some week.
