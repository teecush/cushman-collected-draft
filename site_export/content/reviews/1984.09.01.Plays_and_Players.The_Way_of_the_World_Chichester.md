The Way of the World — Chichester

Well,' said the gentleman ahead of me, tolerantly, as we left the theatre 'you can't win 'em all.' I saw what he meant. The Chichester audience had already been given a vapid The Merchant of Venice to clap and giggle their way through, and by comparison William Gaskill's production of The Way of the World was bound to strike them as discouragingly sober. It actually seemed to be taking the play seriously and, heinously, to be communicating that seriousness. It was also disconcertingly well acted on almost every level, and handsomely rather than prettily designed. Outdoors was economically distinguished from in, the governing and immovable factor of Hayden Griffin's set being a majestic doorway. From wherever you looked at it - and I changed sets at the interval - this arch refused to settle into a perfect central position.

This may conceivably have been a cunning concept, intended to suggest insecurity in a play that is riddled with it; but I have never seen a set that didn't look lopsided on that Chichester platform, which surely has at least one edge too many. (To appreciate what is wrong with it, you have to see the perfectly proportioned stage and auditorium at Stratford, Ontario; Chichester looks as if the Canadian plans were flown over the Atlantic, lost en route, and reconstructed from somebody's very fallible memory). It is no wonder, to be fair to them, that Chichester's regulars have developed such an overwhelming taste for superficiality; the sight-lines and the acoustics are cruel to anything else.

Mr Gaskill's intransigence, as viewed from the middle distance, often seemed to amount to a couple of gaunt profiles in fancy dress lost in a tall story. Most productions of this play that I have seen have strenuously sought, in acting and in programme-notes, to make the narrative clear; an ill-fated endeavour on the whole. Mr. Gaskill is comparatively insouciant about it and yet he captures what is important; the individual situations and their emotional strengths. At most given moments one knew who was double-crossing whom, even if one could not quite fathom how; and one also knew just how the predators and the victims felt about it. It was also clear that a predator could become a victim with lightning speed. Ian Hogg, first-rate when looking in my direction and inaudible when not, plays Fainall as a haggard plotter with a deep awareness of the unfairness of life.

An amused awareness too; his recurrent phrase, Tis but the way of the world', is accompanied each time by a hollower laugh. His bond with Mrs Marwood (which a National Theatre production once tried unhappily to convey by having the two of them start to undress at the first-act curtain) is here a strong but subtle mixture of lust and mutual protection. Sara Kestelman, with her decibels lowered since playing the equivalent role in The Double Dealer, commands the stage whenever she is on it. She may now be our best actresss of villainesses; she makes their case, not by whitewashing but by allowing them the dignity of their own passion. These two in showing what love, wit and marriage can so easily come to provide the right context for the hero and heroine.

Many critics have expressed surprise that Michael Jayston's Mirabell fails to match her in sparkle; surprising surprise since Mr Jayston has always been one of nature's straight men. He does at least suggest a decent disposition and a quizzical mind and is something for the wits, harmless or malicious, to bounce off.

Chief among the harmless is John Moffat's Witwoud; one of the production's chief charms is its eclectic choice of actors. The National, the RSC, the Court are all well represented; and Mr Moffatt points in a strong claim for the Tennent tradition at its best. He gets with total lack of fuss to the heart of every joke, and is well matched with James Villier's beamish Petulant (who does not live up to his name, but what actor in this role ever did?) At other times she laid the character's vulnerability on a bit thick. The lady is a comic monster and she is a tyrant; witness her fury when she turns against her maid. Miss Plowright as in The Cherry Orchard is inclined to sink into self-pity, and Mr. Gaskill, who does not approve of a caricature, is inclined to abet her. The same indulgence affects Geoffrey Hutchings' Sir Wilful; an ingratiating performance (heaven forefend we should mock a country bumpkin) but underpowered for the long drunk scene which no actor except John Woodvine has ever rescued from dullness in my presence. Jane Carr as Foible delivers a welltuned version of her customary performance, Sheila Allen is a gracious presence as Mrs Fainall, and there are even two new actors to round out the cast.

One, Tim Swinton, is presently out of his depth with Waitwell but Amanda Holmes as Mincing is pertness personified. It is 20 years since Mr Gaskill began his cycle of Restoration comedies and the evening's pervading pleasure is the sense that here, at last, is the masterpiece. It was actually the first time that I have felt able to give more than formal assent to the play's official status. Like his only (and very different) rival in the field, Peter Wood, Mr Gaskill is heavily dependent on casting; these plays with their stockcompany roots are the ultimate actors' pieces. This time his touch has been golden. I want to see these actors in this production at a real theatre.

It is the best thing at Chichester since - I am swallowing hard but I think I mean this — since Uncle Vanya.
