On the beach

Caryl Churchill registers her Objections to Sex and Violence (Royal Court) on an English beach, but she has not crowded her stage with deck-chairs. The strip of sand we see is all but deserted, partly because it is rocky and exposed, partly because the play is set in an uncertain June. Miss Churchill's characters are taking their holidays ahead of the crowd, and they suffer for it.

Their situation does however permit their author to move them around in discussion groups of two or three. They have their exits and their entrances and these are not, by any realistic standards, plausibly managed. Still, they have not come to the seaside to tell us a story; they are there to decorate a couple of themes.

In the centre is Jule, who stands--in others' eyes if not her own--for a permissive attitude to both sex and violence. She is on the run, having apparently participated in a bombing. After her comes her sister Annie, a repressed, oppressed, depressed woman, accustomed to being sexually and socially exploited; her current job--cleaning house for an ex-lover and his wife--allows her to wallow in both conditions simultaneously. She regards her sister with a furious mixture ("all right, we all know you're the most liberated woman in the world") of envy and disapproval.

With Annie comes her boyfriend, Phil, who does not seem to be exploiting her; perhaps because he is a born victim himself. Half-way through the play she momentarily convinces him that to kill someone--even someone against whom he has no direct grudge--would be both a release and a relief. This provides an arresting if unconvincing first-act curtain. When we return from the bars we wait to see the matter taken further, but all that happens is that Phil backs down, somewhere off stage.

Also intermittently present are a middle-aged couple named Arthur and Madge. Madge, like the lady in the Canadian Club ads, disapproves of virtually everything; Arthur seeks consolation in soft-core porn, which she forces him to burn when she catches him with it. Two of Jule's young men also turn up. One of them spends the first act taunting Phil and Annie as the nearest available straights. The second act finds him less defiant, disguised so effectively that I took him for a new character; and an object of virulent scorn to Jule. He has betrayed their principles; what they are she never makes very clear, but her ex-husband turns up to clarify what they are not. He is a party-line Communist, and as alien to her as any bourgeois.

The play is a conversation-piece with annoying vestiges of plot. I tried approaching it as a Shavian fantasia, but kept stubbing my toes on doggedly naturalistic fragments of character and dialogue, fragments which refused to build into any kind of structure. Individual lines are witty and revealing. Phil's shuffling declaration that faced with a crisis he would always resort to non-violence, or Madge's ambiguous snort of "Young women today want to be whipped." On a larger scale Miss Churchill contrives a pleasing, even touching, imbroglio between Arthur, Madge and a passing old lady of whose general relevance I remained uncertain.

This is the only occasion on which the author makes action work for her as well as words. Even verbally the play never really coheres. The characters' sexual and political attitudes are mechanically aligned, rather than dramatically knotted. Each scene divides in half; first, let's talk about sex, then about violence. Intellectually we can see where the issues join, but then we always could. Madge abhors sex and wants to see the law upheld as forcefully--violently--as possible. Undoubtedly this kind of woman (or this combination of attitudes) exists, but I knew no more about her when I left the theatre than when I entered it. The only character not cut down to size is Jule, who remains in control, a sphinx in the sand, untouchable because inscrutable.

There could hardly be a better moment for a play that explored the motivation--psychological or ideological--of the urban guerrilla; hardly a worse to concentrate on a character who avows no goals and merely hints that killing might be therapeutic. Asked what kind of society he believes in, the pacific Phil can only stammer ludicrously. Jule never gets caught like that, but her magnificent opacity looks to me like dramatic miscalculation. The part calls for stamina, which Rosemary McHale supplies, and to which she adds grace and a touch of humour. Anna Calder-Marshall partners her with obsessive determination; like a highly strung terrier desperately sniffing round a statue. But the best performances are Stephen Moore's Phil and Ivor Roberts's Arthur, two sad sacks kept equally clear of compromise and condescension.
