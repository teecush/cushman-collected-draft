Dullards and dafties

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on plays at the Cottesloe, 'The Comedy of Errors' and a new farce.

CONSIDERED as the National's fringe studio or smaller-than-anything-else in the National complex, the Cottesloe suffers from having been purpose-built.

The most endearing theatres of this kind -- the Bush, King's Head, Other Place -- are conversion jobs. The closure of the Cottesloe, which has been threatened, would be a disaster: not because of its commitment to the fringe (which can survive without it), but because it alone stands between the National and gigantism.

Economies have already removed from the programme a couple of minor classics that should have been good company fodder; and the choice of new plays has so far seemed overly dutiful: self-conscious gestures towards current modes. Of the two latest offerings, Julian Mitchell's Half-Life bows low before the spirit of elegiac elegance, while John Mackendrick's Lavender Blue falls down and immerses itself in lyric squalor.

Half-Life, which would be an aptly dismal title for either play, actually refers to a method of measuring vast tracts of time, useful for archaeologists, that being the former vocation of the central character. He has not now much purpose of any kind, science having destroyed his favourite theory, viz., that Western culture is indivisibly Hellenic and Stonehenge merely an outcrop of Mycenae. Having established this at length, Mr Mitchell seemingly forgets it. His hero, Noel Cunliffe, passes on to general bitching.

Sir Noel, I should say, and ex-master of an Oxford college; though now residing in a luxurious country-house conveniently close to Salisbury Plain. We have another play about the English upper class being vile with style; and Waris Hussein's production reduces it, for much of the time, to its simplest form.

The voluble protagonist sits talking in his garden, his friends, pupils, hangers-on and (indispensable) lost love around him in a semi-circle. Outside the ring sits a contemptuous young female leftie, who says things like 'You people! You've no more idea of love than politics.'

We might almost be west of Suez, though Mr Mitchell's wit is far sharper than John Osborne's. Noel has an impressive armoury of curled, stinging one-liners; with which to discomfort his guests, among whom the ladies can give nearly as good as they get. The gentlemen are dullards, who reduce such actors as Hugh Paddick, Richard Pearson and even Paul Rogers (who is in golden form) to emblems of conspicuous waste; but the actresses are well displayed: Avril Elgar shrewish and Isabel Dean resplendent.

At the end of the first act, Mr Mitchell springs briefly and effectively into action, but mostly he talks. Relationships are assumed rather than dramatised; that between Noel and a young Australian protege (diggers both) in particular. Everyone talks as if it were the hub of the action but we can barely see it.

Noel has a fine set-to with the radical lady on the perfectibility of man, which he denies; and she asserts; socialism is, quite correctly, identified with religion. That could be the core of a play, but not of this one. They finish as friends: a tribute to Noel's charm which, with John Gielgud the actor, is immense. He handles the brief bitter flourishes with magnificent insouciance, but the real deep corrosion escapes him.

* * *

Social workers and their charges make up the cast of Lavender Blue. But in the printed version the author denies that he is concerned with social issues. 'It is, simply, about people.' This is unfortunate, since the issues are more interesting than the people.

There are at least two characters who have no bearing on the main action, but lend sociological colour. They are a retarded married couple, and they provide one of the play's two good moments when they protect themselves from a wife-beating drunk by pleading that they're 'dafties.' The other involves Susan Fleetwood as a child-care officer, carrying on a weary telephone flirtation with a policeman in order to get information. Her role in the play, like everyone else's, is to suffer and to talk about it; Miss Fleetwood deserves to smile a little.

The worst of the play is not its brutality but its winsomeness. It is not enough for the lead, another daftie, to rape a small girl, murder her, and walk around with her dress on; he also has to talk in nursery rhymes, which the author, and presumably others at the National, find interesting and even beautiful.

* * *

It's high time I gave Trevor Nunn a bad notice; and I approached The Comedy of Errors (Aldwych) with pleasingly low hopes, mindful that at Stratford I had found its good humour forced, the interpolated musical numbers pre-empting the text instead of complementing it. Alas, no joy, he's gone and improved it; or rather, unbounded joy, the comic pressure has been raised so high that song and dance are the only possible release. These chores are distributed among the principals with nice discrimination, the Ephesian twins being the top terpsichoreans (watch Nikolas Grace jump sideways on to tables) and the Syracusans the vocalists (hear Michael Williams's riotous patter on the subject of Time.)

I must have been partially blind at Stratford; Gillian Lynne's choreography, for heaven's sake, is the best in any British musical for decades. Her exorcism sequence tops everything, with John Woodvine's Pinch singing so fine you wonder why he wastes his time being a marvellous actor. The chase which follows -- whether due to Mr Nunn, Miss Lynne, or the shade of Mack Sennett -- is the best ever done on a stage. And that still leaves the final scene, with Mike Gwilym's Antipholus turned to a manic QC and Judi Dench as his wife hardly more patient, just two syllables behind the Duke as he kindly reminds her of the story of her life. Richard Griffiths, a pained and patient officer, promises to rival Arthur Lowe in his soft rotundity. One word more and I shall gibber.

* * *

Shut Your Eyes and Think of England (Apollo) is full of rubbishy jokes about Jews, Arabs and homosexuals (or accepted images thereof), but it is not rubbish. Like all farce, it upsets the status quo; uniquely in West End farce, it leaves it upset. In its modest way it is quite subversive (all right, so it's the fashion) and, though the plot-line goes thin, it never breaks. Donald Sinden begins with a pleasing sketch of a worm, then takes on real glitter as he revolves. Frank Thornton and Patsy Rowlands are the truest of his cohorts.
