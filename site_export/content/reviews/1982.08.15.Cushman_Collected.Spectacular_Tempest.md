Spectacular Tempest

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Stratford and London.

RON DANIELS'S production of The Tempest (Stratford) begins with a first-rate shipwreck. It is mechanically persuasive, and even allows us to tell the characters apart. They are all rushing about at the sharp end, which juts out at us. Then, with a lightning-flash, it is gone, to be replaced by Derek Jacobi's Prospero, a conductor lowering his baton on a taxing finale. Behind him lies the wreck of another ship; presumably the one on which he came to the island. It looks more impressive than you might guess from the paranoid tale he tells his daughter (the way he talks, it was nothing but a coracle), but, as designed by Maria Bjornson, it makes a splendid unifying setting for the rest of the play.

Like Peter Hall in his National production, Mr Daniels pays rewarding attention to music and spectacle. He too has a master showman at the centre; Mr Jacobi's is a gripping performance on several levels. It takes him as an actor beyond the skilled charm that got him through Benedict and Peer Gynt; there are deeper tones in his voice, more iron in his soul.

Technically commanding, he is psychologically very acute. His Prospero is a man moving from one loneliness to another. His magic powers give him a relationship with Ariel that inspires affection on both sides, but no contact; the desires to have freedom and to bestow it become ambiguous things. For the masque of Juno the bleached boat sports a traverse curtain, much in evidence thereafter and making the analogy of Prospero as magician with Shakespeare as the towering playwright more potent than I have known it.

He stops the show with a passion that encompasses vocational disillusion, and leads logically - i.e. bitterly - into "Our revels now are ended." Magic drains him, and it is a relief to give it up. A risk and a sacrifice as well: Mr Jacobi gives careful emphasis to Prospero's decision to choose "reason" over "fury" and foreswear revenge. "And they shall be themselves," he says of his enemies: for better, but probably for worse.

Mark Rylance's Ariel, as widely advertised, comes on with a quintet of lookalikes: actually a backing group, Ariel and his Full Fathom Five. They help out with music - the sea-dirge has been beautifully set by Stephen Oliver and is as beautifully sung - and with the tricks. They are notably effective manipulating the yapping skeletal dogs that torment the clowns, less so when cluttering the place up striking attitudes.

Bob Peck's Caliban is disappointingly average, though vouchsafed a last ominous appearance. The love scenes are a bit giggly, though Alice Krige is coming on as Miranda. The courtiers are livelier than usual, with the descent into madness of Alonso (Paul Webster) given something of its proper weight. They turn up for the final judging one lord short. Did he ever get off the island?

* * *

At Stratford's Other Place, Bulgakov, one of the contenders for RSC Top Ryssian (in place of Gorky, retired), is represented by a play called Moliere. This, according to a prologue and a programme note, dramatises the author's own relation with Stalin (his unpredictable fan) as that between Moliere and Louis XIV, who first protects, then abandons, him.

Moliere had a troubled, foreshortened run at the Moscow Arts in 1936, and scenes of the hero's colleagues pressured for information by a hooded inquisition could well be read with reference to the Cheka. Ralph Koltai's set, unusually splendiferous for this space, is dominated by a giant head-and-shoulders of the Sun King; it seems to get more serenely menacing by the moment.

The surroundings seem to demand an audacious central performance, and Anthony Sher, a dusty weakling who snaps his fingers at surprising moments, duly delivers. But there is nothing to him; all the play tells us about Moliere is that he was a thorough professional who, in all circumstances, judged people by how well they could act.

It is, for all the bustle, a shadowy piece; the scenes with Louis come off best, no actor being better at dangerous urbanity than Derek Godfrey. He is well served by Malcolm Storry, David Bradley and especially Albie Woodington as a cobbler kept on hand to arrest people. Joanna Foster is an alluring Mrs Moliere, Chris Hunter fitfully good as an informer who hides in harpsichords. Some other performances lack poise. But Bill Alexander's production is good at back-stage flurry and, if all else fails, plays with pretty costumes are preferable to plays without.

* * *

One of the Moscow Arts' greater hits, the Cherry Orchard, is at the Round House, en route for the Oxford Playhouse, which has a proscenium stage. That's a problem, since Mike Alfreds's production has to be plonked on a platform at an odd angle to the audience. If he were going to use a set, I think he should have had a proper one, instead of the present arrangement of mingy drapes and ugly furniture. It spoils the considerable if wilful production, which sees the play as a comedy of humours.

The most successful monomaniac is Pam Ferris's fierce German governess (with performing dog); she is helped - though I don't think the play is - by a reversion to the dialogue and scene-order of Chekhov's first draft, which gives her solitude greater prominence. Alison Fiske explodes Ranyevskaya, as Francesca Annis did Natalya in A Month in the Country, presenting, with great integrity, an habitual charmer who turns every situation into a scenario; a trait, inherited by her daughter, reacted against by Varya, who goes to an opposite but equally neurotic extreme. Miss Fiske's performance has a stunning end: she wants to feel for the loss of the orchard; she tries hard; but - unlike her brother, who is really cut up - she can't make it.

John Dicks has the right mixture of intelligence and absurdity for Trofimov, and if Roger Sloman's Lopakhin leaves out too much, what it puts in, in the way of a vulnerable vulgarity, should certainly be there.

* * *

William Gaskill has correctly said that there is nothing much you can do with She Stoops to Conquer (Lyric, Hammersmith), and similarly there is nothing much you can say about his production. Like a good inn, it is clean, warm, funny and hospitable. Anthony Sharp and Betty Marsden set up the Hardcastles to perfection in the exposition (what a nimble piece of writing, incidentally), and if they have nowhere much to go from there, they still mark time admirably; Miss Marsden does a great Gingoldian take when she hears that 50 is a fashionable age.

Tracy Ullman's Kate is an event; too many half-baked insecure classical performances have recently been extravagantly praised, but she is for real; a trifle milky in her best clothes, but solidly radiant. Karen Archer reflects her glow as Constance, and Hugh Fraser actually gets laughs as Hastings; who would have thought this subsidiary couple had so much blood in them? There have probably been more hilarious Lumpkins and Marlows than Ron Cook and Nigel Terry, but both are believable, Mr Cook's bantam rebellion in particular. The production never stoops; the play conquers.
