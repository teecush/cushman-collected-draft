From Sondheim to La Cage

One can hardly complain of a shortage of musicals in London at the moment. All the same there is little sign of the latest Broadway crop getting to us. For the moment we have to be content with the records. Most interesting of them, I supbose predictably, is Stephen Sondheim's Sunday in the Park with George, available as an import on RCA HBC1-5042. This show began its life as a workshop at the off-Broadway Playwrights' Horizons in 1983; it is now playing, most unusually for a musical, at one of the smallest Broadway theatres. But if ever there was a chamber musical, this is it. Its subject and its techniques are almost aggressively rarefied. George is Georges Seurat, the French pointillist painter, at work on his masterpiece A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte.

That at least is the George of Act One. That of Act Two is Seurat's fictitious American great-grandson, an avant-garde artist of our own time. Seurat cailed himself a Chromo-Luminarist (something only revealed to me by the synopsis that accompanies the record); our George calls his creations Chromolumes. Old George created patterns of light on canvas; young George does it with lasers. Old George built up his pictures by connecting a myriad of brightly coloured specks for your George 'connecting means making contacts in the museum and foundation world, to finance his next Chromalume. Andrew Lloyd Webber, the only other composer I can imagine writing a musical about pointillism, would undoubtedly have called it Dots. Sondheim and his librettist-director James Lapine have actually provided Seurat with a mistress called Dot; she, having emigrated to America with another man, bears Seurat's daughter, present in the second act as George's 98-year old grandmother named Marie.

In both these guises she is, as Shaw put it in 'Man and Superman", the Mother Woman to George's Artist Man. Which brings us to the question: what is Sunday in the Park with George actually about? Obviously, it is about The Artist; but what, on the stage, can you do with that? The show manages, to a surprising extent, to dramatise the actual process of creation. Seurat's continual mouthing of abstractions - Design, Composition, Tension, Balance, G Light, Harmony - is too self-conscious to be credible; but it is another thing when he gets into a song called 'Colour and Light', with a compulsive bass-line reminiscent of 'Someone in a Tree' from 'Pacific Overtures' and a freeranging melodic line held in check by a taut, staccato rhythm; the song acts out the artist's balance of liberty and restraint, and almost provides an aural equivalent for the dabbing of paint.

On a larger scale the show's first half enacts the composition of the painting Grande Jatte' is a large group-portrait, of mainly middle-class Parisians at play. The figures from the canvas wander through the musical, leading fragments of their own lives. We watch Seurat observing them while ignoring 98 4 their preoccupations. Finally he breaks up the groups into which they have arranged themselves, and disposes them in accordance with his own plan. plots. And there they are, for ever Fine. But the Job of the Artist will not fill the whole evening, so we get the Plight of the Artist as well. His battle with the philistines, gently adumbrated in Act One, is given fuller treatment when our modern George is let loose among the vultures of the museum, criticising, flattering and, above all, patronising him.

"That is the state of the art, my dear' they croon, with unfortunate glibness. They strongly recall the party crowds of "Merrily We Roll Along" and they arouse the same kind of misgivings. They are crucifying the artist-hero but since he is part of their world just what makes our chromoluminary so superior? There is another aspect: the Artist as Human Being failing to relate to other human beings. Of the figures who fill his painting, the ones with whom Seurat seems to have the greatest rapport are a couple of dogs, for whom he improvises a sprightly dialogue. His apparent human indifference is what Dot, his model as well as his mistress, most objects to.

Not only does he keep her standing around for hours in uncomfortable positions, he refuses to take her out at nights, preferring to work on his picture. (Key song: Furbishing the Hat'). So she leaves him for Louis, a baker and as she insists, an artist in his own way, if not perhaps George's equal. As she puts it, with superb Sondheim economy; '... there are Louis's And there are Georges - Well, Louis's And George'. Sondheim has trodden some of this ground before, with Sweeney Todd and Mrs Lovett. Sweeney was surely an artist, in hair and in death; George, musing over his pallette, hums in the same accents as Sweeney instructing the sailor Anthony in the niceties of wigmaking.

George and Dot are pale by comparison, though their relationship progresses. Dot ends up grateful to George for having given her the most prominent position in the picture. Her act Two alter ego Marie gives affectionate advice to her George, and finally Dot herself makes a spectral re-appearance to reinforce her daughter's good work. Artists, it is suggested may not make good husbands but we should love and tolerate them because they give us immortality. As Keats told us, thereby turning the trick himself, they freeze time for us, on Grecian urns or French impressionist canvases. Seurat, not a noticeably good son, doing what his mother wanted him to do: preserve the Grande Jatte in its glory before progress takes over and ruins it.

In Act Two the island is in fact shown to be overrun with high-rise buildings. These themes are kept in tandem, tantalisingly but stimulatingly, in the first act. The second is more problematic, largely because it is difficult to take young George and his chromolumes as seriously as the show apparently wants us to. (There are satiric notes but they are not consistently sounded). Musicals have traditionally had second-act problems: difficulties in winding up their. "Sunday in the Park", having no plot, experiences corresponding difficulties in developing its ideas. When I saw it in preview in April its second half was definitely undernourished; when I returned in August two new numbers had been added. One of them, a threnody called "Children and Art", does much to pull the show together.

Marie sings it, invoking her mother Dot; as the title suggests it is about two kinds of continuity, the kinds provided by the two central figures of the first half and, perhaps, by their descendants. The "perhaps" is necessary for the song is full of gentle ironies. Dot, with only the painting to go on, blatantly sentimentalises the rather unpleasant characters it depicts. But, since they are dead and the picture lives, she may be right. The song, smilingly, allows us to make up our own minds A later number, 'Move ON', is the hard sell. It is, as the title suggests, Dot's advice to young George; 'Stop worrying if your vision Is new. Let others make that decision - They usually do.' That last bruised qualification is worth having but otherwise the song is uneasy Sondheim, a retreat to Rodgers and Hammerstein territory.

I have nothing against 'You'll Never Walk Alone'; when 'Carousel' was revived in Manchester this year its went straight to my tear-ducts. But there it came naturally; as affirmation has never done to Sondheim. The score picks itself up for a stirring hymn called 'Sunday' during which the Seurat painting reassembles only to vanish again leaving George with a blank sheet. His turn now. If the show doesn't quite add up, the score does. It is constructed (with the help of some piquant percussive building-blocks left over from 'Pacific Overtures') on internal echoes. It may be Sondheim's most tightly-knit composition. An initial impression of severity gives way, on third or fourth hearing, to one of spare, elegant melody.

"The Day Off", George's dog duet, is a song that first strikes the ear as odd and angular and then takes on a wholly satisfying logic. The lyrics, probably the unshowiest he has written, are also among the subtlest and most suggestive. (In his previous two scores I felt that their edge had been blunted). And their half-tones can never have been better conveved than in Bernadette Peters's performances of Dot and Marie. In sensitivity, vivacity, intelligence, sensual ache, and the suggestion of bottomless wit, she is just about the perfect leading lady: for musicals in general and Sondheim in particular, I warmed less to Mandy Patinkin's two Georges but maybe I was meant to; he certainly has extraordinary vocal abilities, with a diabolically haunting upper register that is almost part of the orchestration.

(By Michael Starobin this time, not Jonathan Tunick). Nobody else has much opportunity. I enjoyed the show more on record, you may guess, than in the theatre. Not only Sondheim borrows from Sondheim. The Rink, another of last season's still running Broadway successes, repeats themes and techniques from 'Follies'. That took place in a theatre on the eve of demolition; a haunted theatre whose former inhabitants were squabbling about their wasted lives. "The Rink' takes place in a skating-rink on the boardwalk of (presumably) Atlantic City. It, too, is about to be pulled down, at the instigation of the widow who owns it and in the teeth of her daughter, recently returned from a long spell of hippiedom.

(At least it started as hippiedom; it must, the sixties being long past, have turned into something else. We are made privy to of their rows and reconciliations past and present, additional roles in the past ones being assumed by the wrecking crew brought in to do the rink over. Why librettist Terrence McNally is telling us this story never becomes clear; the show keeps changing focus. It is a horrible mess, but a fascinating one: fascinating because some top practitioners are involved and because the musical is still so central an American form that any show makes, consciously or not, some kind of statement about America. This one resolves into a lament for the time of innocence, before muggers.

It is also full of left-over family melodrama (the characters are Italian) and rouses itself at the end to look at the lost-and-found generation of the sixties. That is to say, daughter recalls her own departure with a song called 'All the Children in a Row'. The song is embarrassing, not because it is technically incompetent (it isn't), but because its authors, Fred Ebb and John Kander, don't know what they are singing about. They are mainline showbiz, and the rock-era names they invoke (Joplin, Kesey, Steppenwolf) sound spurious. Mostly, the show takes itself very seriously; though it loosens up for a comedy duet between mother and daughter, The Apple Doesn't Fall because the roles are being played by two stars, Chita Rivera and Liza Minnelli, and the audience needs to see them at some point together and happy.

The song is modelled on Kander and Ebb's superb "The Grass is Always Greener' (from Woman of the Year') but it provides an enjoyable oasis on its own terms, catapulting the show into yet another convention, the barefaced star vehicle. Maybe it should have stayed there. Strangely, considering the names involved and the box-office success, the show was passed up by the major record companies. That's Entertainment moved in from London and recorded it, on TER 1091. My favourite number is the simplest, the title-song ('I wanna go round the rink, the rink/There's nothing to beat the rink, I think') sung by the wreckers. The same firm have recorded Baby (TER 1089), the most refreshing show on Broadway in some vears uniquely among post- Sondheim musicals it manages to be intelligently celebratory.

Indeed it does one of the things the musical is most basically for; it expresses a joy and wonder too basic and too intense to be From Sondheim to La Cage captured in words alone. The show's climax is childbirth, and it is very moving. The preceding action shows two expectant couples (one young, one middle-aged) and a third, who want are desperately to conceive but cannot: the same situation as in 'Ashes, with some of the same developments, but without self-pity and hence far more affecting. Nothing earth-shaking happens, but Sybille Pearson's book is so humorous and sympathetic that we are amused and involved. (With two of the pairs, anyway; the older ones remain rather wooden).

The score is by Richard Maltby Jr and David Shire, more adroit than any others at blending the traditionally effusive attitudes and concerns show music with contemporary words and sounds. Shire, the composing half, is especially good at rhythm numbers; to match his mixture of sweetness and sophistication you may have to go back to Harold Arlen. in Malthy takes us where no lyricist - on Broadway anyway - had travelled before. Picture a failing spermatozoan, Nor even knowin' where he is goin'. What's that ahead? diaphragm! Screw It! He knows he's dead - my God he slips through it. Nothing here is as good as the best in their anthology 'Starting Here Starting Now', but that after all represented the cream of 15 years' work.

Especial treats here are the title song, sung to the mother's belly, 'Fatherhood Blues', 'Romance' (a grimly determined tango for the deprived couple, regulating their sex-life to medical instruction), a soaring ballad "The Story Goes On, for the tenderness of its lyric, 'At Night She Comes Home to Me'. The show was not especially well reviewed; a particular pleasure of seing it was watching the audience, who had come with low expectations, surprised and, finally, lifted out of their seats. The audience for La Cage Aux Folles, by contrast, comes programmed to cheer. Its status as a pre-sold hit caused RCA (who, one imagines, took on the uncommercial Sunday in the Park out of loyalty to Sondheim and pressure from producer Thomas Z.

Shepard) to record La Cage before the Broadway opening: most unusual. I was present at the session, and enjoyed the atmosphere. Hearing George Hearn as the drag-queen singing 'Mascara', I felt sure I was in the presence of a smash. I still think it the best number in Jerry Herman's score, but neither in the theatre nor on the disc (where something seems to have happened to the dynamics) is the impact as great as in the studio. I have friends who consider the show to be the work of the Cavi Myself, I would not call it anything worse than mediocre. Its chief fault is that, taking over the farcical plot of the film, it reduces the characters to storming or mincing caricatures; the normality that makes farce possible has been sacrificed, and so has much of the structure Why has it been such a success?

It follows Torch Song Trilogy (by the same author Harvey Fierstein) in presenting a view of homosexuality just outrageous enough to make a Broadway (i.e. tourist) audience feel tickled and tolerant,; it has, and parades, what is known as 'heart'; and the Herman score is in what is still taken to be the show-music tradition: simple, plangent, hearty. A couple of numbers dire miscalculations, others are routine. But 'I Am What I Am' is better than such conventional affirmations of unconventionality have any right to be; "The Best of Times' effectively plunders the 'Milord' tradition; and the song that is meant to touch you, 'Look Over There', is actually touching. The score here consists of some standard, and some amended, songs by Jimmy McHugh culminating ina spine-chilling flag-waver called 'You Can't Blame Your Uncle Sammy'. I have been playing through some back numbers in the That's Entertainment catalogue. Pride of place must go to two operettas in their recent Sadler's Wells productions: Lehar's The Count of Luxembourg (TER 1050) and, an especial melodic revelation to me, Kalman's Countess Maritza (TER 1051.) Besides filling gaps in my strictly non- Viennese education, these are excellent digital recordings. (The technology doesn't always pay off so well). Reissues continue as various as ever. They include the derivative tedium of Goodbye Mr Chips (TER 1025) with its watery cinematic score and Thomas and the King (TERS 1009); though I suffered through this in the theatre I had not realised just how dead a ringer it was for Camelot, right down to the prophetic ending.
