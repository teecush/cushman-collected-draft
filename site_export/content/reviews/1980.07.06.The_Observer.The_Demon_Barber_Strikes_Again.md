The Demon Barber strikes again

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Stratford.

THIS week all our heroes have been mad: the Prince of Denmark, the Peasant Poet and the Demon Barber of Fleet Street.

Frenzy is the distinguishing mark of Stephen Sondheim's Sweeney Todd (Drury Lane), both as man and musical. A homicidal maniac in his original incarnation, Sweeney was equipped by Christopher Bond, in the dramatic version on which the current show is based, with a puritanical streak and an avenger's motive: both of them intensifying the blood-lust. The cast also includes an unquestionably demented beggar-woman and a heroine who, though sane, is something of a simpleton and is incarcerated for much of the second act in an asylum. A chorus of escaped lunatics helps speed the story to its climax.

Madness has cropped up in Broadway musicals before, with the afflicted generally represented as the coy repositories of a higher wisdom. Sweeney Todd is the first musical I know to take obsession for its subject and to depict it with a matching steely ferocity. It ends up as a moral show, but not by preaching, simply by the old approved method of showing passion spending itself.

This Sweeney is an escaped convict, who returns to Victorian London to claim the lives of the judge and beadle who had him transported so they could have easy access to his wife. Baulked in his first attempt at revenge, Sweeney, through a variety of motives--self-disgust, misanthropy and a praiseworthy desire to keep his hand in--turns to generalised mayhem.

The stunning number in which this bizarre transition is effected, `Epiphany', alternating fast crashing sequences of staccato determination with high-pitched yearnings for lost happiness and finally fusing them--almost closes the first half. But there is a further, cushioning, development.

Mrs Lovett, the `eminently practical' pie-lady, persuades Todd to put the corpses to use by joining her in business. The ensuing song, in which they jovially detail the contents of their individual meat-pies, is a compendium of sick jokes: some routine, but the best ingenious enough to be seductive. The tune is a robust waltz, and it is by now hardly a novel idea to set outrageous words to so reassuring a rhythm. What gives it distinction is an intervening theme, whose lilt is so insouciant as to be diabolical, and which provides an exhilarating but unsettling climax: it sweeps the characters beyond the pale and, momentarily at least, deposits us there with them. It serves also as an odd but potent love-duet.

Beauty, cankered but still attractive, hovers round the barber's chair; Todd has a habit of singing, and hauntingly too, as he slashes. This is an outstandingly melodious score; but the melodies come in short bursts, disappear and recur. About a dozen themes underpin, in variously ironic guises, a show which is three-quarters of the way to being through-composed. `The Ballad of Sweeney Todd', which flanks the score, is the prime example of power held in reserve; in a superlative orchestration by Jonathan Tunick, it begins as unnerving (a string figure seems to be echoing from the murkiest depths of the Thames) and ends--greatly enhanced by Harold Prince's staging--as shattering.

The music, and the headlong tensions of the melodrama, had me straining out of my seat. The narrative excitement is greater here than in New York. Scaling-down has helped; there is more pace, less fuss, despite some noisy trundling of scenic units wheeled into position so that actors may go up one side and down the other, like so many Dukes of York. Eugene Lee's iron-foundry set--presented in New York almost as an embodiment of the Industrial Revolution--is now just an impressive enclosure for the action; all to the good, since the supposed social comment always struck me as specious. Sweeney may live in a hierarchical society, but it seems more feudal than industrial.

The loss is in the central roles; in New York Len Cariou, satanically possessed, and Angela Lansbury, monumentally comic, were criticised for belonging to two different worlds, but that seemed to me the point; one had an insight into the coalition of deranged visionary and cheerfully amoral subordinate that can bring about mass murder. Here the show touched greatness, justifying its imagery of giant ovens and sulphurously smoking chimneys. There is certainly more contact--erotic contact even--between Denis Quilley and Sheila Hancock at the Lane; Mr Quilley grows steadily through the evening and Miss Hancock misses no syllable of Mr Sondheim's trickiest lyrics. They are admirable, but they are not archetypes. The supporting cast is uneven, but boasts a compelling beggar in Dilys Watling, a prepossessing young lover in Andrew C. Wadsworth and, in the van of a fine chorus, Oz Clarke, whose pointed singing of the first quatrain is the best of auguries.

* * *

A programme note to Hamlet (Stratford-on-Avon) clues us into a proliferation of theatrical references. In John Barton's production the first man on collects his helmet, cloak and spear before our eyes--gets into character, in fact--before embarking on sentry-go. The main acting-area is a raised quadrilateral: a stage upon a stage. Hamlet knows that all the men and women are merely etc., and is keen to get his own performance right. Michael Pennington delivers the pep-talk to the players without looking at them; he is really giving notes to himself. He is careful to get the proper costume for his mock-madness: imagine his surprise when, confronted by Ophelia, he finds himself overtaken by the real thing. His love for her was real as well; while she was tickled pink by his.

Carol Royle, immensely promising, adds an unwonted touch of pride to the girl and makes it plain that when she goes crazy she is imitating her lover. This is altogether the most civilised Elsinore I remember visiting; Tony Church's Polonius manifests unmistakable paternal affection; and Barbara Leigh-Hunt's Gertrude displays twinges of conscience even before the closet-scene (it is unusual for Gertrude to display anything so early in the evening).

Derek Godfrey's Claudius puts on so good a front I felt disinclined to credit all those stories his brother tells about him. The trouble is I liked his brother (Raymond Westwell) too; while there is a fine gentle Horatio from Tom Wilkinson, a man of many faces. (In The Maid's Tragedy he looked a double for Clive James. Here he looks vulnerable.) There is also a fine gentle Hamlet; Mr Pennington's intelligence blazes but never obtrusively (there being enough in Hamlet to occupy any actor's mind) and can crunch himself into and through soliloquy with absolute freshness. He almost gets a traditional exit: borne on four pairs of shoulders, head flung romantically back. But Fortinbras says, `Bear Hamlet to the stage,' and that, in accordance with Mr Barton's scheme, is as far as he gets. This production is simmering excitingly. I hope to be able to write about it again.

* * *

A second chance to see Edward Bond's The Fool (Other Place, Stratford) was certainly rewarding. I still cannot feel that the play's two acts absolutely balance out, but each is so crammed with invincible moments that it ceases to matter. Howard Davies's production is dour but reasonable; James Hazeldine, never pushing for sympathy, nevertheless makes John Clare's descent into the madhouse a thing of pity as well as terror.
