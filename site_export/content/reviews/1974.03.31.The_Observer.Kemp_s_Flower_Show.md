Kemp's flower show

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

LINDSAY KEMP'S Flowers, "a pantomime for Jean Genet," has opened at the Regent Theatre, Oxford Circus. When Flowers was first seen at the Bush it was reviewed by our dance critic, while the Regent itself was, until very recently, the Cameo-Poly Cinema. I am not sure whether to regard myself as a usurper or a simple trespasser.

Flowers is, as it happens, an outsiders' evening. Genet canonised the outcast, but then as a writer--a solitary animal--he had the right to do so; Mr Kemp's troupe have the strength of numbers--ten's a crowd in the theatre--and in creating a sealed-off stage equivalent of Genet's Catholic homosexual underworld they can afford the luxury of mocking their audience as well as defying them.

Though the show pays token tribute to Genet with mock-crucifixions, booming organ-music and such, it is most itself when it ventures into showbiz camp; in a seedy cabaret (a blend of gay bar and sad cafe) three transvestite singers bawl out a tortured version of "Over the Rainbow." Into this setting Mr Kemp makes a still, shy, imposing entrance in Dietrich drag, coyly fluttering a fan. He performs with great delicacy in an ambience that is often overstated to the point of tawdriness, and some of his staging--particularly his use of a gauze-curtain--is inspired. He also gives the impression of being the only person on stage who does not hate women.

* * *

A squarer misogyny pervades Royalty Follies (at the Royalty, which has also been a cinema in its short time, and might be happier that way). I had not realised how deeply sadistic is Paul Raymond's idea of Everyman's fantasy-life. Perhaps I should say how shallowly sadistic, and also how old-fashioned. The set-pieces in this show derive either from the Valentino era ("such a man of Destiny was Genghis Khan; the one thing he never had was Love") or from the even remoter myth of James Bond. The final number even recalls swinging London. Somehow it all figures, and I would not attempt to dispute the wisdom of Mr Raymond's diagnosis. The programme states that the phenomenal success of his play Pyjama Tops has confounded the critics. But really it wasn't the success that confounded us; just the play.

Nudity still flourishes, but striptease is dead. (It may be up to Mr Kemp to revive it.) At the Royalty there are always men around to take--or preferably tear--the girls' clothes off for them. The one thing they cannot remove is the uncertain smile that appears to be the English chorine's birthright. Even the lady in an adagio act, visibly gritting her teeth while she gets thrown around in profile, manages to register a brave grin when she faces front. The show also features a proficient conjuror and ventriloquist, but comedy is not even attempted.

In this respect it is impious for Mr Raymond to invoke the shades of Ziegfeld or George White, whose revues were probably just as rubbishy, but still acted as springboards for a Bert Lahr or Fanny Brice. They also of course introduced some great songs; John Taylor's music and lyrics here are desperately unoriginal and could interest nobody. One item, called "Black and White," is uncannily similar to an early Cole Porter thing called "That Black and White Baby of Mine." There are no real singers here either, only an overworked male vocalist with bizarre mid-Atlantic intonations. ("Diamonds are Forevore.")

* * *

Shorn of Mr Raymond's quaintly cosmopolitan orthography, Royalty Folies might make a good subtitle for King Lear, which is the second play in the Actors' Company season at Wimbledon. Contrary winds forced the company last month to open this production in New York without benefit of sets and costumes. This stroke of ill-luck seems, judging by what we see in Wimbledon, to have been the greatest good fortune. The hats and helmets are a particular affliction; aiming at barbaric grandeur, but suggesting the D'Oyly Carte, they gravely impair the authority and credibility of all who wear them.

David William's direction is not rigorous enough to compensate. Characters skip or scamper on when they might just as well walk; a table is laboriously introduced so that Robert Eddison's Lear, in the height of his fury, can mount an unfortunately feeble attack upon it. There is an intelligent attempt to link the play's cruelty with its eroticism; the blinding of Gloucester practically arouses Cornwall and Regan to orgasm, and there is unusual force in the sisters' involvement with Edmund. (Narrative clarity is the production's greatest virtue.) This theme should explode in Lear's Swiftian diatribes at Dover, but again Mr Eddison, mournful and mellifluous, lacks the strength to bring them off.

In other respects this scene finds him at his best; I have never heard the quicksilver associative transitions of Lear's derangement so fluently expressed. I had expected to be much moved by him in the reconciliation with Cordelia, but it means very little unless we can contrast it with the violence and intransigence of the king's earlier scenes. And these aspects of Lear, Mr Eddison simply cannot encompass. An occasional grace note finds him at his ease, but then he must be off again, tilting at windmills. His whimsy suits one aspect of Lear's madness, but he lacks the granite. Where is the actor who could give us both? (That is a rhetorical question. Anyone who has heard Ralph Richardson read Blake will know where to find Lear.)

With no centre to play to, the other actors necessarily flounder; the air is full of unpointed rhetoric. Definition returns--with a vengeance, I would say--in Ford's 'Tis Pity She's a Whore, the Actors' Company's third production at Wimbledon. David Giles has transferred this incest tragedy from a timeless Renaissance Italy to one firmly rooted at the end of the last century. A play whose host of characters and intrigues can easily become confusing in some neutral limbo gains enormously from the obligation which modern dress imposes to find a rationale for every scene.

Ian McKellen's moody schoolboy Giovanni, his voice even more tousled than his hair, has grown since I saw it two years ago; he now has the diabolism to sweep the play to its blood-boltered end. But this Giovanni is such a narcissist that his love for his sister--freshly and beguilingly played by Paola Dionisotti--is hard to credit. There is more believable passion in John Bennett's stony avenger, and in the jealous rage of Edward Petherbridge's bridegroom who, starting out chill and dapper, blows his cool to stunning effect. Margery Mason (with more than a hint of a moustache) and Tenniel Evans (ditto, with a trim beard) give rock-solid support, though I could wish that Mr Evans, when finally ordered to take Miss Mason away and burn her to ashes, would refrain from sobbing "It is poor dust." A simple assent would be far less risible.
