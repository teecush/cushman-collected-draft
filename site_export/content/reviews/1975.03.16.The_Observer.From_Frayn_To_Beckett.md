From Frayn to Beckett

MICHAEL FRAYN'S Alphabetical Order (Hampstead Theatre Club) is the most likeable play to have appeared in London in months. Obviously it possesses a particular appeal for journalists--it is set in the cuttings library of a provincial daily and might conceivably be retitled The Back Page--but it is likely to mean as much to anyone who has ever worked in companionable chaos, which is to say to everyone.

The chaos in this instance may be attributed partly to institutional lethargy and partly to the personality of the central character, though the second cause could be regarded as merely an aspect of the first. Lucy, Mr Frayn's librarian, is as appealing as the play; she is also, especially as played by Billie Whitelaw, rather desirable--sufficiently disorganised for men to offer to protect her, strong and generous enough for them to hope that actually she will protect them. Even the lady features editor, fulsomely envious, admits that "everybody loves our Lucy." So it happens that at least three of the male staff are liable to spend whole afternoons hunting among the files.

All this changes when Lucy is supplied with a new assistant, a brisk Mancunian girl called Leslie (Barbara Ferris, forthright). Mr Frayn has dealt before with the impact of an ambitious, efficient newcomer on a cosily comatose section of the supposedly dynamic newspaper profession. Lucy is not as high a climber as the tele-bound whizz-kid of Towards the End of the Morning, any more than this play is the equal of that dazzling novel; but she and it are still pretty effective.

I would not like to be an assistant stage manager on Alphabetical Order; the tidying-up that has to be effected between the end of Act One (Leslie's first day at work) and the beginning of Act Two (Six Months Later) is monstrous. Lucy is still nominally in charge, but effectively Leslie rules. The difference in their regimes may be illustrated by their respective views on the proper limitations of their work. Lucy instructs Leslie only to extract cuttings that have to be fitted into existing folders, "otherwise we have to start a new file." Leslie instructs the world in general not to seek information before the library is officially open. "They know the rules" and "The rules are rules"; filling words are the deftest, most economical of the evening.

They are not, however, the most obviously quotable; I could fill the rest of my space with those that are. "I sometimes think I've got so little objective reality that people don't even talk about me behind my back": that is the plaint of John, a leader-writer who once held a philosophy fellowship at All Souls. He is thus what you would call highly qualified; even his briefest utterances--"Oh, as it might be, God"--have qualifications. He stoops and hovers round the library searching, with imperious vagueness, for a fugitive quotation; his attributes--which include the best first-act curtain line in London--are shown off to the fullest advantage by Dinsdale Landen. James Cossins and Bernard Gallagher are the other members of a splendidly seedy male trio, all, in some sort, suitors to Lucy; a tangled situation on which Leslie lavishes the same ruthless benevolence as characterises her professional activities. She really comes into her own as an organiser when the paper, all unexpected, folds.

This jars. We have had little warning of it, and its theatrical import is that Mr Frayn has tired of indulging his characters and proposes to test them. They go ape, unfolding the folders, defiling the files. This scene is intended as one of those striking theatrical images, of which we write so often and which you are quite right to distrust; they generally look effortful. Mr Frayn is not a theatrical storyteller and when he attempts to gather up the threads of his action he comes near to choking himself with them. The play is most alive in its details, mostly affectionate, occasionally lethal: as when Leslie breaks open a first-aid box while everybody else is hunting for the key. That is an image; I would call it meaningful.

* * *

Mr Frayn's play survives its narrative deficiencies. Anthony Shaffer's Murderer (Garrick) does not. Any thriller must play games with the audience's assumptions; Mr Shaffer's special characteristic is to include the assumptions we have derived from the billing. In Sleuth he succeeded; I actually believed that he had killed off one of his stars by the interval. But you cannot play that trick twice, and anyway there are limits to credulity. I did not believe that any management would engage an actress of the standing of Caroline Blakiston merely to be talked of in Act I, and to say a few offstage lines before being killed (also offstage) at the beginning of Act Two. Obviously she would appear as advertised; but I resented being kept waiting for her, since nobody else onstage was much fun.

This was not altogether the actors' fault; they were required to expend a great many words on a simple situation, and make them seem not merely functional but witty. Robert Stephens, playing an inept assassin with delusions of sophistication, looks apt: his head-movements are aptly obsessive. A police sergeant called, and I longed to dispatch him to Tom Stoppard for rewriting. Mr Stephens rushed frantically around carrying a nude body, and I wanted Joe Orton's mummy (from Loot). Murderer, a slack thriller with pretensions, goes beyond ordinary tedium; the boredom it engenders is actually painful.

* * *

The Case in Question (Haymarket) is much better: the drama of a disputed inheritance (how the old words come back) derived by Ronald Millar from a C. P. Snow novel. Zena Walker as the embattled claimant stills the stalls with a passionate plea for distressed gentility, later delivering a directly contradictory anti-materialist diatribe with equal conviction. John Clements goes through similar contortions, but his are more subtly plotted (though less well acted): beginning as a good-fairy tycoon of immense wealth and urbanity (jokes against Wedgwood Benn, an intimate acquaintance with Dr Barnard), he emerges as a selfish manipulator determined to make Miss Walker rich despite herself.

Mr Millar spoils everything by hinting that Sir John may be Miss Walker's father, but you may close your ears to the hint and concentrate instead on the amoral force of riches, a theme also powerfully embodied in Simon Cadell's debonair young opportunist. A jovial trial scene lightens the atmosphere, and an austere appeal hearing impressively stiffens it.

An uneven evening. Happy Days (Old Vic) is the thinnest of Samuel Beckett's major plays, its first act over-insistent and even patronising. But its second deepens to situation-tragedy, magnificently handled by Peggy Ashcroft in Peter Hall's production, desperately steering her eyes, her thoughts and her voice (Fortnum-and-Mason, gently overlaid with Irish) away from engulfing disaster. For much of the evening I wondered whether her husband (mostly silent or invisible or both) really needed the distinguished attentions of Alan Webb; when he finally appeared full-length, the way he wore his hat (a topper), shot his cuffs and preened his moustache gave me my answer.
