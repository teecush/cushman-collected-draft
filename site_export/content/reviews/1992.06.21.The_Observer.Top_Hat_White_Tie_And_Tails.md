Top hat, white tie and tails

Robert Cushman

JAZZ SINGING
America's Great Voices from Bessie Smith to Bebop and Beyond
by Will Friedwald
Quartet £22.50

ONE of my favourite neglected vocalists, an intelligent lady named Barbara Lea, once wrote that she believed in jazz and in singing but not necessarily in jazz singing. She counts as a jazz singer none the less. Her work is ignored in Will Friedwald's book -- one of the few things I have against it -- but he takes a wide view of the territory.

He includes Fred Astaire. In 1952 Astaire, with Oscar Peterson as his accompanist, recorded a three-LP set of his signature songs: the most delightful vocal album I know. I was waiting in suspense to see whether Friedwald would include him, and on page 364 he came through with a passage that left me, as any good description of Astaire should, smiling. 'Astaire doesn't try to scat . . . or "sing like a horn" or any of the other things that hack reviewers think "jazz singers" are supposed to do.' He 'sensitively interpret[s] great songs through a dancer's sense of rhythmic style'.

Except that they didn't get their sense of rhythm from being dancers, the same holds good for most of the other figures in this chronicle. Innumerable lyricists have named Astaire as their favourite mouthpiece, and at the outset Friedwald notes that far from being note-bending for its own sake, vocal jazz 'operates as a key to lyric interpretation'. Manipulating the beat entails balancing the words, which calls for words and melodies worth interpreting. Here again Astaire provides a key.

The jazz-singer's repertoire, like the art itself, expands as you look at it, but in practice it has drawn most heavily on the songs that Gershwin, Berlin and their peers furnished for Broadway, Hollywood, and Tin Pan Alley. In one of the century's happier coincidences, jazz and musical theatre grew up together with the technology needed to preserve them and to inspire new practitioners.

Pursue these lines of repertoire and interpretation to their logical conclusion and you arrive at Frank Sinatra. Arriving after Bing Crosby, Louis Armstrong and others, Sinatra can hardly be judged America's first great singer, but Friedwald makes a convincing case for him as the first to be great on purpose. And if he isn't a jazz singer, then so much the worse for jazz.

Perhaps anticipating some purist grunts, Friedwald sometimes falls back on a parallel category that he calls 'adult pop', figureheaded by Sinatra and Peggy Lee, and best represented now -- in my judgement -- by Rosemary Clooney, who has found an easy route, comparable to Billie Holiday's, except that where Holiday's was bruised, hers is bruised-and-healed. It is rare to find Lee and Clooney taken with the seriousness they deserve, and unprecedented to find it accorded the male post-Sinatras, generally dismissed as faded showbiz. Friedwald, by distinguishing among them, rehabilitates them. At last I can take my Bobby Darin and Sammy Davis records out of hiding.

His technique is to start with an aphorism -- 'Billie Holiday's art is the kind that takes you deeper inside yourself and ultimately out again; Ella Fitzgerald's is the kind that takes you outside yourself and ultimately in again' -- and then dig in to what his favourites do and how they do it. He hangs this on a lively narrative thread that runs from Al Jolson, at whom he declines to scoff, to Bobby McFerrin who abandons conventional song but exhilarates anyway. He does all this with an authority unpardonable in one who turned 30 last year. But his youth may be an advantage. So much of this happened before he was born that he surveys an even field. It makes his dismissal of rock'n'roll particularly invigorating.

He has his deaf spots, principally comic or cerebral singers: Blossom Dearie scanted, the wonderfully brainy Jeri Southern buried in a list of 'cool singers', Pearl Bailey ignored though her clowning depended on her jazz-derived sense of time. And to dismiss Judy Garland as 'the stuff of anti-swing' is evasive; this 'square at the fair' interpreted pop songs better than most of the hip.

I wish too that some of the book were better written and much of it better edited. He sneaks his boldest assertion into a parenthesis; Bing Crosby 'gave American music the wherewithal at last to compete with (and, in my ears at least, surpass) opera and the European art-song tradition.' I agree. I would not make an equivalent claim for instrumental jazz, much as I love it. Jazz that disdains singing doesn't know its own strength.
