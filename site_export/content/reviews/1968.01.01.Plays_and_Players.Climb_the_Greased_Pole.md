The Mermaid should have a hit here, following along the line of their Bill Naughton successes. This is another family comedy involving the conversion to reason of a tyrannical father. There is, of course, the slight difference that *Climb the Greased Pole* isn’t set in Bolton. In fact, it takes place in Little Italy, New York, but don’t let that bother you. It doesn’t seem, to judge from their accents, to have bothered the cast.

Least of all does it seem to have perturbed Bernard Miles, whose paterfamilias here, give or take a few flattened a’s, is the exact double of Big Daddy Fitton in *All in Good Time*. Even if you can’t accept his monstrous un-Americanness (or, come to think of it, his equally monstrous un-Italianness), you may well be caught off guard by the touching delicacy that infuses this performance in its last moments. Earlier on he rampages vigorously enough, but once again destroys all his laugh lines by the apologetic cough with which he finishes them off. I suppose Mr Miles may have been attracted to this role, ethnic difficulties notwithstanding, by all the nostalgic glances it affords him at previous Mermaid performances. The Bill Naughton fathers are here (most notably the one in *Spring and Port Wine* whom, to be fair and for some inscrutable reason Mr Miles didn’t actually play), but would you believe a rationalist docker who, in protest against his wife’s Catholicism, christens his son *Galileo* yet? At the end Mr Miles, to prove he still loves her, sets off to climb the high greased pole that forms the centrepiece of the St Anthony’s Day celebrations, while those left below provide a stirring running commentary on his pride, progress and presumption. Does there lurk in Mr Miles a hitherto repressed ambition to play Master Builder Solness?

Unlike Ibsen, Vincent Longhi does not solely rely on towers and things for phallic effects. He brings in a giant rocket-shaped cheese, which broods over the action and was fittingly given centre-stage in the curtain-call. Aside from its fully exploited gag-potential, the cheese serves as a catalyst; Momma and Poppa Pappalardo have lived in uneasy truce for twenty-five years, but the cheese, Momma’s big specially-imported gesture to St Anthony, means war—the house split literally down the middle between God and Einstein. This is not the most mobile of situations, and the resolution is planned with rather too obvious an eye to offending neither side; Mr Longhi frankly admits that the family is his own, and he has not totally avoided the self-indulgent shapelessness that is endemic to autobiographical drama. But the vitality is genuine enough, and would probably be irresistible in the hands of an American cast. Frank Dunlop’s production is fast and efficient and only one of his actors is unbearable; but the all-round effort is too obvious. ‘Oh God, can’t you all stop *gesticulating* for just *five minutes*?’ That’s not a line from the play but it ought to be. Given a native cast, I think the play would bear comparison with Neil Simon; funnier than *Barefoot in the Park* but not as good as *The Odd Couple*.

As it happens, Libby Morris’s performance is pure Neil Simon—a Yiddisher Momma but never an Italian one; still, authentically American, and so fifty per cent ahead of the rest. Hers is the kind of crackling freewheeling display that always attracts superlatives, so let me be careful and say that I found her *comparatively* irresistible. In the second act she breaks out into a snatch of song about having this wonderful dream, which destroys the last shred of excuse for not bringing *Gypsy* to London, even if they can’t get Ethel Merman. Miss Morris’s recorded singing voice wafting us out of the theatre is an inspired touch and a great advance on the National Anthem. The neatest performance is given by Edward Atienza who spends the second act going mad in monk’s costume like John Stride in *Love for Love* though Mr Longhi’s dialogue here suffers rather by comparison with Congreve.

All great fun; what’s the Italian for schmaltz?
