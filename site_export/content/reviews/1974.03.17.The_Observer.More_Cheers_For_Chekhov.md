More cheers for Chekhov

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

I RHAPSODISED at length over the Actors' Company production of The Wood Demon when it first appeared at last year's Edinburgh Festival; so I had not planned to bestow more than a final paragraph of benediction on its arrival at Wimbledon Theatre, the first in a season of five plays.

But this rare Chekhov (rare and yet formidably well done) is so vivid an experience that I have to dwell on it once more. It presents an almost ideal conjunction of material with interpreters; in time this company must obviously get to one of Chekhov's later, sterner, tidier plays, but at present such a challenge might hem them in. This extravagant malformed piece allows them to run free; but some tight residual discipline in the writing, in David Giles's direction, and in themselves prevents them from running wild.

The more they are together, the happier we can be. Wimbledon is a barn of a theatre (it goes, I suppose, with the common and the tennis courts) and for the first quarter of an hour, mainly duologues and, would it be, triologues, I feared that the production's vital juices had evaporated. But once the stage had filled for the first-act luncheon party misgivings fled; everything was as good as I had remembered it, except for some bits which were better.

Several characterisations have matured: Juan Moreno's hopelessly jittery suitor for one and Tenniel Evans's Uncle George for a definite other. No, he is not Uncle Vanya nor, whatever Chekhov may have decided afterwards, was he meant to be. I have seen great Vanyas, Redgrave and Scofield, and they were all over the place. Mr Evans is firm-set, upon a rock of bitterness and rancour. His insights, unlike Vanya's, are firm and undeviating; during the second act, which he plays barefoot, his very toes curl at the unfairness of it all.

The most dramatic advance has been made by Marian Diamond, who registers every gradation in Helen's response, and final capitulation, to the futility and vulgarity engulfing her in the Professor's 26 gloomy rooms. And for the benefit of posterity (which likes to know these things) I must record a joyous action-packed moment in which Robin Ellis tosses into the air what he believes to be a peppermint, catches it in his mouth, swallows it, gags, inspects the container whence it came and inquires with jovial insouciance: "Is the Professor ill, then?" Having last week lamented the impending departure of the National Theatre from the Old Vic, I would add my voice to those urging that the Actors' Company should take over there--though preferably for no more than six months in a year. To mount a year-round repertory they would have to inflate themselves, and they might easily burst.

* * *

Chekhov, being a doctor, is a welcome presence in a theatrical week rife with bugs and germs and uncleanliness generally. At the Vaudeville there is Snap!; if the author, Charles Laurence, had not lost his nerve it would have been Clap. (Or did he perhaps fear confusion with the recent musical hit Applause?)

Most of the characters are infected; the hero begins by wondering whether it's good old gonorrhoea or what he rather charmingly describes as "this new thing," non-specific urethritis.

The first 30 minutes of the play are in fact quite springy; after that the conventions close in and it becomes just another mechanical West End comedy. Outside the sun is shining (this is mentioned so often that you may well feel like going outside to enjoy yourself); within, the hero's apartment is got up like a 1930s liner, so that the other characters can make jokes about it. Other running gags stumble unrelatedly through the evening: one character is a lapsed Catholic, another an unlapsed lesbian, very hearty and a champion dog-breeder. (Jolly well done, Elspeth March.) VD and homosexuality in the same play--are there no taboos left? (Well, yes, there are; characters are allowed to be gay or diseased, but not both. There is one minor character who is neither, but is toothy and an opera-fanatic. I mention her while I am in this parenthesis because I would not like to see her get loose outside it.)

But there is reason in most things, and in this thing there is Maggie Smith. Miss Smith is timing of unholy perfection, a larynx that can droop over the unlikeliest lines (I will swear her vocal cords have limp wrists) and draw laughter from them, and a pair of knees that waddle in tune with the dialogue and can be drawn up to hide her face in moments of deep embarrassment; she is also a person, gleeful, superior, and very nervous. When she appears in good plays (which is usually) her battery of accomplishments can seem excessive, but to this one she can do nothing but good, except in the closing stages when she can do nothing. The director is William Gaskill; maybe this is the kind of work he really yearned to do during his years at the Royal Court.

* * *

Industrial rather than sexual disease is the form there; Peter Ransley's Runaway is the latest of the North Country family dramas that usually seem to be running in Sloane Square, and a fair specimen of its kind. Daughter (Susan Tracy) has come home after a broken marriage and takes out her frustrations on her 11-year-old son (Kevin Moreton, no drama-school monster, but a real, breathing, swearing kid). He, not surprisingly, is the runaway; but so, in a way, is his grandfather. (Bill Owen, too soft--in both senses--but a kindly presence none the less.) A union leader, he has partially betrayed his workmates by keeping quiet about the presence in the works of a poisonous chemical; and now he himself may have developed cancer from it. Mr Ransley strikes sparks from a number of themes and encounters, but he fails to weld them together.

* * *

Once again the most reviving thing in town is a revival. The West End is strewn with them, and Tennessee Williams's A Streetcar Named Desire (Piccadilly) is the best. This is a dream of a play, with dialogue that glows on actors' lips. I had not realised how much of it has passed into history: "Sometimes there's God so quickly"; "I have always depended on the kindness of strangers."

That last quivering line of broken Blanche Dubois is delivered by Claire Bloom with a marvellous dazed dignity. There is sweetness in it, and a hint of flirtatiousness, and a subtler hint of priggishness and the faintest memory of faded aristocracy. It is in fact Blanche, the southern schoolteacher as nymphomaniac, gathered together and placed before your eyes: the summation of the most moving performance I have seen in a year.

It is also a constantly surprising performance, since Miss Bloom's resources continually outpace expectation; she looks, at first glance, limited. Blanche's credo (and her undoing) is that she prefers magic to realism, "and if that is a sin, then let me be damned for it." Miss Bloom, like Mr Williams, gives us both magic and realism in generous measure. I have no intention whatever of damning them.

There is a slight atmospheric glibness about the play; the lighting and sound effects are written in (dead on cue), and any director must be grateful for a play set in New Orleans, a chance to fill the scene-breaks with a dreamy jazz cornet. Edwin Sherrin's production goes with the text, a rewarding journey and there are no weaknesses in his cast. Actually, there are amazing strengths: Martin Shaw, insolent and brutally honest as Stanley (only his bellowing lets him down), Morag Hood, whose Stella is incarnate sense to Blanche's sensibility, and as Mitch, a gentle shambles, Joss Ackland, whose ingratiatingly unexpected question "How much do you think I weigh?" is something else posterity should know about.
