Illuminating Labour

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Oxford.

HISTORY tells us that in the Forties and Fifties the Lyric, Hammersmith, performed a valuable function as a launching-pad for plays felt by their producers to be too specialised in appeal for a direct assault on the West End, but capable of being slipped in at a later date to raise the tone of the capital.

That category of plays has grown alarmingly; and it is more than encouraging that the new Lyric, as well as reproducing its predecessor's architecture and decoration, has inherited its policy. We cannot afford to lose it; nobody else is doing the job. No other London theatre could so appropriately have taken the chance on Peter Jenkins's first play; and before dissecting it I had better say that Illuminations is an auspicious debut. It cracks along, taking character, plot and dialogue in its stride. What it doesn't have is unity, which may be appropriate, since Mr Jenkins is writing about the Labour Party, but isn't helpful. He seems to be writing three plays at once.

First there is an allegory, treating equally of the state of the party, of the nation, and of Blackpool, which is where, during a party conference, the play is set. A head porter delivers an opening monologue about how nothing ever changes, whoever's in power; it turns out that he's referring mainly to the state of trade in the four-star hotel that employs him and shelters the delegates, but we can laugh ironically at that.

At curtain-fall a Labour Government has left office and all the characters link hands to sing, with varying degrees of enthusiasm, The Red Flag with the lights of the city winking garishly behind them. These devices might possibly work if they were marked boldly out from everything else, but Richard Cottrell's production tries instead to squeeze them in.

Then there is straight political debate. Late on we have a stand-up fight between two Ministers: on my right, smooth Martin Gale, on my left, rugged Ron Henriques. This is a fizzy scene with Mr Jenkins getting things off his chest through Martin while trying to be dramatically fair to Ron. But he is unfair, and not through any inequality of rhetoric. We don't know Ron; up to now this has been Martin's play, much the most substantial of the three on offer, and it is too late to change tracks.

Martin is Home Secretary, and under fire over public schools. He has a son at Charterhouse; actually his estranged wife is responsible for the boy's education, but his enemies don't know that and he won't talk about it. We're already into a specialised dramatic situation: specialised comic, in fact, since Mr Jenkins surrounds his hero with nice observations on the rigours of luxurious living away from home and with a lady journalist (very Sunday Times), whose indifference to what are supposed to be burning issues tellingly hits off disillusion chic.

And Martin himself, cursing room-service while donning a cap to go off and eat with the real people, is a risible figure, especially as played by Paul Eddington, a high comedian (one of our tallest in fact) whose gift for undercutting eventually extends to himself; fully capable of passion, he still carries absurdity with him. His entourage, consisting of Nigel Stock as an attendant trade-union baron and Bill Nighy as a rising young something, is excellently played; other performances, compounding the imbalance, range from competent to wretched. Mr Jenkins's illuminations are fitful but sparky.

* * *

Unemployment is mentioned. In Snoo Wilson's Space Ache (Tricycle, Kilburn) it's dramatised. Christine, a London teenager without prospects, elects to be deep-frozen for a million years in the hope that there will be a job, and a better world, when she thaws. However, due to some sort of foul-up on the space-craft that's supposed to be taking her, in a sealed canister, for safe-keeping, she revives, and this sends the ship right off course: dislocating most of the solar system, travelling sideways through time, and ending up on Neptune. (Look, I only like Mr Wilson's plays; I don't claim to follow all the details.)

Neptune turns out to be a super-Sybaritic sort of place where you play the fruit machines to obtain an orgasm (and get your arms cut off by the proprietor if you hit too many jackpots) and where pain, as a rare commodity, is highly valued. The health service is highly developed; the surgeons, as Shaw once suggested in The Doctor's Dilemma, pay you for the privilege of letting them cut you up. Chrissy gets rich from having an abortion, and decides anyway that she wants to live, now. (What do you know, Mr Wilson has written a sci-fi temperature-reversed version of The Lady's Not for Burning.)

All ends happy, in a sour kind of way, and Mr Wilson, along with a beguiling imagination and a supply of off-the-wall gags, offers a surprising amount of goodwill. This play is a doodle (the ship's crew are a prolonged RAF joke), but it is not a doddle and Kenneth Chubb's production, which comes untidily wrapped in silver foil, is not up to it. The actors are left, by and large, stranded; some of them may deserve it. Frances Barber as Chris survives best, which seems suitable. Mr Chubb's sloppiness is probably preferable to the solemnity this playwright used to elicit, but is no substitute for the precision he needs.

* * *

There was plenty of tacky acting too in the Oxford Playhouse production of Ariane Mnouchkine's Mephisto (the loss from the title role of Ian McDiarmid, replaced by the director Gordon McDougall, who isn't an actor and doesn't claim to be, was probably demoralising), but the play was worth seeing. Based on a novel by Klaus (son of Thomas) Mann it follows a group of actors and other artists in Germany from Hitler's defeat in 1923 to his ascension in 1933.

Most are Communists; the most vociferous, famous for his Mephistopheles and based on an actual and most celebrated figure, ends up going along with the Nazis, selling his soul, if you like, to himself. Earlier he had been the hardest of all, a bully in fact, on Nazi sympathisers; the play makes scrupulous moral distinctions, and its closing scenes, horrific without explicit horror, tear at you. It sprawls; its narrator seems its least necessary figure. It ends with a salute to the fallen; honourably, it is also a lament for the misled.

* * *

At least at the National Theatre there is a play with a full cast of ace performers. Mind you, in Harold Pinter's The Caretaker (Lyttelton), the last English classic, there are only three characters; and Warren Mitchell's tramp seems somewhat inhibited by his Welsh accent (which is necessary to the part; read the lines). He is an expert wheedler and, towards the close, a very nasty threat; and one realises, as in Death of a Salesman, what a contained actor he is. There are no stray bits of Warren Mitchell left flapping about at the edges; everything goes into the character.

Jonathan Pryce does a snaky virtuoso turn on Mick, oozing through his cadenzas in the kind of cockney (patented by Harold Steptoe) in which all sound seems to proceed from the gums. Kenneth Cranham meanwhile conveys a stilled aggression behind Aston's lobotomised calm; he is recognisably his brother's brother. This falls short of being a definitive production; Kenneth Ives's direction lacks defining power. But it impresses.
