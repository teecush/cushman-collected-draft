Hero of the Big Top

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

TEN minutes from the final curtain the stage of Barnum (Palladium) erupts with movement and colour. Clowns clown, jugglers juggle, high-wires are walked, trapezes swung from and batons twirled, stick-puppet wild animals appear in shadow-play upstage. In the centre struts Michael Crawford, resplendently uniformed, while cast and band exhort him fortissimo to 'Join the Circus.'

It should be exhilarating. It is, a bit. But there is something curiously redundant about it. Before this climactic sequence, we have watched Barnum as an entrepreneur of midgets, white whales, centenarian and bearded women, and voluptuous Swedish nightingales.

The difference between his kind of raree-show and the circus proper is bound to seem academic, especially as the circus is this production's chosen metaphor throughout. The company is made up of singing dancing acrobats, the set is a heavily strung big top, a ringmaster announces each crisis in the hero's life; leaving his wife for a brief fling with Jenny Lind, Barnum approaches her by tight-rope, singing. So the show's climax proudly leads us to exactly where we've been all evening.

The general pattern is that Barnum finds a new attraction, and then a number outlines the attraction's attractions. Michael Stewart's lyrics accordingly boil down to a series of nimble but static tongue-twisters. The Jenny Lind romance turns out to be a false trail, and the only conflict is between Barnum and his wife who wants him to be respectable. She temporarily succeeds when he goes into politics, to the accompaniment of a pounding blues called 'Black and White' with a stage design to match.

Eventually he revolts and the stage goes garish again; we are meant to rejoice but I found myself lamenting the departure of the most stylish visuals of the evening. The theme is fully stated in an oppressive ballad called 'The Colours of My Life'; this however is the only blemish on Cy Coleman's score which again demonstrates his ability to spark new and quirky life out of old forms. One song, 'I Like Your Style,' may be the first ever jazz-jig. A succession of cheer-up songs threatens to turn the show into a hip 'Hello Dolly' but Mr Coleman usually contrives some way to throw them jauntily off-centre. But here too the show's scheme proves self-defeating; the sophistication written into the tunes is largely erased by having them orchestrated for a deafening circus band.

Mr Crawford won the standingest ovation I have ever witnessed, a tribute to his energy and intrepidity. (William C. Witter, the ringmaster, spends the whole evening nonchalantly shinning up and down ropes without gaining so much as a hand-clap but then he isn't the star.) But Mr Crawford has not managed to transform his personality; his Barnum is curiously harmless. Jim Dale on Broadway gave the part more swagger but he was still unable to warm it up. For the truth is that beneath all the energy and inventiveness there is a very large hole.

* * *

I responded far less to Alan Bleasdale's Having a Ball at the Lyric, Hammersmith, than when it was premiered a few months ago in Oldham. Three explanations suggest themselves: (a) London critics relax their standards out of town; (b) the Oldham production was better; (c) I boobed. There is probably truth in all three, but I'll stick with (b), though I have to qualify it immediately. Technically, in the matters of sight-gags and visual finesse, Alan Dossor's London direction is much superior. It loses on human values, and most importantly on the most important.

The play concerns three men who come to a private clinic to be vasectomised. They are a council official, a one-time school bully, and his former victim, who is just beginning to get clever. This last was played by David Ross at Oldham as a fine example of the turning Northern worm. Mr Ross is still in the cast, but in the small role of a male nurse (which he plays very well). Meanwhile Philip Donaghy, most recently seen as Jesus and St Paul, tries without much success to change his uncompromising spots. But the play depends inordinately on getting us on his side.

For what has decided him to have no more children is the threat of nuclear war. If we can't swallow this theme when he introduces it we are really going to gag when it becomes blatant; as it does when the councillor, under sedation, starts babbling of privileged shelters and martial law. Other aspects of the play--which at Oldham seemed a rough but bracing life-and-death farce--start falling away, notably the conflict between the nurse and the (female) surgeon over the ethics of abortion. At Oldham this was at least resolved; at Hammersmith it is just forgotten (or was there a literal loss of memory on the first night?) disconcerting the audience so that the McGillian last line was received with puzzlement instead of uproar. There are still plenty of laughs, but they don't build.

* * *

Waiting for Godot is given immortal embodiment at the Roundhouse by Max Wall and Trevor Peacock. Mr Wall's Vladimir is the more dapper of the two; he does sport a bow-tie even if it's pinned somewhere far down his lapel, and his tones of high-strung indignation, resonant with sour memories of former glories and recent miseries, suit the text uncannily. Mr Peacock meanwhile is as grumpy as grubby, with the bedrock humour that comes of enforced tolerance, twinkling only when he gets the chance literally to put the boot in.

The funnier the play gets, the more disturbing; these two clowns don't have to manufacture laughs; they make a human relationship out of a professional bond. Wolfe Morris throws away much of Pozzo through huffing and puffing, though when he allows himself some legato he comes through. Gary Waldhorn's careful delivery of Lucky's tirade is the best I have heard.

* * *

Observer readers may have noticed that Clive James has written a new long poem called Charles Charming's Challenges on His Pathway to the Throne and that he is reading it, with assistance, at the Apollo. He's queering his own pitch; for though a brilliant off-the-cuffer he goes to pieces when confronted by a script, and his method of pulling himself together is to come down heavily on all the words like a Movietone newsreader with a grievance. He thus spoils many of the best jokes in a text that rarely falls below a decent level of funniness and sometimes--as in a sustained fantasia on Gordonstoun--gets well above it.

For the opposition Russell Davies blows horns of steadily increasing magnitude and impersonates on a very wide front (Pamela Stephenson, doing the ladies, has less scope and less tact until she springs her Lady Di in a coda) but his most important contribution is to establish Charles himself as shy, well-meaning and thoroughly sweet, thus giving the evening a dramatic centre: one that it needs since the media-figures brought into the princely presence for the purpose of instant lampoon are pretty indiscriminately assembled. The author's views on royalty were unwittingly summarised by another accomplished coupleteer as:

Always keep ahold of nurse
For fear of finding something worse.
