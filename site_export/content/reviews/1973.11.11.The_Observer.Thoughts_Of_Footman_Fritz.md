Thoughts of footman Fritz

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

The scene of *A Lesson in Blood and Roses* (Royal Shakespeare Company at The Place), says the programme, is the library of the Villa von Kellig on the outskirts of Vienna in 1848, Europe's year of revolutions.

Farrah's set is something of a non-starter as a library, being completely devoid of books, but this conceivably is a comment on the essential illiteracy of the ruling classes; and otherwise the stage looks pleasing, an arrangement of plush curtains, plusher doors, and elegant furniture strategically placed to give the characters plenty of room to attack each other. One thing that John Wiles's play does not lack is conflict.

Fritz the footman is a revolutionary; he at any rate can read and does, aloud, from a suspiciously modern red book. He informs us, by way of prologue, that the art of the successful guerrilla lies in defeating the enemy with his own weapons. And though it takes more than an anachronistic reference to Mao to disconcert the hardened audiences of today, Fritz feels obliged to follow it with a joky reassurance that we are, in the right theatre. Even as he said it I began to wonder.

The objects of Fritz's guerrilla strategy are the children of the house. While daddy, a general, is away at the wars, they have been left in the charge of a governess who, despite a Miss Julie-ish passion for Fritz, believes in the established order. She, however, is on the way out, to be replaced by a male tutor from Paris. The Monsieur duly appears, to be greeted by the footman with considerably more warmth than he ever bestowed on the poor mademoiselle.

It seems that Monsieur is Fritz's half-brother and that his entry into the house has been arranged by machiavellian intrigues, whose full subtleties I cannot divulge since the author has neglected to. Monsieur sets about disorientating the boy and girl Von Kellig; beginning in a small but significant way with the casual assertion that sound travels faster than light, he goes on to instruct them in Archimedes's Premium, whereby two and two make at least five and preferably more, and to disabuse them of certain common geographical fallacies. One can see that if his ideas caught on, and the children of the rich all came to doubt that Rome and Florence were both in Italy, the popular cause might eventually benefit, particularly in the event of a pitched battle being fought near either city.

But this does not seem to be quite what Fritz wants (what he does want is another matter on which Mr Wiles keeps silence) and he remonstrates. Monsieur, however, is now unstoppable, fired by the memory of a public orgy (rather stirringly described), at which all the whores in town gave their services, freely and repeatedly, "for the people." He has seen the future and it fornicates: where his brother quoted Mao at us, he proffers de Sade. (And as the Marquis said to Jean Paul Marat, when they met one day in that house of Weiss near Charenton, "What's the good of a revolution without general copulation?") So he wreaks untold havoc on the children's bodies and, indeed, on their sexual identity; by the time he is through, they are wearing each other's clothes or none at all, little Annamarie is out killing rabbits, young Max-Ernst is cooking them, and the library is frankly a mess. Such is their decadent state that they are chopping up the furniture for firewood; could this have been what Fritz meant when he spoke of "turning the tables"? By this time Fritz has been shot for attempting to interfere with Monsieur's personal hedonistic revolution. But the more established puritanism represented by Mademoiselle (I bet you had forgotten about her) proves harder to beat and it is she who by a final turn of the screw (the apposite phrase) wins possession of the children. Or nearly final, because, but I must not deprive you of the pleasure of guessing for yourselves Mr Wiles's highly foreseeable surprise ending.

Schematic drama always has a certain built-in force and this is occasionally generated when Mr Wiles's representative figures are at each other's throats. But the higher melodrama is a risky and risible genre, and the author's verbal tactlessness, which first shows itself in the supposedly disarming prologue and grows steadily thereafter, finally scuppers it. Ben Kingsley puts his louring forehead and snarling delivery to good use as Fritz. One of the RSC's intensity specialists, he is here matched against their acknowledged leader, John Wood, bubbling over with intellectual bravura as Monsieur.

* * *

English history takes a beating in the cause of youth in Paul Foster's *Elizabeth I*, mounted at the Theatre Upstairs in association with the Royal Court's Young People's Theatre Scheme. I am not too sure about the purport of this piece; I expected a straight piece of demystification, but though a savage opening song ("My God, what an age") suggests a critical approach, when Mr Foster gets up close to Elizabeth he finds he rather likes her. Carole Hayman plays her as an amiable fishwife (which is how she plays most parts) who wants to keep the peace, however funny it will make her look in the history books. The business of Mary Stuart is shadier (the play goes very heavy and reverent for her execution) and I didn't much care for Bess's councillors: Francis Bacon is thoroughly unsavoury and, besides, you can't understand a gnomic word he says. Still, nobody's immaculate or, as the delightful Charlotte Cornwell puts it with a soft rock backing: "The Queen of England is a virgin queen, ho, ho." Walter Donohue's production is lively, though the grouping (in an old-fashioned show one would have criticised the grouping) is definitely sub-standard.

* * *

The situation downstairs is altogether more reassuring: D. H. Lawrence is to the Court as Pinero to Chichester, a thoroughly reliable commodity, and *The Merry-Go-Round* is the most enjoyable exhumation yet. The loving care of Peter Gill's direction is again phenomenal, from the opening scene in which opposing characters are denoted by their approach to the making of a bed to the familiar set-piece of the coalminer's homecoming. At Mr Gill's stately pace the play's parade of lovers, tentatively pairing and repairing, is more of a processional than a carousel and the final burst of speed for a traditional comic finale jars horribly. But for most of the time Lawrence's style of playmaking is his own invention and it serves him well. So do the players: Marjorie Yates rampaging round her parents' cottage in a fury of young widowhood, George Malpas as an aged bashful suitor and, as the object of every man's attention, Mary Miller, a passionately sensible heroine who would have delighted Jane Austen. Derrick O'Connor is a towering presence and his major outburst of violence is amazing, but the casualness of his speech puts him out of place and period.

* * *

John Hopkins's *Economic Necessity* (at the new Haymarket, Leicester) is the victim of its own structure. Beginning with the familiar situation of a man who quits his job because it no longer makes sense to him, it wearily catalogues the reactions of his friends and relations and conscientiously charts the estrangement of his wife. Finally we are allowed a glimpse inside his mind, where an awareness of the world's cruelty is blotting out everything else. Whether this was the cause or the effect of his withdrawal is not stated; either way it bites and frightens, but it has no organic relation to the rest of the play, and the final effect is of laborious sentimentality.
