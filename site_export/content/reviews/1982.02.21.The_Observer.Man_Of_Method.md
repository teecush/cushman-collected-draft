Man of Method

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Lee Strasberg, founder of the Actors' Studio, who died last week.

IF EVER there was an eminence grise of the theatre, it was Lee Strasberg: The public saw very little of his work directly, but he is credited as the major influence on two generations of American actors, and through them on two generations of American playwriting. American movies would be almost unrecognisable without his pupils and proteges. And it was in a movie -- 'The Godfather, Part Two' -- that he himself made his greatest popular impact, at the age of 73.

His career was comparatively simple. He was born in Poland in 1901; he was eight when his parents settled in America. He became an actor in 1925, becoming known for his work with the Theatre Guild, the foremost 'prestige' management in New York. Among his contemporaries there were two other theatrical gurus, Harold Clurman and Sanford Meisner.

The late Twenties saw the first major surge of American playwriting, most of it naturalistic, even journalistic. Sidney Kingsley's hospital drama, 'Men in White,' won a Pulitzer Prize, and Strasberg was in it. He became at first obsessed with finding a new American acting style to match the new drama.

In 1929 the Group Theatre came into existence, nurturing the talents of writer Clifford Odets, producer Cheryl Crawford and directors Clurman, Robert Lewis and Elia Kazan. Strasberg emerged as chief theorist, promoting an Americanised version -- now unfortunately but inescapably known as the Method -- of the Russian gospel of Konstantin Stanislavsky.

One crucial difference in circumstances was that Stanislavsky had a classical tradition to work from, and Strasberg hadn't. In the Thirties American drama meant exclusively contemporary drama, and America, first shaken by the Depression, then stirred by the New Deal, had little use for external disciplines. This at least seems to have been Strasberg's problem, conscious or not; the charge against him has always been that he emphasised the internal mechanics of acting, while neglecting visible and audible technique. He was not a showman, as Stanislavsky unquestionably was (Chekhov used to complain about it) and as both Kazan and Lewis became. Clurman's book 'The Fervent Years' provides evidence of how unharmonious the Group Theatre could be.

Nevertheless, between Strasberg and Kazan at any rate a fruitful partnership emerged. The Group collapsed during the war, but Strasberg went on to found the Actors' Studio (as a sort of post-graduate gymnasium), while Kazan became Broadway's leading dramatic director, developing the actors Strasberg trained. We forget now that for the 15 years after the war America led world theatre. Kazan had the early work of Arthur Miller and Tennessee Williams to direct, and Strasberg's actors to play them; if some other plays lacked fantasy and invention, the gap was supplied by the musicals; two men -- Kazan and Jerome Robbins -- effectively divided the territory between them.

Then, around 1960, it all fell apart. It was a legacy of the Thirties anyway, with the tensions of the old Left kept bitterly alive by the challenge of McCarthyism. When Odets, Williams and Miller slackened, there was no one to replace them; their contemporaries and successors offered only routine naturalism, psychologically intense but narrow -- the only kind of play, it was frequently said, that Studio actors knew how to act. There has -- despite Albee, despite Mamet, despite Shepard, despite McClure -- been no general revival. Besides, everyone had gone to Hollywood, Kazan leading such actors as Marlon Brando, James Dean, Rod Steiger and Paul Newman.

Those who thought Method acting mere behaviourism -- and it's certainly the opposite of character acting as practised by an Olivier -- thought this only logical. But, from Barrymore onwards, the best American actors, of whatever school, have always defected to the movies; the logic of stardom, emotional as well as economic, propels them. It may be significant that Strasberg's most publicised pupil was that greatest of non-actresses, Marilyn Monroe (who could hardly make a move on set without guidance from Strasberg's wife Paula), but of his other proteges nobody ever accused Julie Harris of being untheatrical, and those who saw Brando on stage in 'Truckline Cafe' and 'A Streetcar Named Desire' found him mesmeric.

That the Method failed when it faced up to the classics is undeniable. Kazan burned himself on a Jacobean tragedy and never tried again; Strasberg, having finally turned the Actors' Studio into a company, came up with a humiliating 'Three Sisters' (with Sandy Dennis, George C. Scott and Kim Stanley in the cast). He failed as a director. But teachers are as important for what they are and what they inspire as what they do, and his continued influence down to Al Pacino and Robert de Niro speaks for itself. At the very least he got actors, in Britain as well as America, thinking more intensely (if often talking too intensely) about acting. And when he came to play the senior mobster in 'Godfather II' his effortlessly towering performance proved that he could not only deliver the goods; he was the goods.
