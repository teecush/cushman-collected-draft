The whirlpool's edge

ONE of the more sinister and shaming things about the Vietnam war is that few can remember when it actually started. David Rabe's Streamers (Roundhouse), which is set in 1965, does nothing to fill in the international background, but it offers a first-hand, and first-class, picture of American soldiers poised on the edge of the whirlpool.

They are still in barracks in Virginia. The war surfaces only briefly in their conversation; there is a lighthearted discussion on the relative demerits as aggressors of LBJ and Ho Chi Minh whose upshot is that they personally have no desire to go out and get unpleasantly killed, whichever. The knowledge (half-formed in them and assumed by us) that they almost certainly will be naturally underpins the play, though, as it turns out, ends almost equally grisly are to be met with at home.

The play posits and evokes but does not explain a climate of violence. It is a chicken-and-egg situation; Mr Rabe does not appear to have decided whether the war was the cause or the effect of the mentality he describes. I suppose that is a flaw, but since nobody else has decided either I cannot reckon it a grievous one. Unarguably, though, Mr Rabe's own war service gives life to the play; its flare-ups are both credible and inevitable.

To me at least the London production, which comes most worthily from Liverpool Playhouse, is more persuasive than the high-gloss, high-tension version directed by Mike Nichols in New York two seasons ago. That one sealed the play off in its own special world: a fearsome but unrecognisable ritual. The British actors have to try harder, grapple with the accents, explain more; making it accessible to themselves, they do the same for us.

Timing helps as well. Two years ago the American nightmare seemed just, but thankfully, over; Mr Rabe, having promised the world a Vietnam trilogy, seemed to be completing it too late and wallowing a bit. Now the deja vu seems more explicable, and the wisdom of going back to base more evident. Other things beside the war are simmering, though Vietnam helped greatly to focus them. Someone said that the war was a matter of 'white men sending black men to kill yellow men'; another that the army, especially in Saigon, institutionalised homosexuality.

Streamers gives us the start of both processes; it serves, among other things, to remind us of a lost naivete. At its centre is Richie, flauntingly gay -- except that the word at the time is 'queer.' You're not really queer? say his friends incredulously, and Richie does have his moments of uncertainty. He shares a room with Billy, a nervous college graduate, and Roger, who is black and the best adjusted of the three. They are all privileged soldiers; the underside of the army is represented by Carlyle, also black, aggressively bisexual, and the only character with fully-formed intimations of cataclysm.

But foreshadowing it, he also hastens it on; ferociously disinclined to die for a country that has done nothing for him, trusting only in his senses, he contemptuously seduces Richie and then, cornered, knifes two men. He is festering America: sociologically a victim, dramatically a daemon. If he is the immediate future, the past is personified in two veteran sergeants, full of funny drunk stories; mainly about parachutes that failed to open.

The play is hardly flawless; when not actually letting blood it can be verbose or portentous. But it ranks (with Bedroom Farce and The Bundle) as one of the three best plays in London, and its awesome strength is preserved in Leslie Lawton's production. With no visible sweat Don Warrington brings the animal Carlyle to immediate life; James Aubrey, Jeffery Kissoon and Trevor Jones as the three new musketeers show us the fraying edge of civilisation.

American plays are welcome, apart from anything else, as a means of forcing our actors into hysteria; not, of course, that one wants it to become a habit, but the occasional confrontation must do them good. Simon Gray's The Rear Column (Globe) -- another military play, though the title might suggest a backward English equivalent of The Front Page -- has characters in equally desperate straits but more reticent about it. They go to pieces, they tell us they are going to pieces, occasionally they even display their symptoms, but they never make us feel their disintegration. Something tells me I should prefer Mr Gray, who is all subtlety and suggestion, to Mr Rabe, who lets most of it hang out, but I cannot concur.

The play's faults are not all necessarily the author's. One relates to fashion: his five principal characters are of the Victorian officer class and we are conditioned to expect either an expose or (exceptionally) a defence of their code. Mr Gray simply presents it, and our expectations (to which, for a couple of moments, he seems to subscribe) get in his way.

Another is due to history: the rear column is a back-up force for H. M. Stanley on his last expedition, left waiting for him in the Congo for more than a year, short of men and also of supplies, since Stanley has left them no explicit permission to break open provisions and the officer in charge, not wishing to be caught out by a greasy civilian, will do nothing without orders.

The length of their endurance is its essence, but it is difficult to dramatise; things get worse, but not different, character-conflicts recur. Since Mr Gray writes elegantly, knows his men and has an exotic setting going for him, the play is interesting in 10-minute bursts, but it never fulfils what it promises. Nor is the acting in Harold Pinter's production all that satisfying. Barry Foster underplays the CO's rigid lunacy, though Jeremy Irons as his second deals superbly with the play's central irony. Gentle and considerate, Uncle Osborne of Journey's End to his commander's battered Stanhope, he precipitates final moral breakdown by conniving at a cannibal feast among the locals. Is this, he asks, any worse than the flogging and killing they have all indulged in? A good question, not necessarily in need of an answer, but late in coming.

James Saunders's Bodies (Hampstead) has an awful first act in which two couples, dimly lit, remember mainly in soliloquy (a duologue is a great treat) their reciprocal adulteries. Act Two mercifully brings them together, turns up the lights, and contrasts the satisfaction found by one pair through encounter therapy (physical peace and satisfaction are all that matter, forget all yearnings) with the full-scale middle-class angst of the other.

Most of the latter belongs to Dinsdale Landen, and the play is really entirely within him, since he is about one-third on his antagonist's side and knows it. Given the few jokes of the first half, Mr Landen also has the gratifying rhetoric and self-torment of the second and deals nobly with both.

At the Royal Court a rather clumsy revival of The Bear, Chekhov's variant on the primal Widow-of-Ephesus theme (lady lured out of weeds into arms of impulsive wooer), precedes David Suchet's justly-praised solo rendition of The Kreutzer Sonata, Tolstoy's confession of an uxoricide. The story itself matters less than the misogyny beneath it, and that in its turn is dwarfed by the self-hatred that lies under all.
