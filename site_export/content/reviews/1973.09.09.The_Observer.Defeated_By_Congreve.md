Defeated by Congreve

THEATRE

Robert Cushman on a revival of 'Way of the World.'

FIRST it is necessary to mop up after the Edinburgh Festival. During their two weeks at the Lyceum the Actors' Company followed 'The Wood Demon' with The Way of the World; both productions are now embarked on a provincial tour (for the next two weeks they are at the Arts Theatre, Cambridge). Having been elevated by Chekhov, the company were brought down a few bumps by Congreve. Such has been the way of this world for a good two and a half centuries.

David William's production can claim an honourable place in the doom-stretching line of good tries. That is not nothing; and for its first scene I had even higher hopes of it. The action has been advanced from the close of the seventeenth century to the beginning of the twentieth; in the cool spaciousness of a St James's club, the sparring of Fainall and Mirabell has a deliberate casualness which is both dangerous and appropriate. Robin Ellis's Fainall is a robust schemer and hypocrite; Edward Petherbridge's Mirabell is barely more likeable or trustworthy, but his fastidious pallor commands respect. In a battle of styles he could be the one to back; and since that is largely what the play consists of, this establishment of the combatants should yield valuable dividends.

They fail, however, to accrue. It is difficult for any director to keep the thread of a personal duel through the labyrinth of Congreve's plotting, and Mr William gets lost with his predecessors. The antagonists prove unequally balanced, and the choice of period unduly favourable to the villains. Fainall's Victorian mustachios seem to grow curlier by the minute, while he plays a series of vigorous duets with Paola Dionisotti's striking Mrs Marwood. These two characters, traditionally dismissed as mechanical creatures of intrigue, are emerging as the most convincing in the play. We are no longer scared or sceptical of melodrama, and their disregard of finer feelings works positively to their advantage.

For this play's finer feelings are notoriously difficult to convey, wrapped up as they are in the sad brilliance of its style. Passion, say the critics, underlies it all, but the passion of the schemers is far easier to realise than that of the lovers. Mirabell figures in both guises: Mr Petherbridge is a curiously detached actor and the distance which he so effectively maintains in his dealings with Fainall or the play's various fops is scarcely reduced in his courtship of Millamant. Caroline Blakiston (who enters pastoral-fashion, carrying a shepherd's crook like a large Bo-Peep) brings warmth and poise on with her, but little sense of erotic challenge: 'What would you give that you could help loving me?'--that line, with its combination of cruelty and tenderness, is the heart of the play; their ability to feel and inspire such emotions constitutes the true victory of Millamant and Mirabell over their adversaries. Neither Miss Blakiston's delivery of the line nor Mr Petherbridge's response to it has left any impression on me. (She, at least, is battling against historic odds; radio be thanked, even junior citizens now know how Edith Evans sounded in the part.)

Other complaints: the later settings look both fussy and cheap, and the same adjectives may serve for the playing of the fops (especially Witwoud), whose scenes can and do drag horribly. Help is to hand in a lively posse of servants, and in John Woodvine's Salopian intruder who, in what Arnold Bennett called the great cause of cheering us all up, does noble battle with a tiger-skin rug.

* * *

Stanley Eveling is one of the few current dramatists to whom one turns with a positive expectation of pleasure: his Union Jack (and Bonzo) (Traverse) was predictably the brightest new play of the Festival. For much of its length I felt some resentment at its apparent slightness; I laughed but nothing grabbed me. By the end, however, Mr Eveling had fashioned from his Boy Scout extravaganza a chilling image of alienated youth. Very alienated and very young: just a couple of pubescent schoolkids knifing their piggish elders and gleefully shouting their four-letter word for the day.

The seniors nearly justify such blithe contempt; the only honest soul among them is the scoutmaster, Union Jack himself, a hung-up adolescent of 40 or so, depicted by Richard Ireson with a caricaturist's unflinching conviction. You can almost smell his 'halitosis.' Katy Manning makes a dazzling addition to Mr Eveling's impressive gallery of nymphets (fantasies of juvenile seduction almost invariably furnish the most persuasive scenes of his plays) and Susan Carpenter draws a sharp, resonant picture of a district nurse who, to judge from the debauched angle at which her cigarette slouches from her lips, is clearly in training for matron of St Trinian's. Mike Ockrent's production makes exuberant use of the Traverse stage.

* * *

Returning to London, I can add little to other critics' findings on An Inspector Calls (Mermaid); like them I responded to J. B. Priestley's mastery of traditional stagecraft, agog to know what happened next or, since this inspector is concerned to elucidate the truth of past events, what happened last. I responded, too, to much of the acting in Bernard Miles's production: Philip Stone, his entry fittingly preceded by a cry of 'Give us some more light,' is so perfectly the police inspector, so unassuming and yet so authoritative with his bald head, his raincoat and his gentle disdain for plutocratic pastimes, like golf, that we will willingly accept him as a symbol of anything the author chooses (and, man, he chooses). The family he investigates is notably tough at the top--Campbell Singer as the cocksure industrialist and especially Elizabeth Tyrrell as his genteel armour-plated wife.

The family smugness proves impenetrable; so much so that a second inspector is required, and is on the way as the play ends. Gogol closed The Government Inspector on an identical note, but for him it was a comic climax and his instinct was surely correct. Mr Priestley has had to work hard to keep laughter out of this piece (despite some facile ironies of the kind associated with his 'time' plays), and this, coupled with the relentlessness of his reiterated if humane message, makes for a thinness in texture.

* * *

Finishing Touches (Apollo) is an American comedy about a menopausal marriage, whose central role has been Anglicised for Wendy Craig; she should have declined the compliment since her lines are still recognisably American and she is landed with all the wrong rhythms. The author, Jean Kerr, once wrote a book called Please Don't Eat the Daisies, about how it felt to be the wife of a theatre critic; since I might one day be married to the wife of a theatre critic this seemed to me amusing and useful information. After Finishing Touches, which is neither amusing nor useful, I no longer want to know. The author's husband, Walter Kerr of the New York Times, once wrote, 'Some plays are so bad you don't even feel like a drink afterwards.' Finishing Touches is a play for abstainers.
