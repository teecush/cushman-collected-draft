Tolstoy at the National

THE most surprising feature of Tolstoy's comedy The Fruits of Enlightenment (Olivier) is its mildness.

It was written late in its author's life at a time when he was denouncing all sexuality while wrestling unsuccessfully with his own; and while he bore the scars of battles with the peasants who had stubbornly refused to do what he thought was good for them.

Art, presumably, purifies. There is not a trace of sex-hatred in The Fruits of Enlightenment. Indeed, there is hardly any sex at all, only the conventionalised courtship of a maid and kitchen boy. As for the peasants, they seem to have been conceived in accordance with the beautiful visions in their author's head rather than with the reality on which he kept stumbling.

The play presents a trio of them, come to Moscow to buy land on credit from the master of the house, who would rather have cash. The plot turns on their efforts to get the bargain made on their own terms; the dramatic interest hangs on their bewildered sceptical reaction to the fashionable life of which they are the temporary spectators, and to the absurdities of that life itself.

The master believes in spiritualism, and the marriageable maidservant, by passing her boyfriend off as a medium, rigs a seance in the peasants' interests. The translator, Michael Frayn, has by his own account transferred the seance from the play's three-quarter mark to its end, thus keeping the story going, giving us the real climax, and sparing us a final act consisting mainly of comings and goings. It sounds reasonable, but I suspect that he has miscalculated; the comings and goings are the most enjoyable part of the play, while the story is thin and gains nothing from being protracted.

Mr Frayn's introduction to the published text (reprinted, with tactful abridgements, in the National Theatre programme) is engagingly frank about the play's defects. He acknowledges that the peasants are sentimentalised ("two of them are comic characters, one of them is sturdily outspoken"--I'm still not sure which is which) and that Tolstoy's jibes at the credulity of the educated classes weigh a little heavy at times too. He might have added that the whole menage--mother is a fussy hypochondriac, the son is a wastrel, the daughter is a blue stocking (or at any rate is outspoken and wears spectacles)--is hardly the occasion of trenchant satire, despite the amount of stage time that it takes up.

The Master is described by Tolstoy as "in the English sense a 'gentleman'"; and it is of course in the English manner of golden eccentricity--benevolent, slightly touched, and disregarding people even in the act of radiating good will at them--that Ralph Richardson plays. John Harding and Selina Cadell make him a bright pair of offspring, but everything in this area of the play seems forced; however hard and fast the actors talk, the play moves slowly.

There is, though, an intermediate layer to the play: that of the servants, who regard their employers with varying degrees of envy, amazement and, just occasionally, hatred. (The mutterings of an old cashiered cook, who has crept back to sleep on the kitchen stove, make one wish that Tolstoy had written a more splenetic play.) They also have a tremendous nostalgia for the land, sparked off by the arrival of the peasants. The lovers in particular can hardly wait to get back there and the fatherly valet wants a place kept warm for him.

As a displaced pastoral, the play has resonance if not exactly urgency; and there are good performances below stairs from Brenda Blethyn, Peter Copley, Peter Needham and Greg Hicks, whose Cossack dance is the best thing in the show. It may seem glib to blame Christopher Morahan's production for a general lack of Russian-ness (and I have no idea how he could have overcome the deficiency), but the play does seem to lack a necessary context. One notes yet again that the Olivier stage is a powerful devourer of detail.

* * *

Hoagy, Bix and Wolfgang Beethoven Bunkhaus (King's Head) is Adrian Mitchell's latest spasm of slightly dramatised hero worship. Hoagy is Carmichael of course, Bix is Beiderbecke, and Bunkhaus, we are given to understand, is their Indiana college pal William Monkhaus, a surrealist poet who re-christens Hoagy "Hogwash McCorkle" on sight. He talks that way all the time. An exasperatingly fey creation, he seems to be driving his actor (Murray Melvin) to distraction. Bix is fey in another fashion; white-faced and appealing, in Sylvester McCoy's performance, and naturally doomed. The play seems to be searching for somebody or something to blame for his dissolution, but not even Paul Whiteman (played, along with half a dozen other parts, very well by Roland Oliver) can quite make it as an exploiting villain.

I spent the evening waiting with increasing impatience for the music, which, of course, is lovely: most of Mr Carmichael's best songs are delivered with modest insouciance by John Vine, who actually bears a helpful facial resemblance to the patient young Hoagy, and with measured punch by Nicola McAuliffe, whose "Washboard Blues" is magnificent. The play, to use a suitable Dixieland word, rambles.

* * *

So, in effect if not in structure, does John Byrne's Normal Service (Hampstead), which is set in the design department of a Scottish commercial television company in the fifties. The author has a firmer controlling idea than is immediately apparent. He manages entrances and exits less mechanically than he did in The Slab Boys and he gets a sheaf of solid performances, notably from Garfield Morgan and Richard Wilson. But Mr Frayn's Alphabetic Order has rather spoiled the market for typical day-in-the-office plays, and Mr Byrne's dialogue is not funny enough (not consistently, anyway) nor his characters' passing troubles arresting enough, to make me sit through another one. I am rather sad, remembering the vaudeville of his Writers' Cramp, to see him buckling down to naturalism so soon.

* * *

Frederick Harrison's Subjects for Interrogation (Bush) is a double bill of cautionary parables about the breeding of torturers. The first is pre-packaged, an argument between a young terrorist and a gently mannered sensory depriver who have nothing to say to each other and at great length, and you may as well miss it. Be on time, though, for the second, in which an average unemployed man (most convincingly played by David Hargreaves) is put through the ordeal of putting someone else through one. Three characters--judge, tormentor and victim--make up a scale susceptible to gripping and cunning variations. The play also works, a lot of the time, as a parlour game, which is more than you can say for most plays.
