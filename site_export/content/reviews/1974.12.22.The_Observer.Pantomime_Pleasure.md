Pantomime pleasure

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Cinderella' and 'Hans Andersen.'

HAVING been numbed by West End pantomimes over the years I found Cinderella (London Casino) very refreshing. Essentially it is not of the West End at all, being a blown-up transfer of the last Christmas show at the Oxford Playhouse, written and directed by that theatre's former chieftain, Frank Hauser.

Mr Hauser has supplied an unusually witty script. I hesitate to call traditional because I am no longer sure what constitutes pantomime tradition. Metropolitan or provincial, few of our shows bear much relation to the Victorian extravaganzas seasonally exhumed by the Players' Theatre; and even Palladium pantomime may be said to have established some traditions of its own.

There is, for example, the convention that every opening scene shall be set in a village square, inhabited by a rollicking, mildly bucolic chorus. Cinderella gets there in its second scene, but it begins, in revolutionary fashion, in Baron Hardup's kitchen, where our heroine, in some kind of trance, is listening to the voices of her dead parents filling her in on her situation. This is undeniably getting down to business, and betrays a concern for narrative that might serve the entire evening, if only the Cinderella story had enough plot to be stretched that far. To help it along Mr Hauser has furnished a sub-plot: conceivably pantomime's first.

I have always thought of Prince Charming, when I have thought of him at all, as an orphan, but he turns out, like other theatrical princes, to have a mother and an uncle. His mother is all right, but his uncle is rotten and even stoops (literally) to stealing the slipper. He is in league with the nastier of the Ugly Sisters. There goes another tradition: when I was young no such gradations in villainy were permitted and both the Uglies were as bad as each other. The mould was first broken, I think, by Hugh Lloyd and Terry Scott; and their example is followed here. Hugh Paddick is prim, attenuated, with the makings of a conscientious objector; Roy Kinnear--in the governing performance of the evening--is more robust, physical, vocally and immorally, his behaviour towards the end earning him some thoroughly justified hisses. "Let us," he remarks on an early exit, "decamp--for a change," and he seems to have taken his own injunction to heart; his demeanour--considering his present context and past record--is quite remarkably straightforward.

Messrs Kinnear and Paddick are finely foiled by Nicky Henson, the most masterful actor in the country under 30; as Buttons he is nimble, tuneful, warm and precise. The only thing he lacks is pathos, but this is a notably unsentimental panto all round. Twiggy is certifiably magic when she arrives at the ball, but for most of the time she is a pretty tough Cinders ("an insolent chit with appalling vowel sounds" is Mr Kinnear's prejudiced estimate), who takes the stage with a shrugging assurance that blossoms in song and, more especially, dance.

Joyce Grant is an enchantingly arch Fairy Godmother, thrown, but not very far, by her inability to remember the appropriate spells and their effects. Her funny fairy makes up for a disappointing pair of Brokers' Men. Harry H. Corbett and Wilfrid Brambell have presumably been hired to reenact their TV relationship, but the script takes little account of this; so they are miscast, and since they are actors and not personality clowns (though Mr Corbett's mugging affords him some independent comic identity) they necessarily flounder.

They do in fact reflect a split in the enterprise. Pleasant though it is to have the Casino reclaimed after two decades of Cinerama, its size distorts a basically intimate show; the spaces have to be arbitrarily filled in with hunks of show biz. I do not expect classical consistency in a pantomime; when the company breaks into a number called "Australia" (for no better reason than that the Queen, the Prince and Dandini are all Aussies), the occasion has the pleasingly zany irrelevance of the production numbers in early Marx Brothers movies.

This song, some adapted music hall tunes, and Vivian Ellis's jaunty "Spread a Little Happiness" belong to one world (reflected in some pretty two-dimensional sets); a stupefyingly unnecessary ensemble about a fox belongs to another. So does the show's main ballad, "For All We Know," though song-buffs might like to note that this is the original song of that name, written by the immortal J. Fred ("You Go To My Head") Coots, and still surviving a prolonged pummelling from Ray Conniff.

* * *

The same enthusiasts are directed to this year's Palladium show, Hans Andersen, where they will find Frank Loesser's fifties film score in excellent nick. The songs, originally a fleet of vehicles for Danny Kaye, are now beguilingly handled by Tommy Steele; he does especially well with "The Ugly Duckling," though the rangy "Anywhere I Wander" nearly defeats him (a heavenly choir has to be called to the rescue) and he has been robbed of what should have been his main prize: the title song, which has been fed to an anonymous, semi-audible crowd. Somebody called Marvin Laird has furnished additional numbers, and they are horrible.

The production is in a far more advanced state of schizophrenia than Cinderella; to judge by the staging it is another pantomime. (This time we do get the village square opening, first glimpsed as always through a gauze; later on a coach is transformed into a ship through purely theatrical magic, which I'll admit is more fun than white mice and pumpkins.) However, Beverley Cross, who wrote the book, obviously sees the thing as an intelligent, or at least non-cretinous, musical, though the necessity of linking pre-existent songs causes some strain. The close, where Hans, rejected by his beloved Jenny Lind (a creditable performance by Colette Gleeson), realises he can write happy endings even if he can't live them, is very touching.

* * *

Off the festive track the Open Space continues its assault on the cold and glittering comedies of Carl Sternheim with The Snob. The conception is less of a travesty than its predecessor, Schippel's; the execution is considerably worse. The production is stylish, or rather attitudinised, when it remembers; and nondescript when it forgets.
