Marching Song

Reviewed by Robert Cushman

[Editorial note: One partly obscured word is supplied in square brackets below.]

Nobody in the contemporary theatre writes quite like John Whiting; Arden has inherited his moral preoccupation, but has added a flamboyance alien to his precursor. "The Devils" at least offers fancy dress and ear-catching rhetoric; "Saint's Day" has the manic wildfire of anybody's first play; but "Marching Song", which the Dryden Society mounted at the A.D.C. in the first week of term, is the bleakest bone of Whiting.

In one of those chilly ultra-modern rooms in one of those nameless European states, a defeated war-hero faces the alternatives of suicide or disgrace by public trial. Years of imprisonment have left him emotionally stripped; and the memory of his past, as exemplified in his elegantly-preserved mistress and a decayed film-director, holds no attraction for him. But the harsh uncommitted vitality of a waif-like young girl awakens a response in him, a belief that there may still be a present to live for.

**Honourable Service**

Dido's reference to her archaeologist father—"Always grubbing in the past. Disgusting occupation"—offers the clearest pointer to the [thematic] centre, but it is not the play's definitive voice. Rupert Forster has to come to terms with his past before his suicide can be, by his own questing standards, honourable. The young officer who will re-fight Forster's battles without Forster's scruples does not suggest an unconditional brief for starry-eyed youth.

The play is intellectually and atmospherically water-tight. But its very completeness within its own terms creates dangers. It is the coolest of plays, and unless its peculiar temperature can be re-created in performance, it reaches the danger-point and freezes. And ice is for falling through.

**Parallel to the footlights**

Michael Woosnam-Mills' production had the initial advantage of a good skeletal set and was pleasingly unpretentious. But a play which lives through its dialogue as completely as this one needs far more careful pacing than it received here. Ideas and images came at the audience in an undifferentiated heap which obscured the play's inner shape. The tension was never spun tight enough to justify the continuous presentation. Too many duologues were handled by lining up the actors parallel to the footlights and letting them speak through the back of each others' necks.

Understandably the characters who fared best were the weak recognisable ones: Liz Goodwin beautifully caught the mistress' frayed nerves and suppressed jealousy, and Christopher Pinfield, though his boyish appearance militated against belief, eased his way securely into the director. David Ross, attacking Forster with presence and intelligence, never latched on to the steely intellectuality of the text; his long speeches came over as nicely-prepared audition-pieces, poised between invisible quotation marks. The fault, perhaps, is Whiting's; in paring the play down to the bone, he neglected an audience's craving for human flesh.
