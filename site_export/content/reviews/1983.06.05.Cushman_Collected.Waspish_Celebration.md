Waspish celebration

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Nancy.

IN the first scene of The Dining Room (Greenwich) a house is apparently changing hands. In the second scene, equally apparently, a house or its contents are being divided up by a separating couple. It is probably not the same house, though it is definitely the same set: scenically and socially.

Locationwise, we are in the room promised by the title. In class terms we are American upper, and rich. We have, invariably, servants. We are in a play by A. R. Gurney Jr, celebrated for celebrating the WASP and for doing it, on the whole, waspishly.

For what happens in the third scene, or the fourth, or any of those that follow I cannot precisely vouch. The final tally is well into double figures, and a fellow gets confused. There is no story line to cling to, and no continuing or even recurring characters. Half a dozen actors play a kaleidoscopic assemblage of parents, children and domestics.

The dining-room symbolises the house, the house the family, and the family a social attitude. The dining-room is cherished, especially by the more authoritarian figures, as a sanctum and a retreat. "Nowadays," says a grandfather, "people eat in living-rooms and kitchens," and he deprecates these people, though he recognises their inevitability. He appears late in the play and late also in the chronology--not necessarily the same thing.

An earlier patriarch is more inflexible. He disapproves of progressive education, of any American involvement in foreign affairs, of any grammatical sloppiness ("Dad, can I read the funnies?" "Certainly you may."), and of the New Deal. These dining rooms are cold beds of isolationism. One diner took shredded wheat with him on his only trip to Europe, and returned when he had eaten it all.

What happens? Younger generations shock their elders, exchanging spouses, uttering slang (the room is described as "viciously nice") and even profanity ("it sucks out loud . . . it bites the big one"), and planning slide-shows on the "vanishing culture" they inherit. Elders shock their youngers by being caught with lovers. Fathers with hernias face fist-fights to defend the honour of homosexual uncles. Staff are flattered or berated. Drinks are drunk and meals of course eaten, though not by those who have cooked them.

Mr Gurney is shuffling and recycling the conventions of drawing-room drama, and he brings to his task, as you will have noted, an exceedingly sharp ear and tongue. Some of his cliche situations, though, take their revenge, refusing to keep the elegant distance he has prescribed for them.

Alan Strachan has directed with precision, aided by fine lighting from Mick Hughes. His cast comprises four former stalwarts of Alan Ayckbourn's Scarborough company, one real American, and Polly James. They all slide up and down the age-scale very proficiently, though a lack of Yankee boldness sometimes results, paradoxically, in near-caricature. The teak and the archways of Bernard Culshaw's set are a grand environment.

* * *

"We think it is important for you to see both productions," say the RSC in their new leaflet, referring to The Roaring Girl and The Taming of the Shrew. They have gone to the trouble of juxtaposing these two plays; the least we can do is show up. The same schoolmasterly tone is implicit in their plan to have their production of King Lear (Barbican) cross-pollinate with Edward Bond's Lear in the Pit.

Actually Adrian Noble's production now seems less Bondish, more generous, than it did at Stratford. True, the crucial protesting servant at Gloucester's blinding who claims to have served Cornwall ever since he himself was a child sounds as if he still is a child, but I'm sure this is accident rather than debasing design. I had expected this production to suffer from my intervening exposure to the Olivier television version, but it is in fact as thrilling as before, and richer.

Michael Gambon's Lear has, so to speak, grown into his age; and he takes the first half of the play superbly. His capriciousness is massive, more so because of this actor's native reasonableness. He never sounds saner than when he curses Goneril or prays not to go mad; gradually his sanity becomes alarming; it cloaks gigantic self-deception. He goes mad logically; of course Poor Tom was ruined by his daughters. The mock-trial remains wonderfully serious, its crowning paradox, Lear's earnest "Is there any cause in Nature that makes these hard hearts," uttered as he stabs the Fool.

That irony aside, this, the production's most famous invention, is perplexing. People say it is symbolic, but of what? Why should Lear, who is learning to think, kill his conscience? It makes, of course, a spectacular end to a spectacular performance, though I doubt if Antony Sher's pyrotechnics actually reveal more than Michael Williams's quieter music-hall veteran in the last Stratford version. If the play dwindles in its second half, it is not through the Fool's absence, but through Lear's. There is no special vision at Dover, and no surprise at the reconciliation; it is just Mr Gambon being gentle, as usual. But he is moving at the end.

In Shakespeare, intelligence backed by experience equals authority: practice has made Malcolm Storry's Kent perfect, and Sara Kestelman's Goneril, gradually extinguishing conscience, very near.

The Scottish 7:84 production of Men Should Weep, which I admired at last year's Edinburgh Festival, is now at the Theatre Royal, Stratford East.

The peripatetic Theatre of the Nations should come to rest next year at Nancy: an architecturally gracious French town currently holding its own sixteenth drama festival, directed by Mira Trailovic, who presides over the similar jamboree in Belgrade. Fitting, then, that the most distinguished production I saw should have been Yugoslav: Requiem for Boris Davidovich, a blend of poor and epic theatre.

The audience sit in the middle of the floor; the action unfolds in tableaux around the four walls. The life, death (under Stalin) and, especially, times of a Russian revolutionary are depicted in every available style: narrative, choric, choreographic--and in, we were promised, 10 alternating languages. English seemed, to my defective ears, to come round less often than was fair, but it did have the last words; they were sung to the surprising tune of She's Leaving Home--Goodbye U.S.S.R.

Elsewhere jadedness could be scented. From Cologne came a solemn Misanthrope, impressive at any given moment by virtue of its slow pace and its set, a huge and perilous flight of stairs; I don't suppose German directors actually want to maim their actors.

There was a Chilean play at which respect--since these were actors playing peasants and probably playing them well--faded into uncomprehending boredom. It is no accident that festivals like Nancy flourished in the heyday of the American alternative groups, whose appeal was largely non-verbal.

Some performers seem to exist wholly for the festival circuit; at home they are prophets, without honour, or honoured without profits. I had never heard of the British representative, Johnny Melville, but his one-man show, Members Only, was packed out; perhaps because word had spread that it was funny. Mr Melville proved to be Scots and an accomplished mime: too winsome, like most mimes, but engaging, especially in the merciful failure of his benevolent fantasy to deal with its advertised subjects of protest unemployment and punkdom.

The festival utilised 14 venues: from municipal opera house, for establishment types, to bare rooms for those who yearn for the days when fringe was fringe and only walking counted, for seating or heating. A scarce-converted tobacco factory housed three of these penitentiaries; it seemed a tacit acknowledgment that some theatricalia should carry a health-warning.
