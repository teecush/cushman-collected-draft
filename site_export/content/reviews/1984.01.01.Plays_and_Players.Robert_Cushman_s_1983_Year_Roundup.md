Robert Cushman's 1983 Year Roundup

Robert Cushman OBSERVER BEST NEW PLAY: Glengarry Glen Ross (David Mamet) BEST NEW MUSICAL: Little Shop of BEST PERFORMANCE (ACTOR): Michael Gambon (Tales from Hollywood) BEST PERFORMANCE (ACTRESS): Geraldine McEwan (The Rivals) BEST DIRECTOR: Terry Hands (Cyrano de Bergerac) BEST DESIGNER: John Gunter (The MOST PROMISING NEWCOMER: Alexandra Mathie (Daisy Pulls It Off) MOST PROMISING PLAYWRIGHT: Michael Rudman 1983 will be remembered as the year in which we lost Ralph Richardson, but also as the year in which he gave one of his finest performances. He came, with Derek Jacobi, at the top of my runner-up list. Several of my perennial favourites suggested themselves for other awards: Mike Alfreds finding a way - indeed two ways - to do Marivaux; Victoria Wood in the onlv show of the vear to make me helpless with laughter (but what category is there for her?); Stephen Sondheim, whose Merrily We Roll Along certainly had the best score of the year but which, since we only saw it in a student production, cannot qualify; Judi Dench, again.

I have voted for her on three occasions in the past, and she has won on two of them. I rather hope she does it again. I count her actress the most important thing to hap. pen in my ten years as a theatre critic. If I were forced into voting for British play I would probably choose Howard Barker's Victory (as much, I imagine, to his surprise as to mine) and follow it with Falkland Sound if it was a play, and Tales from Hollywood whose wit and good nature had me on its side from the start, though they proved insufficient to carry the evening. I have reluctantly to place it at the head of the Great Overrated, a bulging category that this year embraced plays, musicals, productions and performances.

I have never known so many marks to be given for good intentions: for the drama with the socially significant theme or the actor with the throbbingly sincere voice. A patchy year for the National Theatre, though, as my nominations indicate, when they delivered they delivered. Some of their less trumpeted shows remind me that this was also the year of the Great Underrated: Inner Voices (even without Richardson) and The Fawn were absorbing, unobtrusively ings; even The Trojan War might have worked. The Rivals set wins because it knocked me out from the moment I entered the auditorium, but there were plenty of contenders; Cyrano by the omnipresent Ralph Koltai, Hayden Griffin's Chinese restaurant in Glengarry, the light and shade of Paul Dart's French double for Shared Experience.

It was also a bumper year for new players: at the National, Fiona Shaw and Miranda Foster, and at Stratford the whey-faced Paul McGann. I wrote in P&P that I wanted the theatre to make me laugh, cry, tap my feet or hold my breath. Using those criteria let me put in words for Noises Off (new cast), The Hard Shoulder, Blondel, Volpone, Pack of Lies, Mr Cinders, Snoopy, Pericles, Susan Fleetwood, Sinead Cusack and Juliet Stevenson.
