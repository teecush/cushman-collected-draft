Room for all of Russia

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a Chekhov revival and new plays from Hull Truck and Barrie Keeffe.

THE whole of Russia is our orchard; and the set for The Cherry Orchard at Riverside Studios looks capable of accommodating the whole of Russia. Since the main auditorium of Hammersmith's new arts centre is a flexible space, the director, Peter Gill, must have consciously decided to give the Ranevsky family this infinity of lebensraum.

The width of the stage seems daunting when you enter the auditorium. Looking ahead, I thought I could see it working for the play's alfresco second act, but not for the interiors of the other three. I was wrong; the second act works the least well, perhaps because Mr Gill shows, understandably, fewer inhibitions about stringing it out across the entire area of William Dudley's spare, timbered, strictly non-representational scene. Indoors he concentrates his actors, usually centre-stage, and lets the surrounding vastness contract or expand more or less at will. (Ours rather than his.)

If you choose to look at the empty spaces, you may become aware, as never before, of the geographical isolation of the play. At the end Varya is off to keep house for people who are spoken of as if they were neighbours, and who live 70 miles away. The orchard itself exists not, as usual, in a spray of pink blossom glimpsed through windows at the back, but somewhere invisible behind our heads. As the characters line up at the edge of the stage to confront and describe it, it takes on more reality than I have ever known.

Mr Gill excels in deploying formal tactics in the service of untrammelled life. I have already said that he concentrates; and concentration, in another sense, is the strength and hallmark of this production. Shifts in relationships are denoted by slight but precise alterations in generally sedentary groupings. Thus Trofimov, at the beginning of the ball scene, moves along a row of empty chairs to sit next to Pishchik when he finds that this man whom he has despised has heard of Nietzsche.

Emotions among the minor characters (whatever people say, there are such animals in Chekhov) are played to the full, but never enlarged or prolonged. So the maid Dunyasha (Elizabeth Estensen) in the opening moments of the play goes into full-throated but momentary ecstasy at the false alarm of the family's return. Leigh Lawson as the flashy valet Yasha, usually the unregarded villain of the piece, sounds perfectly reasonable when he pleads to be allowed to return to Paris and escape provincial stagnation.

Eleanor Bron gives Charlotta a nervous protective hauteur (and is the best ventriloquist, though not the best conjurer, I have known in the part). Wensley Pithey is hugely good as Pishchik, George Howe's Firs is hardly a believable serf but his last moments are very affecting. Here the production's feeling for stress, pace and control is most evident. And the plot, of which there is a great deal in The Cherry Orchard, is clearly and rhythmically presented. We appreciate the full significance of the telegrams Ranevskaya receives from Paris, one each act, leading up to her departure in the last.

It is always possible to see what is driving Judy Parfitt's Ranevskaya; not so easy, though, to be moved by the results. This is a sharp, elegant and uncompromising portrayal of a character who compromises all the time, and the actress's steeliness has infected the character. Ranevskaya remains, stubbornly, a role for a star; since that is how she is regarded by everybody else in the play.

The playing in the upper echelons of this production (apart from Philip Locke's brilliant Gaev, whose billiards mannerism is plainly all that stops him from going to pieces) is just not volatile enough; Michael Elphick's Lopahin is well blocked in to begin with, but falls down (not literally, though that might work) in his drunk scene. All the same, welcome.

* * *

Hull Truck has its most complex show yet in A Bed of Roses (Bush). A vicar begins by announcing a Keep Our Churches Open campaign. ("KOCO," somebody christens it later.) He means psychologically as well as physically open; he and his wife are waif-and-stray collectors, and one of their acquisitions is a recidivist with a plate in his head and leanings towards the occult, living with a fierce girl who claims not to love him: an assertion which baffles the vicar's wife, though her own marriage is falling apart.

In another part of the forest we find a third doomed pair: a compulsive joker who can hardly speak without donning a funny voice, and a humourless Ulster girl out to lead him to the altar. Actionwise, they fall into line when they have a hilarious, prenuptial chat with the vicar, who lists as prime marriage-wreckers sex, money and "infuriating habits": with which last every character in the show is loaded. In the last scene he reads the banns, along with other bad news, straining and agonising farther along the booby-trapped road to Christian charity.

This struck me as the best deal religion has had on our stage in a long time; Robin Soans's performance, funny and anguished, is superb, as, in a simpler fashion, is Alan Williams's ferocious crook. The standards set by Mike Bradwell in his direction and improvised dramaturgy are now very high, and one may fault a couple of actresses for falling below them: one too theatrical to be real, another too real to be theatrical. But everybody joins with sparkling expertise in the songs, which are almost integrated enough to make a musical. Anyone on for studying the Rise of the Strolling Player as Musician?

* * *

Hull Truck is floating. Barrie Keeffe isn't drowning either, though in Frozen Assets (Warehouse) he splashes a bit wildly. He has returned to his old protagonist, the embittered young underdog, but he still has the more recent farcical style of A Mad World, My Masters clinging to him. The two do not blend. His hero, Buddy, is a borstal boy on the run, cheerful except that he has accidentally impaled a warder and has a raging toothache. The latter complaint is finally cured by an affluent dentist, described as the first kind person Buddy has met.

His memory is playing tricks; he has also been well treated by a priest (again), an MP and a Socialist peer who admittedly wants his body, but then who's perfect? Certainly not Buddy's brother-in-law, a house-breaker who shows him no sympathy whatever. The ambitions of the poor are shown to mimic those of the rich: a villa in the Canaries or an expense-account trip to the West Indies. Both sides too can deep-freeze their acquisitions: for further explanation of the title see the play. It will be worth your while, for though what Mr Keeffe conveys is different from what he means, his vigour, however diffused, remains unquenchable.

Barry Kyle's production does nothing much to tidy the play up, but Allen Hendrick as Buddy holds it together, and though David Waller comes on a bit strong for a screw in scene one, he doubles up finely at a later hour as the peer, who is as drunk as a lord.

* * *

I hardly warmed to the idea of a two-hander about the last days of Sarah Bernhardt, dictating her reminiscences to a male secretary who assists her in acting out scenes from her past. But John Murrell's Memoir (Ambassadors) is a very pleasant surprise, wittily and fastidiously written. Siobhan McKenna's Sarah is, as Beerbohm said of the lady's Hamlet, tres grande dame; Niall Buggy as her primly reluctant co-star is perfection.
