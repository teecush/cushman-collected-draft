The Musical Gets Respectable

Musicals may decay but music proliferates. It is becoming harder than ever to decide exactly what a musical is; or, if you prefer, what is a musical. Songs are increasingly squeezed into plays; the audience welcomes them, irrespective of quality, since they break up all that endless talking. Incidental music in the classier Shakespeare productions is now omnipresent music; directors grab at any attempt to do a number. Since nostalgia is in — or, as I prefer to put it, since the classic popular songs are recognised to be just that — there is a rush on those plays of the 20s and 30s into which they can plausibly be slotted. The actual presented by the National Theatre (Guys and Dolls, The Beggar's Opera, Jean Seberg) and the Royal Shakespeare Company (Poppy)fall outside our time-scheme, but their presence has been pervasively felt.

They have given the form a new respectability. Forget about the clown wanting to play Hamlet; our Hamlets are lining up for tap-lessons. We are constantly being told that the main theatrical growth-area is cabaret, and especially alternative cabaret, and most alternative feminist cabaret. But little of this has worked its way through to the world outside its own circuits. The most notable example has been Cut and Thrust, presented at the Drill Hall with some technical aplomb and a lot of glitter. Devised and directed by the Australian Robyn Archer, in the wake of her one-woman success A Star is Torn, it turned out to be a radical rag-bag, tossing satirical or just plain abusive grenades at all the accepted targets.

As a celebration of left-wing togetherness it had some point, but it quickly became monotonous because nothing was being challenged and few jokes were being made. Miss Archer herself, who did not appear, contributed some good original material and there were raids on other sources from Brecht and Adrian Mitchell to Noël Coward, who might have been surprised to find himself in such company but whose 1951 masterpiece 'There Are Bad Times Just Around The Corner' remains perenially topical, and whose conservative sentiments can easily be turned themselves. Two neglected Broadway musicals, How Now Dow Jones (Elmer Bernstein and Carolyn Leigh) and Jamaica (Harold Arlen and EY Harburg) were plundered for numbers on, respectively, the stock market and the bomb; *Leave The Atom Alone', sung by Australian dynamo, Noni Haslehurst, was the highlight of the show.

The same inbreeding afflicted a show at the other end of the cabaret spectrum. At the other extreme from this gentility was Yakety Yak, the second attempt at fashioning a show out of the 1950's hit of Jerry Leiber and Mike Stoller, maybe the only team to take the craftsmanlike? values of American popular song into the raunchier precincts of rock-and-roll. Leiber's barbed lyrics and Stoller's holy-rolling music should be theatrical naturals; though their own attempts at writing shows have so far been abortive. Robert Walker's choice of songs in Yakety Yak was less enterprising than Ned Sherrin's a couple of years back in Only in America, but Mr Walker revealed a far shrewder grasp of what the songs are about; urban blues for teenagers. There was plenty of guts in the playing of the Darts and the singing of the McGann brothers (of whom one, Paul, has blossomed as an actor).

There was an appallingly scrappy script, but at the Half Moon, where the show originated, it hardly seemed to matter. I can imagine that when the production transferred to the Astoria, Charing Cross Road, its faults were far more noticeable. It must still have been able to give any number of points to a subsequent Astoria attraction, Jukebox. This merely strung together all manner of hits from the fifites on, and put projections behind them. The cast were energetic, Cliff Richard impersonation stopped the show, and if it all seemed silly, then so did the music it was celebrating. Only when it became apparent, with nods to yesterday's protest and today's feminism, was it unbearable.

There is a kind of almost-musical that one thinks of as a play-with-music. Maybe this is because they are written -book and lyrics - by real playwrights; and the only real playwright who has shown any signs of being a real lyricist is Peter Nichols (in Privates on Parade and Poppy). Alan Ayckbourn has tried often, with mixed results; the best were in Suburban Strains. The worst - that I have heard, at any rate were in Me, Myself and I, staged at the Orange Tree in Richmond, equipped by Paul Todd with short-breathed tunes that emphasised the plonking rhymes. This could be a way of writing comedy songs but Mr Ayckbourn was out to write character songs, about a woman treated unfeelingly by men.

Three actresses played our heroine simultaneously, hence the title; but there was hardly enough of her for one consciousness, let alone a trio. Mr Ayckbourn can rhyme; what mars his lyrics is a lack of fluency. They sit awkwardly on the music; the words have no zing to them. Willy Russell, who wrote book, music and lyrics for Blood Brothers, hardly bothers at all with rhyme. He doesn't take the logical - and perfectly permissible step - of dispensing with it altogether; he merely takes boss-shots at it. It didn't seem to bother many critics, or maybe many members ofthe audience; pop lyrics have obviously desensitised them. But it is worth protesting about; we are being denied an acute source of pleasure.

Singin' in the Rain, of course, I know by heart; and there was something hopelessly self-defeating (except, it seems, commercially) in Tommy Steele's attempt to put so well-loved a picture into another medium. He complained that the critics had misunderstood him, that what he was trying to do was not to stage Singin' in the Rain, but to create a new work out of the same material. In fact what appeared at the Palladium looked as if Steele had attempted a facsimile, only deviating from it when something proved technically impossible or when the rights to some of the material was not available. (The film score was nearly all by the same composers, Arthur Freed and Nacio Herb Brown, but it was handled by a variety of publishers).

So sometimes one applauded the cleverness of the imitation, but it was hardly possible-to get excited over it. With all the money and enthusiasm in the world the show could never re-produce the speed and lightness of the film; those who had never seen the original actually found the Betty Comden-Adolph Green script (one of Hollywood's greatest and most unified comedies) toothless and overblown. The thinking behind the production was objectionable than the production itself.

Apparently producers believe that they can lure audiences into a theatre by taking some property the public already knows, and ruining it. Marilyn almost counts musical. The work of an American team it premiered in London in expectation (presumably dashed) of a Broadway transfer. Larry Fuller, Hal Prince's regular choreographer directed in his master's (especially a narrator) from Evita but doing a tidy enough job. The score was no worse than that of most current musicals. But it was another self-destroying mechanism that did for the show. *Did you know Marilyn Monroe' they all chanted at curtain-rise, and proceeded to tell us that only the movie-camera really did. That seems to rule her out as a stage subject, and indeed they finally upstaged their own production with a brilliantlyedited montage of authentic Monroe film-clips.

This was very unfair to Stephanie Lawrence, who played Marilyn and revealed herself as that rarity, a British musical performer with style. She doesn't brandish a big number at you; she sings it. The nearest thing to a large, successful original musical was Windy City, and it was still a good way off. It was craftsmanlike, and it was authentic. Based on the classic American newspaper comedy The Front Page it maintained much of the flavour of its original. What it could not maintain was its pace; the songs stopped the story without stopping the show. A musical adaptation of a good play has to alter the original decisively (and it can be done, as in My Fair Lady, with only a slight tilt) to justify itself. Dick Vosburgh's lyrics were though not up to his Macaulay's music competent, sometimes snazzy. Dennis Waterman's Hildy Johnson was fatally lacking in dynamism, but Anton Rogers had it as Machiavellian Walter Burns, and as the murderer Earl Williams, Longden did sometimes manage to move the show into a world of its own. That leaves us with three small treasures. One was a revival of a 1929 British hit Mr Cinders; British in setting and origin, though actually the work of five composers and lyricisits, from both sides of the Atlantic.

The joints didn't show; there was a carefree international style in musicals at this period. The major partner- still around to write a new title song - was Vivian Ellis. I have to confess an interest; when Dan Crawford, who runs the King's Head, first told me of his intention to revive this show, I said he was mad. I was founded. The show, a variant on the Cinderella story in which the exploited poor relation is a man, proved to be not only tuneful but funny; and intentionally so. It gained much from the casting of Denis Lawson in the central role; he gave the show a sardonic edge, and — when he sang the magical cheer-up song 'Spread a Little Happiness' — understated emotion.

He consummately; incorporating parody of the 'Dying Swan' at one point, and at another singing a polysyallabic nonsense song 'On the Amazon' with lunatic authority. The show transfered from the King's Head to the Fortune, and lost some magic en route; but it ran through the year. The other gems came from off-Broadway. One was Snoopy, the second musical to be based on the Peanuts strip, and taking the logical step of putting the dog in the centre of it. A situation outlined in the drawings took on effortless threedimensional life. Snoopy is ineffably superior to the mortals who surround him, but he is also dependent on them. This, together with the assorted one-upmanship the young humans with the beaming Charlie Brown eternally at the bottom of the heap, made an evening.

There was no story but the unity of tone and characters made it a musical rather than a revue. It was spunkily performed by a British cast, headed by Teddy Kempner as Snoopy; and was buoyed up by a score of America's best current craftsmen, Hal Hackady and Larry Grossman. Finally there was Little Shop of Horrors which proved my point about rock as celebration. Emotionally, rock songs are overblown; they are always louder and usually longer than the situation warrants. Little Shop of Horrors reached back to the seedy 1950s, age of the Chiffons, the Ronettes, and the Shirelles.

A girl trio, called Chiffon, Ronette, and Shirelle, told the story. A sadist who had found his vocation as a dentist, came on like Presley. Having disarmed us, the show plumed into another shock, the junk horror movie, and took our breath away by playing it for real. A strange plant, reared in a Skid Row flower-shop, grew and began eating everything in range, including the hero and ominously over the stalls, it threatened to devour the world, beginning with Cleveland. It now had a raucous voice of its own, and it tempted the schlemiel who had tended itlike a Mephistopheles combining soul-snaring with soul-singing. Barry James was a perfect victim, and Ellen Greene came from New York to recreate her original role of Audrey, masochistic girl-friend of the dentist and self-sacrificing love of our hero; a dazzlingly stylised dumb blonde, she even walked with a lisp.

Howard Ashman directed niftily, and also authored the book and lyrics, to gleaming parody tunes by Alan Menken. Their work had rare insouciance; and they trusted nobody. When the florist, rather than lose the services of his green-fingered assistant, offered to adopt him and make him a partner, they came up with a tango harnessing exploited vulnerability ('come kiss me quick, I'll be your son') to calculating disgust ('don't make me sick, just be my son'). One had almost forgotten such snappiness was still possible, and it was a tonic.
