Errors of comedy

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

WHEN he was very young, Kenneth Tynan laid down that no really first-rate director could be at home with broad comedy. He was wrong, of course, but I find his opinion of some comfort when considering Adrian Noble's Stratford production of The Comedy of Errors. Mr Noble is a director of proven brilliance, but his first major attempt at drama designed to provoke laughter is catastrophic.

There are some touches that are truly Noble. One is the lost, questing arrival in Ephesus of the visiting Antipholus and Dromio. Coming through a door in the white wall of Ultz's set, they appear dazed, almost blind. This of course is not a funny moment, nor meant to be. When the farce begins -- when master and man are mistaken for their respective twins -- Mr Noble works diligently at it. He must have done no end of research into traditional comic routines. Some of it showed in his production of King Lear with Antony Sher's clown-compendium performance as the Fool. The one thing that performance was not was amusing; nor did it need to be. But The Comedy of Errors does need, and this production is all technique and no humour. It keeps stepping on its own laughs. Blows and pratfalls, of which there are many, are accompanied by tympani-rolls in the orchestra. The pulling of a Dromio's nose provokes ruder noises in the pit. Pedantry, the RSC's recurring vice, here takes a new form. This, the production seems to be saying, is clowning; aren't you impressed?

Design and music run the show. Nigel Hess draws for his tunes, pleasingly enough, on Scott Joplin, Vincent Youmans and the Keystone Kops. In the scene of Pinch the conjuror he takes over (or the play throws in the sponge) and we get a number. Pinch is painted yellow, and the text does indeed say that he has a saffron face. But it makes no mention of the Dromios having red noses, or of the Antipholi being blue. They look like Atlantines (who, old Eagle readers will remember, were the good Venusians oppressed by the green Treens). Farce works by putting recognisable people in extreme situations. I don't recognise anyone with a blue face. I even jib at a heroine (Jane Booker) whose hair has been tied up like an ice-cream cone, however stylish.

The performances display some skill and, in the case of Paul Greenwood, who plays a scene hanging upside-down from a window, intrepidity. Peter McEnery is brisk, Henry Goodman earthy, Richard O'Callaghan nicely woeful and Zoe Wanamaker, trimly volcanic, nearly creates a character. Joseph O'Conor and Timothy Kightley are solid elders. There are some graceful notes: the final reconciliation, and an earlier rapprochement between master and servant when the former has beaten the latter and, penitent, gives him a cuddle.

There is also some atrocious verse-speaking, especially from the Duke. While they wait to mature into comedy directors (and it is not just Mr Noble's problem -- John Caird with The Twin Rivals is about the only young director in or out of the RSC to have displayed any sustained lightness of touch), Stratford chiefs could practise counting the number of beats in iambic pentameter. They used to know how.

* * *

Shakespeare festivals seem doomed to be erratic. The Canadian one at Stratford, Ontario, is still, after a period of upheaval, trying to find itself. It remains the best Shakespeare stage in the world: a beautifully proportioned timbered platform edged and surmounted by steps but with plenty of flat space around a simple central facade. It is timeless; but it lends itself with dangerous ease to archaic production. Men-at-arms may line its perimeter in stolid ranks, or a play be reduced to a series of entrances down right and exits up left.

Two of the three plays I saw this year succumbed. Macbeth had some good notions about witches but sank around a featureless performance by Nicholas Pennell. Another Briton, Richard Cottrell, directed a more efficient but equally uninspired Richard II, distinguished only by the performance of one of our great lost actors, Brian Bedford. Most Richards play for irony, but Mr Bedford's was the completest tease I have seen. Having sent the whole of England up rotten, he turned on himself; "for God's sake let us sit upon the ground" was a self-conscious bright idea. How much was Richard having fun, and how much the actor, was difficult to say but the role yielded a surprising amount to the approach. A flash of royal lightning, though, was needed to round it out.

This Stratford is not only, or even primarily, a haven for expatriates. It was left to the theatre's director, John Hirsch, European-born but Canadian-nurtured, to redeem matters with an admirable As You Like It. This not only filled the stage but animated it. There was a strong contrast between court and forest: the first a finely detailed sadistic tyranny, the second near to paradise. Probably too near: everyone looked well insulated, and some of the play's sting was drawn.

But there was delight in the playing; led by Roberta Maxwell (an unusually intent Rosalind, considerably less than common tall), actors who had been dullards in the other plays shone with spirit and intelligence, acting on and through the lines in a manner the current RSC rarely achieves. They revealed that a Canadian school of Shakespeare might, after all, exist; and that it would not be all that different from a good English school.

Back to Warwickshire, and how. The Dillen (old Midland, apparently, for runt) takes the National Theatre promenade style a step further: indeed, a whole route-march. It starts in the Other Place and finishes there; but for three hours in between it takes itself and us on a tour of the district. The company is swollen by a hundred local amateurs who pick peas in a field, run races, punt or fish on the Avon (and, in one case, get thrown into it) and, as night falls, form exhilarating torchlight processions.

The play itself, adapted by Ron Hutchinson from a book by Angela Hewins about the long hard life of her grandfather, runs thin. Barry Kyle's production, aiming to forge a link between theatre and town, does so more through his method than his subject. Actually, though most of the story is set in and around Stratford, the local fields are most effectively used to represent the trenches of the First World War. The Somme can never have been this green, but to see men disappearing in the long grass, never to return, is an extraordinary experience. Mr Kyle reveals something already known to the Greeks and to the druidical fringe group Welfare State: we have completely misjudged the nature of drama in the open air. We think of it as pastoral. Actually it is -- or should be -- epic.

* * *

Cowardice (Ambassadors), by Sean Mathias, is about a brother and sister -- he a would-be playwright, she an actress -- obsessed with Noel Coward and Gertrude Lawrence. Why with them rather than with any other stars is never explored; nor is much else. A couple of songs accurately mimic the odd shapelessness of Coward's own: the words pulling the music, the rhymes pulling the words. Ian McKellen is monotonously little-boy-lost; Janet Suzman, allowed to range more freely, does so with vigour.
