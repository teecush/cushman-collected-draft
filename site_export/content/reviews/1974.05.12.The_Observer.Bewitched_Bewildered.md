Bewitched, Bewildered

SOMEWHERE towards the end of Peter Barnes's The Bewitched (Aldwych), Alan Howard, who for most of the evening has been impersonating a gibbering idiot, has a few lucid moments in which to lay a message on us.

The news he bears is that there is no news; events have no meaning and effects no causes. 'We stand alone at the mercy of chance.' In one respect I found this comforting; it excused my incomprehension of the dynastic and political intrigues that make up the bulk of the play. Bulky it is, going on for three and a half hours, after which it is hard to be informed that they might as well have been three and a half other hours.

We have come through so much; is all our pain to be of no account? We have watched Mr Howard expertly limping, hunching, screeching and snorting his way through the role of Carlos II of Spain, an object lesson in what can be achieved through generations of determined inbreeding. (In this case, at least, there seems to have been a quite recognisable link between cause and effect.) We have watched his wife, his mother and his councillors squabbling among themselves while trying every means to save Carlos from his biologically predestined role as the last of the Hapsburgs. So that he may beget an heir, heretics have been burned and witches hunted; we have spent much time in the torture-chamber, with an interlude, by way of sub-plot, in the dentist's chair. It is, please bear in mind, a seventeenth-century dentist's chair.

None of this suffering engages our sympathy nor, I imagine, is it intended to. Mr Barnes invites our shudders, our disgust, and our giggles. When, on the eve of an auto da fe, the officers of the Inquisition give out with a couple of choruses of 'That's Entertainment' they appear to have their author with them. Certainly this showman's view of death at the stake has been adopted by the director, Terry Hands, and his designer, Farrah, who have staged the scene in their most palatial style. As the victims (a joky bunch, considering) expire, the king and queen, done up as vast effigies of themselves, are united by a huge golden phallus. This is spectacular, but confusing. Are we meant to assume that such a symbolic protuberance really formed part of the ritual at therapeutic autos? Is it the author's and director's satiric comment on the superstition involved? Or is it a kind of theatrical shorthand to assure us that the magic actually worked and that on this occasion the royal couple, however fruitlessly, made it?

Mr Hands and his company -- with the glowing exception of Joe Melia -- are less at home with the occasional musical numbers. But I suspect the function of these anyway. Mr Barnes is a former programmer for the National Film Theatre, and he is obviously in thrall to the great MGM musicals. So am I, but though I enjoy hearing the old tunes I cannot feel that their introduction at climactic moments does much for the cause of popular theatre. (For one thing the joke works only if you recognise the songs and these days not too many people do.) Their use is really a form of pedantry. Mr Barnes can be a dreadfully earnest funster, breathing -- for all his command of scenic effect -- the thin air of closet-drama in which story and characters wither.

Of course, in so long an evening there are successful passages, more than in Mr Barnes's previous play, The Ruling Class, which had a modern setting. Fancy-dress seems to suit him better. There is a funny, surprising duel between a dwarf jester and a full-sized grandee, and there is the spectacle of a huge box gradually closing on a dying queen mother. A previous line of this lady's -- 'I hurt, therefore I am' -- taken with the torture scenes and the agonised deformity of Carlos himself, suggest -- though the theme is unfocused -- that pain, loneliness and death are the only realities. Mr Barnes has provided witty lines for Philip Locke, playing a sinister archbishop rather like (to be honest, exactly like) Mr Locke's Professor Moriarty; and has invented a period jargon (people saying 'casn't' as a compromise between 'canst not' and 'can't'), which is at any rate original. He aims for a Renaissance variety of tone by conscientiously laying contrasting styles end to end.

* * *

As far as I know Jason Miller is not Arthur Miller's son, but his play That Championship Season (Garrick) assaults one of the old man's favourite targets: the belief that success comes to the guy who plays ball -- foot, base, or in this case, basket. The team that once brought their town a cherished trophy have grown up to wealth and power (one of them is mayor) guided in work as they were in play by their revered and rednecked coach. However: they are all insecure, all unhealthy (one is an alcoholic, a rewarding disease for author and actor, since it is a theatrical rule that unbridled drinkers shall also be unbridled cynics) and it finally transpires that they couldn't even play clean basketball. This last is one twist too many; it suggests that if they had been decent sportsmen they would be perfectly fitted to run the town. Once again audiences on Broadway (where the play has been a huge success) have been taken in by the pretence of seriousness, but they have benefited from a shared understanding with their author; America (unlike England) is still a valid and palatable dramatic subject. Otherwise the play is fair to middlebrow, offering some thrills, more spills, wisecracks and effective acting. Broderick Crawford makes a fearsome coach, all jowls and scowls.

* * *

I remember Mr Crawford as the hero of television's Highway Patrol, alternately tracking down crooks and preaching homilies on road safety. Two other experienced TV cops, Stratford Johns and Lee Montague, duel to the death in the new thriller Who Saw Him Die? (Haymarket). I am not allowed to reveal the outcome, or even the substance, of their combat, but I can disclose that Mr Montague wins on acting.
