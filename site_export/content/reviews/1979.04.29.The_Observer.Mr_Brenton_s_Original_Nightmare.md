Mr Brenton's original nightmare

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'The Churchill Play.'

HOWARD BRENTON'S The Churchill Play (Warehouse) has finally come to London four years after its premiere in Nottingham; and seeing it again proves an instructive experience.

We have been heavily dosed with nightmare plays about Britain's totalitarian future, envisioned as transpiring any time from next decade to next week; this is the daddy of them all -- the first, the most ambitious and considerably the best written. It is set, you may recall, in 1984. There is a coalition government, predominantly Tory, and disaffected elements are placed in internment camps.

The play centres on a group of prisoners in one such place, mostly in, it seems, for minor offences such as being around picket lines when the police arrived. Few are consciously political; almost all are working class. (What has happened to the revolutionary playwrights, middle class almost to a man? Have they all been summarily shot?)

Under the sponsorship of the camp medical officer, who is a liberal (i.e. despised by everybody), the lags put on a play about Winston Churchill, and use the performance as the cover for an escape attempt; they must all have watched 'Colditz' in younger, happier days.

As it happens the puncturing of wartime legends is one of the things Mr Brenton is on about. The Churchill of his play-within-the-play rises from his catafalque and revisits former scenes, recalling his miserable boyhood, his lifelong fits of agonising depression and the heady times of his popularity, when the blitzed populace cheerily told him, "We can take it, guv, give it back to them."

Only his guide on this spectral trip informs him he got the second part wrong: the workers really said, "And one day we'll give it back to you." This is presumably what the prisoners are doing, or trying to do, when they stage their final break-out. Asked to justify himself, the mock Churchill grandiloquently says, "I won the war." He is informed that he didn't: "people won the war." Quite true: and on television last week Mr Brenton admitted that Churchill was one of the people in question. But there is no hint of it in the play.

That doesn't matter too much; the main snag remains that of linking the play's two myths. What is Churchill doing in a play about 1984? The most obvious answer is that the sentimental pull he exerts is central to modern Conservatism. I doubt it: I haven't heard many references to the Battle of Britain spirit in the election campaign; and if Mrs Thatcher gets in, her victory will not have much to do with the late Sir Winston. Besides, half the time Mr Brenton is asserting that the people never really fell for him anyhow; he can't bear to conceive of the Tory spell actually working. Or, rather, he can contemplate the result but not the process. (The truth is that the last generation did worship Churchill; this one doesn't.) So Churchill ends up in the play as just a symbol of upper-classness in a straight class-struggle; though even this is complicated by his scenes of intensive introspection accompanied by the "black dog" of melancholy.

That makes the play sound like a mess; and so, in some ways, it is. It is redeemed, though, by its narrative thrust, and by the power of its dialogue, second only to Edward Bond's; also by the fact that it has a direct line to current fears -- witness the number of plays that have cannibalised it since its first prophetic appearance.

Barry Kyle's production brings out most of its strengths, and though Raymond Westwell doesn't go much beyond impersonation as Churchill, he is excellent in his outer role as the prisoner who actually writes the play -- as are his colleagues: David Bradley bewildered, Bill Dean pawky and, especially, Hilton McRae ferocious. Their opponents are less well done; Mr Brenton's periodic attempts to get inside the heads of the officers running the camp are no more than dutiful and the roles are pallidly played. A brace of visiting politicians are straightforwardly -- and more efficiently -- caricatured.

Mr Brenton is more forthcoming than most of his successors have been in documenting his nightmare, but like them he can stop short at awkward junctures. Despairing of the escape attempt, one of his internees declares that there is nowhere to go: "They'll concrete over the whole world." A good image -- and one that almost sums up the succession of plays we have seen at the Warehouse -- but dramatically an odd moment to start getting rhetorical. They should have more practical things on their minds. One basic point of Mr Brenton's scenario is that Britain will become an extension of Ulster, and subject to similar military rule.

* * *

There has been a whole school of plays on this hypothesis and David Leland's Psy-Warriors (Theatre Upstairs) is the latest. It starts as a confrontation between a trio of suspected London terrorists and their military interrogators; we get an anthology of physical and psychological humiliation.

We have been here before and it seems that Mr Leland has nothing new to tell us; he hardly seems interested in probing the motives of either side. Then -- by a ploy which it would be dishonourable of me to reveal, but which resembles one already used in a play at the Bush -- he changes track, and the play becomes a debate on the ethics of counter terrorism. Or at least some of it does; there is still a story to be told and it gets in the way.

The arguments meanwhile are largely disembodied; and the actors, under the author's direction, tie themselves in knots trying to characterise them. They should probably settle for simple lucidity. It is not an enlivening evening and the scheme is over-complicated, but it does yield a few dogged insights.

* * *

But if you are really searching for metaphors-for-Britain, try Jonathan Gems's The Tax Exile (Bush), which sees the place as a high-rise flat, lavishly furnished but liable to systematic plundering. It is owned by an inventor who is having taxation trouble and should really flee the country, but somehow doesn't. He is estranged from his wife but becomes involved with her radical-feminist best friend, who finds that principles are not everything.

These characters are confused but likeable, and given performances to match by Richard Kane and Jennie Stoller. The young are represented by the inventor's hippie son (who, having been in prison, doesn't realise time has passed him by), his pregnant girlfriend and his blue-rinsed punk sister -- who may, amazingly enough, be the first of her kind to reach a London stage.

There is also a manic tax inspector acting, more or less, as diabolus ex machina. These are put through a farcical evening which sometimes runs out of steam but rarely of point, and has a rare ring of truth about it, dividing the characters into those who can't bear to leave home and those who can't wait to. Nancy Meckler's direction is lively; June Page's punkette is sensational.

* * *

We are more used to New York comedies of contemporary manners. James Kirkwood's PS Your Cat is Dead (Open Space) is actually cut to an old-fashioned pattern: uptight hero shown the way to a freer life by a kooky intruder. It always used to be a kooky girl, an actress perhaps: now, time having moved on, it's a boy burglar. It's the actor who has to burst out. There is not enough wit to go round, but what there is is pleasant, and is well dispatched by Christopher Gable and David Baxt.
