PETER BARNES has all the right ideas. He wants theatre to matter, to change our thoughts, and if possible our lives.

He disapproves of cruelty, suffering, bigotry and privilege; also of routine theatrical naturalism. He believes in breaking down barriers by having his characters break into song, and for this material he goes to the best of modern musical comedy. He believes, I dare say, in the alienation-effect more wholeheartedly than Brecht himself ever did.

Mr Barnes often gives the impression of having read the shriller contemporary critics, and swallowed them whole. He then takes a further step--one the pundits themselves were too canny or too cowardly to take--and actually sets about writing and adapting plays. In the latter capacity at least, he has done us all some favours. I don't want, for the moment, to get involved in the rights and wrongs of his tamperings with the text of Ben Jonson (except to remark that his zeal to render the classics "relevant" is another example of his susceptibility to current dogma), but it is certain that without his enthusiasm we would never have seen The Devil is an Ass. Recently, too, he introduced us to a very funny Feydeau, The Purging, though it was typical that he should claim for his discovery that it shows "a Frenchman's main concern is not sex but money and the state of his bowels." The hero of The Purging is a manufacturer of chamber-pots; his professional preoccupations prove nothing at all about the rest of Parisian society.

Which brings us to Mr Barnes's latest, a double bill at the Royal Court: Part One, Ivan the Terrible; Part Two, Auschwitz; overall title Laughter!; purpose uncertain.

It may be to demonstrate that you can laugh at anything however horrible and feel better for it; or that you can laugh at anything however horrible and feel worse for it; or that some things are so horrible you can't laugh at all. The aim may be to challenge black comedy as a genre, or to show how people (as opposed to playwrights and audiences) can only contemplate atrocity by making jokes. Here Mr Barnes runs up against two handicaps: (a) he can't write people and (b) he can't write jokes.

He begins with an author-figure on the receiving-end of a custard-pie. No, he insists, it's not going to be that kind of show. "A sense of humour's no remedy for evil... It softens our hatred. We must root out comedy." The refrain "root it out, root it out" is taken up by the actors in the plays proper whenever they succeed in raising a chuckle. They are clutching, most of the time, at straws.

Tsar Ivan rages, repents, kills his heir and torments his courtiers while upstage sits one of his agonised victims, bloodily impaled. His presence has, no doubt of it, a dampening effect on one's sense of humour, but Ivan's jests would hardly raise the roof in any circumstances. "They don't," he says of the sufferer's screams, "write songs like that any more."

This is Mr Barnes's favourite, and draggy, anachronism joke. Most of the dialogue is in the mock-archaic style he used in The Bewitched, and it has a certain dusty effectiveness. At the close Ivan meets Death, who tells him that his random cruelties will soon be eclipsed by the computerised carnage of the twentieth century. This is our bridge to Part Two, which does indeed make us unexpectedly nostalgic for Part One, though not perhaps in the way intended.

We meet the civil servants who helped, from a distance, to run the death-camps, filing, typing memos, indulging in office squabbles, trying to get each other fired. Favourite weapon: trapping your colleagues into making jokes about the Fuhrer, which aligns with Mr Barnes's theme without illustrating it. Much of the piece is conventional home-front black-market comedy, and very feeble: ersatz seems the appropriate word. They are made, momentarily, to face the realities behind their work; they cry first that they couldn't help it, and second that it didn't happen anyhow. All accurate and unarguable, and in this lifeless context totally uninteresting.

At the end Mr Barnes does make a couple of points. He has his characters break into The Brotherhood of Man, a chorus from Frank Loesser's How to Succeed in Business Without Really Trying; Loesser's amiable satire on office life suddenly becomes sabre-toothed. (A couple of gags have earlier been pillaged from the same score, which makes, I suppose, for consistency.) Then a pair of Jews do a cross-talk routine on their way to the gas-chambers. (Timothy West and Derek Francis are very good here; the only point in Charles Marowitz's production at which the actors seemed at ease.)

Some good lines have been appropriated, and we do laugh, and then we do feel sheepish: needlessly, since our revulsion at Auschwitz is not altered in the slightest--any more than Tom Stoppard's two plays last year made us feel indulgent towards the treatment of dissidents in Russia and Czechoslovakia. These plays, which were really funny, make the final case against Mr Barnes: that comedy of any complexion requires an unforgeable gaiety of spirit, and that wishing will not make it so.

* * *

Charles Wood's Dingo is a rerun of the war in the desert, designed to destroy our illusions about Montgomery, Churchill and glory generally. Ten years ago, when the play was first done, this was necessary and even exhilarating; now it suffers, in every sense, from overkill. But Mr Wood's disillusioned fascination with army and theatre language and his bizarre stagecraft still strike intermittent sparks; when he fractures forms he does it from personal necessity, not according to a programme. (So he gets attacked by critics; his last, Jingo, got a fearful unmerited lashing from Martin Esslin, who usually likes everything. Especially Peter Barnes.)

Barry Kyle's Warehouse production does everything for the play except cut it. Paul Shelley as the clean-nosed malcontent Dingo and Ian McDiarmid as the mountebank Monty give nifty performances, though they cannot disguise that their roles are respectively under- and over-written. The best on hand are Paul Moriarty and the extraordinary Richard Griffiths, gentle and immovable.
