Rocking at the Court

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

Give us a play whose characters are young and we will take it to be a comment on a generation; give us a play about nothing at all and we will read in it as an allegory on the state of the nation. That is the way we have been programmed, over the last couple of years; and David Hare's Teeth 'n' Smiles (Royal Court), in some of its aspects, makes things easy for us. How else can we take a play that ends with a haunting chorus entitled "Last Orders on the Titanic"?

It is a fact, though, that this chorus (music by Tony Bicât) would sound just as haunting, and indeed just as meaningful, if heard completely out of context. The play is full of self-contained effects. They happen to be extremely effective effects, and your reaction to Teeth 'n' Smiles may well depend on whether you most value taste or aftertaste, on whether you want your gratification in the theatre or wherever you do your musing afterwards.

The production is crammed with life. Mr Hare joins the impressive line of dramatists (Howard Brenton, Trevor Griffiths, Snoo Wilson) whose plays have been shown off to best advantage when directed by Mr Hare. His staging here moves through a variety of tones with equal mastery: from a wild, captivating rock-and-roll chorus round a piano to a love-scene where visibility is reduced to--in the words of an older kind of song--two cigarettes in the dark.

The play, though, is concerned with a later variety of pop than either of these; it concerns a rock group, playing at a Cambridge May Ball in 1969. Much of its appeal is that of a backstage musical with superior dialogue; we watch the musicians before, between and after sets and enjoy their reactions--a mixture of incredulity and derision--to the surroundings in which their manager has landed them. Their weariness breeds wit; Mr Hare has a dashing way with one-liners and a rarer gift for building a gag over a period.

The band singer, Maggie, comes apart and doing her best to hurry the process along, living on alcohol, exhibitionism and contempt. Helen Mirren gives a terrific, lashing, frazzled performance, screeching her songs into the mike as if she hated herself and the whole world. Her performance is all that remains though it, too, is now poisoned. But the causes of her malaise are left muddy; the band are apt to remark, inaccurately, that it is no more than a pose that has got into the bone, and Mr Hare seems three-quarters inclined to agree with them. Alternately crying up Maggie's anguish and laughing it down, he ends up with what he probably most wants to avoid: sentimentality. The band's manager makes an overlong speech about a war-time bomb in the Cafe de Paris from which he finally draws a moral about the persistence of the class war. This, Maggie points out, is an idea that has for years saved him the risk and effort of action; but if she has an idea or a plan of action of her own she never lets on. Neither does the songwriter who discovered her and is trying to end their affair, furnished though he is with many of the play's best quips. Neither do the band, one of whom is going sparer than Maggie.

Occasionally a line like "the acid dream is over" suggests that we are in at the end of an era; 1969 was, no argument, the end of the sixties; but Teeth 'n' Smiles, unlike Kennedy's Children, cannot make this appear of more than statistical significance. Towards the close the play goes strident and a touch didactic, as if Mr Hare were trying to force us and himself into seeing a meaning. But he is defeated by his own eye (partly a satirist's eye) for the truth of the situation, and of the characters. If they behave like showbiz stereotypes (for Maggie read any suffering singer from Helen Morgan to Janis Joplin) that is what entertainers do; sticking a counter-culture label on them makes no difference. The manager and the tunesmith are stylishly played by Dave King and Jack Shepherd while the band, acting it up or laying it down, are marvellous.

* * *

The lack of any confrontation between rock and Cambridge is fair enough; rock presents no challenge and Cambridge is hardly present, though its one student is charmingly played by Antony Sher. (To have Mr Sher, last seen as Ringo Starr, as the only character from outside the music business, is a witty piece of casting; another is to bring on Heinz, one of the strange raves of the early sixties, as a projected star of the seventies.) There are moments in the play where the confrontation is presumed to have taken place but it is best to forget these and take the evening as a celebration--almost an embodiment--of musicians' cool, a perennial and generally uncommitted thing. On those terms, play and performance positively bulge with pleasures.

The highlight of the official Edinburgh Festival was, unexpectedly, the Nottingham Playhouse production of that chestnut As You Like It (Lyceum). Peter Gill's production begins slowly but forcefully; I have never known the battles in Oliver's orchard to be so determinedly fought. In the second scene the entry of Jane Lapotaire as Rosalind, resplendent in golden brown, destroys all doubts. She and Celia (Susan Tracy) appear almost like a couple of Moliere heroines as they discuss Rorqual, Nature and falling in love; from black and gold, straight-backed chairs, two elegant eminences on William Dudley's green box set.

Some Rosalinds drench themselves in love and then shower us with it; Miss Lapotaire is dryer, though as sensible of her own folly as of anyone else's. In the mock-wooing she describes ecstatic circles with her foot, the only outlet for her sense of the joy and absurdity of her situation. With her wit goes an unaccustomed gravity; no other Rosalind has spoken so seriously of her disguised meeting with her father or of her claims to be possessed of magic powers. These you may half believe; Mr Gill has arranged a cunningly magisterial exit for her on "I have left you commands." He has also dug into the relationship of Alun Armstrong's Touchstone, shining with city slickness, and Susan Porrett's Audrey, a large well-meaning girl trying to live up to a lover who, until the end, hardly deserves her; and he has achieved a consummate staging of "Blow, blow, thou winter wind," the singer spotlit and his hearers silhouetted.

The casting of old vaudevillian Leslie Sarony perhaps diverts more attention than is tactful to the faithful Adam (who goaded Shaw to exclaim "O, William Shakespeare, Esquire, you who died drunk, WHAT a moral chap you were!") but he doubles as a usefully befuddled Martext. Mr Gill's firm grip on the play expresses itself through the actors rather than bypassing them.

* * *

Moving towards the fringe, the Traverse, too, has an excellent company; Susan Carpenter, an actress at once sharp and sympathetic, governs a team notable for its pudgy vulnerability. This is a quality for which the Traverse's two tame playwrights offer much scope: Stanley Eveling's The Dead of Night comes over as a blend (not incongruous) of Peter Ustinov and Tom Stoppard. Little cosmopolitan soldiers debate on the roof of Hitler's bunker the night of his death. Only with Hitler himself, who emerges as rather a bore, does Mr Eveling falter. I preferred C. P. Taylor's Gynt to his reworkings of Sternheim and minor Brecht, the original Ibsen being so huge a work that it qualifies almost as a myth and so leaves plenty of room for retreading; it stays itself regardless. Mr Taylor's version requires that three actors be raised to the Peerage. Mike Ockrent's production is lovely.

On the fringe proper I was cast down by Instant Sunshine, four songster-pranksters dedicated to perpetuating the style of Flanders and Swann beyond its natural; I was cast up again by the Oxford Theatre Group's skilful and unpretentious production of Semmelweiss, a play by Garry O'Connor with a cracking good story (about surgical hygiene) and the wit to open out its implications. The University of Southern California, who regularly revive the very best musicals, lacked the guns for Gypsy, but were delightful in the more congenial Dames at Sea. Kimme Chaloukian might be Ruby Keeler incarnate (and she is a very touching Baby Gypsy). There were hundreds of other shows: I missed them.
