The Winter's Tale / The Crucible

Promenade performances as we have known them, mainly at the National, have been of pieces specially adapted from non-dramatic sources, or - as with The Passion or the Exeter version of The Dynasts - of plays so extensive as to need drastic re-working anyway. Making them go walkabout was only the final emendation; and one that suited their essential sprawlingness. It was almost comforting that we had to trail around behind the performance, never quite catching all of it; it testified to the size of the endeavour. The RSC's Nicholas Nickleby, was sort of a promenade in reverse; it moved around among us. These two RSC productions, brought to a cold and echoing but undeniably imposing London church after the provincial tour for which they were conceived, are the first promenades I have seen (or I suppose the right word is experienced) of orthodox plays: the kind you think twice about cutting.

Promenade Shakespeare sounds a reasonable idea; Bill Bryden thought of trying it with A Midsummer Night's Dream (or so said the initial publicity) but settled in the end for a modicum of audience involvement. It has been left to Adrian Noble, with The Winter's Tale, to go the whole peripatetic. He did not, except in the most literal sense, take me with him. Some of the fault may well have been mine. I arrived late, to find Leontes into his first jealous soliloquy. Even in a conventional performance, latecoming is its own penance. It takes time to adjust, physically and psychologically. You miss more than you actually missed. Sometimes you never catch up. That was my case here. My fellow spectators were pretty well rapt; caught up in the fable, Shared Experience style.

(The production is played on Persian carpets that reminded me strongly of that company's Arabian Nights.) Some magic had been worked of the kind that this tall and beautiful story requires. But when I looked at Alun Armstrong's Leontes, with his genial nose, I could not believe in him. The production risked a lot in making his jealousy comic, but it was the wrong kind of comedy: a therapeutic demonstration of the savage silliness of an imaginary cuckold. It would in fact have done very well in Moliere, but Mr Armstrong as a king - even in this production's variegated modern dress - I could not credit. Lynn Farleigh was a good if not remarkable Hermione, Janet Dale a labouring Paulina; she pushed her way through the verse instead of riding it.

Ron Cook's Autolycus, a historically early exponent of Cockney rock, left me dispassionate for or against, but the clowns were fine (they actually dared to be funny) with Trevor Martin's Old Shepherd, solid lovely, the best performance of the evening. There were some coups of the calibre we expect of Mr Noble. A frightening bear was one, remarkable at these quarters. The whole conduct of the trial was another, with king and queen on rostra at opposing ends of the hall, the freezing judicial atmosphere reinforced by the use of microphones. Hermione's defence, with Miss Farleigh at her best, was at once distanced and brought closer: distant from her auditors (she was a woman in a cruelly impossible position), closer to us (the woman on the stand became part of our own experience.) The literal and forcible breaking-up of Apollo's oracle was really exciting.

A quieter detail was the establishing of the married state of Paulina and Antigonus: something on which Shakespeare presumes but which he does little to depict. If the Bohemian revels- done with effortfully phallic folk-dancing and the audience set on the sidelines at supposedly festive tables - failed to convince they were in good traditional company. Less expected was the failure - for me, at any rate - of the final resurrection; that moving statue usually brings on the gooseflesh even in the most dismal productions. was not dismal; I should probably acknowledge it as the best Nunn's sixties version. I do, with the proviso that it comes a long way atter. The Winter's Tale, a play that rambles famously in space and notoriously in time, would seem a better bet for promenade production than The Crucible, a traditionally well-made piece composed of four long and independently located scenes.

It is the portrait of a society, but mainly by implication. The Barry Kyle- Nick Hamm production attempted to open it up with some community hymnsinging and by showing us, before the scripted action began, actors engaged in seventeenth-century rural crafts. looked, for the most part, like RSC actors engaged in seventeenth-century rural crafts; one could see why they were doing it but it was the attempt and not the deed that registered. The first scene was en- livened by letting us see Reverend Hale from Beverley arrive at Reverend Parris's Salem front door in a covered wagon. (Mr Parris seemed to have lodged his sick daughter in a very exposed place.) After all this period detail it seemed strange to find the court-room,like the one in the modern dress Winter's Tale, equipped with a microphone.

Was it to affirm the twinship of the two productions? Or to remind us of the affinity between Judge Danforth - the man behind the mike and contemporary witch-hunters? If the latter, it was needless. I have never seen a production which so strongly affirmed Salem's links with McCarthyism and subsequent persecutions: not because it was trying hard to be relevant, but because the play itself is rooted in the parallels and they were doing very well by the play. In its essentials the production was superb, outclassing both its National Theatre predecessors: by Laurence Olivier's florid version and Mr Bryden's tight-lipped one. Melodrama was given full rein without ever getting out of control, and the trial was the most physically exciting piece of theatre I have seen in ages.

This had something to do with the mechanics of staging - we lined the walls of a long courtroom-but more with the quality of performance. The actors were the right weight for this play, as they were not for The Winter's Tale, and they drove the scene on to its culmination in the cry of Mr Cook as the disillusioned Hale: "I denounce these proceedings! I quit this court!" The stage was in uproar but he dominated it; I have seen no other actor dare to carry off this moment. Actually it was the only moment I liked in his entire performance, which was otherwise maddeningly studied. It seemed to have its basis in Arthur Miller's description of him as like a young doctor on a house-call rather than in the ebb and flow of his relations with the other characters.

He played a generalised idea of quiet fanaticism; Hale is more interesting and more ordinary than that: more interesting because more ordinary, the play's Everyman in whose crumbling beliefs the action is mirrored. But everyone else was wonderful. Mr Armstrong's bulldozing honesty surpassed even Colin Blakely's; for once the long final scene between Proctor and his wife -usually a grinding shift from the political to the personal - did not seem an anti-climax. Miss Farleigh's melting ice-maiden of an Elizabeth, moved and moving (a vast improvement on her National performance) set him off splendidly. Julian Curry made a suave, powerful judge: an emblem of civilised bigotry. The girls were young enough for history but strong enough for drama, and the elders (Mr Martin and Margery Mason) invincibly and invaluably human.

And, yes, in the end you did feel you had met a community because you had met, and been involved with, a number of its inhabitants. Which proves that the truth about promenade performance is the same as the truth about the theatre in general: acting is all.
