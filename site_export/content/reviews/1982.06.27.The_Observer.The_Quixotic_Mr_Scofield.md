The Quixotic Mr Scofield

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Cervantes at the National Theatre.

THE increasingly prevalent view of Paul Scofield is that he is clearly a great actor, but. The reservations have to do with monotony. No actor can so transfix you with his voice; it grows more weirdly sonorous with each new role, and it more or less dictates the content of each new role. Mr Scofield offers us a cracked nobility; only the volume of the cracks varies from play to play. Think of an ideal role for him, and you might well come up with Don Quixote.

So, at the Olivier, enter Don Q. on cue. He is riding a tricycle, a penny-farthing with two small back wheels instead of one. Affixed to the handlebars is the head of his horse, Rozinante; Sancho Panza's donkey, and the play's other steeds, are similarly represented though in appropriately smaller fashion. (The convention works surprisingly well.)

In the second half of Keith Dewhurst's adaptation Mr Scofield has a beautiful, even moving, vision of universal harmony. At other times he incorporates into his delivery a virtuosic number of wheezes which make him difficult to understand, but which do convey the impression of a dear, deluded old man.

Also, of course, a gentle one. It would be unbearable to see Mr Scofield roughed up, and Cervantes's cruelty is accordingly soft-pedalled. The fight with the windmills takes place off-stage; this may well be a technical necessity, but it sets the tone of the production. Even Tony Haygarth, more vulnerable as Sancho, escapes with one hurried tossing in a blanket.

The interplay between the two leads is much the most rewarding feature of the evening; it reminds us of Mr Scofield's skill in comic timing and it allows Mr Haygarth, too, to be gentle. In his brief regime as governor he dispenses justice with the paradoxical panache of Azdak, but with a grief all his own. In comforting his master he draws closer to him, comes to share his madness. Michael Redgrave once nominated Paul Rogers as the only actor who could play both Don Quixote and Sancho Panza. Mr Haygarth is a contender.

The benefits, of course, from the fact that Sancho develops; Mr Scofield suffers from the fact that Quixote doesn't. The play suffers too. Ken Campbell's Illuminatus, Shared Experience's Bleak House, the RSC's Nicholas Nickleby, have all shown us that novels can live on the stage if their plots are respected. They have of course to be respectable plots in the first place. Don Quixote, being a comic epic, hasn't got one, and I don't see how any adaptation could avoid turning into a repetition of one joke.

In the later stages Mr Dewhurst (maybe the only adaptor who ever got as far as Part II of the original) tries to inject modern allusions, but they merely confuse the issue while further slackening its pace. Meanwhile Bill Bryden's production, faced with repeated scenes of rural riot, falls back on bucolic cliche: when in doubt, thwack a chambermaid. He has brought his Cottesloe company with him, but after the considered richness of their work in The Passion and Lark Rise, they are here dismayingly hesitant.

We don't even get period prose clearly delivered; we get the prose, but not the delivery. We also get what used to be the Albion Band, now hispanicised, at least in their headgear, and initially, and excitingly, in their rhythms. But they too grow homogenised, dragging in echoes of protest-rock and what sounded like the theme from Rawhide. William Dudley's set is a flat plain, that sometimes raises a corner flap to suggest Andalusian foothills. I, who have never been to Spain, was convinced, but still wished that it would do something else occasionally.

* * *

Even less hospitable terrain is explored in Abe Polsky's Devoir the Snow (Bush), an American play based on the experiences of the notoriously unfortunate Donner party who attempted to cross the mountains to California in 1846, and who mostly froze or starved or both. Those who survived had to eat their dead companions, and Mr Polsky's play concerns the trial of one survivor, accused by his companions of having slaughtered his own meal, rather than waiting for it to drop. (Actually he sues them, for slander, but the play comes off as his trial.)

Mr Polsky seems to be asking whether a man in his last extremity is still human; he decides, of course, that he is, but merely having the man mutter "human . . . humano . . . humanum" with desperate conviction does not illuminate the matter much. Nor is the fact that he is German, while his opponents are Americans who may have killed their Indian guides, much more than that: a simple fact.

But it is the facts that make this play thunderously successful, arranged as they are into an infallible courtroom drama on the 1950s pattern of The Caine Mutiny Court Martial and The Andersonville Trial, spotlighting characters, issues and events. If you stop to roll Mr Polsky's dialogue around in your mind, you are liable to find it stilted, but most of the time you will be too involved to care. Simon Stokes's production is as well orchestrated as his previous and very different American venture, The Miss Firecracker Contest: a palpable sense of horrors re-lived hangs over it, not least during the hushed interrogation of a 10-year-old child (Joanna Dukes, quite remarkable). Two performances, in opposite modes, drive the play: Stuart Wilson as the German in whom spitting resentment wars with burning self-hatred, and David Hargreaves, an actor whose excellence only seems to be recognised by this theatre and who finds stirring drama in an upright judge.

* * *

And so to the Warehouse. No sooner has the RSC turned its back on its experimental pad than Ian Albery, its former landlord, does the place up with mirrors, pink wallpaper, a bar and even comfortable seats with leg-room. All very stylish. So too is Robert Walker's production of Aunt Mary, a play in which Pam Gems seems to be writing under the influence of her son Jonathan, a specialist in the presentation of bizarre life-styles.

Here we have three male transvestites, running a garage and a small theatre while functioning pseudonymously as, according to convention, the 10 leading authors in the country. For all this 'bizarre' may not be the right word; Miss Gems takes her characters as they are, subjecting them to nothing so exploitative as dramatic exploration.

This may be tolerable, but it is also dull; there is the occasional smart line, but the story--involving love, jealousy and marriage, just like any menage--is negligible. The cast seem to know what they are about; Alfred Marks makes a splendid first entrance in a blue frock, looking like the all-time bully of St Trinian's, but his gruffness wears a little thin.
