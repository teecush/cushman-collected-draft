Problem of Plays and Praisers

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the year's theatre--'except in one area, the worst in recent memory.'

EVERY year the theatre magazine Plays and Players takes a poll of the London critics, counts their votes, and bestows its annual awards accordingly. We electors then scan the published results, eager less to learn who has won than to watch our colleagues laying their preferences on the line.

Very rum they are too, especially for 1977. I speak, mind you, as one who this year failed to spot a single winner, but then who wants to run with the pack? Especially when you consider some of the things the pack is running after.

Actually I am worried less by their enthusiasms (which were not in most cases silly, merely wrong) than by their enthusiasm. A rumour is going round to the effect that 1977 was a good year in the theatre. Actually it was, except in one area, the worst in recent memory; and I can only surmise that the local scribes, having cried doom in excess of the facts for a couple of seasons, had worn themselves down into hoarseness and tolerance.

This was the year in which off-form plays by Alan Bennett (The Old Country), Peter Nichols (Privates on Parade), Tom Stoppard (the one-night LSO bash Every Good Boy Deserves Favour), Howard Brenton (Epsom Downs), Howard Barker (That Good Between Us), Ted Whitehead (Mecca), Robert Bolt (State of Revolution) and William Douglas Home (The Kingfisher) were both offered and lauded. (Stephen Poliakoff hit a bad patch too with Strawberry Fields, but he got clobbered.) Wills were taken for deeds on an unheard-of scale.

There were deeds as well of course. I counted six: Alan Ayckbourn's Bedroom Farce, David Edgar's Destiny, Terence Rattigan's Cause Célèbre, Arnold Wesker's The Wedding Feast, Ken Campbell's Illuminatus! and Barrie Keeffe's A Mad World My Masters--the last, incidentally, the only one to begin its life on a metropolitan stage.

The Ayckbourn case is particularly interesting. Bedroom Farce is probably his funniest play to date and by the end it has become his most disconcerting. Just Between Ourselves does prod and so disconcerts less, though the fact that it isn't very funny either (certainly not in its London production) was enough to get it some rave notices. An advance for Mr Ayckbourn--an advance presumably towards being as turgid as the other critical favourites. But I doubt if he'll oblige.

Cause Célèbre was a beautiful swan-song for its good (not great) and lamented author, and the only piece of the year to communicate deeply-felt emotion. That art may have gone with him. Some writers--John Byrne, Pam Gems, Mary O'Malley, Howard Schuman--promised to be good, and two or three others promised to be promising. Several of the acknowledged mastodons were silent, though John Osborne published a closet playlet (in the coloured pages of the Sunday Times) about reviewers: over-indulgent to both its subject and (the usual Osborne failing) its loquacious central character. The supports, though, were allowed to make some bitchily accurate points.

The West End maintained its accustomed state of suspended animation. Bubbling Brown Sugar had a troubling success: troubling since it confirmed British ignorance of popular music, and the consequent improbability of our producing a good musical of our own. The ravers, presumably, had never heard those old Harlem songs before: otherwise they would have noticed that they were being misinterpreted, and lamely at that.

Another successful musical, Something's Afoot, launched a new management, Backstage Productions, who subsequently introduced us to the American playwright David Mamet, and re-acquainted us with Eduardo de Filippo. Franco Zeffirelli's production of Filumena got performances of Latin intensity from all its actors and showed us Joan Plowright and Colin Blakely at their best.

Joy was otherwise this year almost the sole prerogative of the Royal Shakespeare Company. My columns have been so full of its good works that I feel obliged to offer a few provisos; the 1977 company, after all, arrived in London having run in its actors and several of its productions during 1976 at Stratford. But it built on these foundations superbly; by the end of the year we had been made acquainted with close on 50 actors.

The National Theatre, by contrast, had names: some famous, many still indistinguishable. This made its shows less fun: a factor particularly evident in its studio productions. The RSC's Bandits and That Good Between Us are not better plays than the National's Half Life and Strawberry Fields, but they had, in the circumstances, more personality. The National seems committed to juggernaut tactics even in the Cottesloe--where, incidentally, its two friendliest and most stimulating productions (The Passion and Bow Down) reached for their material far into the past.

This is a pointer; the National needs to do classics in a less demanding setting than the Olivier. The actors, brain-washed into contemporaneity, might complain, but it would be good for them and, more importantly, for us. We are not starved of new plays, and only a playwrights' pressure-group pretends that we are. A fully-stretched company could heat up the open spaces of the South Bank. Peter Hall created one at Stratford 17 years ago; now the finest thing we have, it still bears his mark. It's time he repeated himself.
