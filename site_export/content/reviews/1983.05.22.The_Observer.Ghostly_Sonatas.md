Ghostly sonatas

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

SOMETHING, to put it gently, has been missing from the Peter Ustinov plays seen in London in recent years; Beethoven's Tenth (Vaudeville) strongly suggests that it was Mr Ustinov himself. As writer he has furnished an amiable little play, which as actor he proceeds to turn into a major comic event.

The text is a mix of Amadeus and Blithe Spirit. A contemporary London music critic (and failed composer) lives in unease with his wife who is a former singer, at odds with his son who has already written his own fifth symphony, and in open warfare with the au pair, an Austrian girl named Irmgard. She worships Beethoven, who happens to be the critic's special subject.

At the height of an argument, Irmgard, screwing her face up, wishes that Beethoven might actually appear before them, and what you imagine would happen happens. Everyone takes this manifestation calmly; a doctor fits the great man with a hearing aid. He has never, of course, heard his own complete works, so the critic sits him down to listen to them on disc.

Beethoven is very impressed by the shelves of review copies ('You mean critics get presents these days -- as well as bribes') and by his host's industry ('Articles appear week after week and will be collected in one large volume.') He is delighted to have aural proof of his own genius, though he thinks the Ninth far too long; it sends him to sleep, and is still playing when he wakes up.

He also knocks at the door like fate at the start of the Fifth, and refers to Liszt as 'that Hungarian boy with warts.' The play is full of jokes like these, shamelessly flattering the audience's shallow cultural sensibilities: the kind by which I am almost invariably slain. (I suspect that Tom Stoppard was bitten at an impressionable age by Mr Ustinov.)

Mr Ustinov moves slowly around in a cloud of dust, occasionally going into nervous spasms which leave his head and hands vibrating long after your ordinary limb would have called it a day; his uniqueness is to be simultaneously febrile and lethargic. He is cunningly matched here against the trim, barbed urbanity of Robin Bailey, an actor still coming into his kingdom. (As he says, accurately and with superlative timing, 'You are a great man and I am merely rather important.')

In Robert Chetwyn's refreshing production, enviably if unrealistically set by Kenneth Mellor, the rest of the household is well acted by Clare Higgins, Reece Dinsdale and Dilys Laye, though they have little to play. When Ludwig (so he is billed in the programme) sorts out their lives for them, it hardly matters, since their lives seem of so little account.

Nor is there anything fresh in his view of modern London; the writing gets tired in the second act and drops right off in a solemn reconstruction of the composer's own past. You do not go to the play for what Mr Ustinov has to say, but for the way in which he and Mr Bailey say it.

I now want to see our author in a revival of his greatest hit, The Love of Four Colonels. I am vulnerable of course; I grew up hanging on his every word as relayed on radio or television. (By the way, and while being nostalgic, I should like to clarify last week's gremlined copy; I first read Tiger at the Gates, as it then was, in the 1950s, not in 1950; I am not that old, nor, since the translation did not appear till 1955, that clever.)

Tom Lehrer wrote a song about the super-special just plain folks, all sluts and maniacs, in his home town; and another ('be it ever so decadent') about the South. Beth Henley's Crimes of the Heart (Bush) is like a simultaneous animation of the two of them.

In Hazlehurst, Mississippi, 'five years after Hurricane Camille,' live three sisters: Lenny, who ain't never had a man on account of feelin' self-conscious 'bout her shrunken ovary; Meg, who went to the big city to make it as a singer and couldn't; and beautiful Babe who has just shot her husband after prolonged unpleasantness.

She says 'he started hating me cos I couldn't laugh at his jokes' (a key line) and eventually she took up with a well-endowed coloured boy; 'Why, Babe,' says one of her sisters, interested rather than aghast, 'I didn't even know you were a liberal.'

Miss Henley obviously cares about her characters, but finds them, and the gothic world they inhabit, very funny. But she is satirising a literary convention rather than a real one. Her wide-eyed view of disaster recalls Joe Orton, but unlike him she is not attacking a social structure: she is merely sending herself up as she goes along. This has a tart, appealing, but unsatisfying effect.

Two of the players in Simon Stokes's production seem out of place, but Robert Glenister as Babe's defence lawyer is shyly effective, and I have few complaints about the sisterhood. Wendy Morgan has intermittent trouble with Babe's outrageousness, but Amanda Redman makes a swashbuckling Meg, and Brenda Blethyn as Lenny gives a wonderful display of mixed-emotion close-up acting.

Liza Minnelli (Apollo Victoria) begins her act singing, offstage, a neglected Yip Harburg lyric, 'There's a Great Day Coming Manana.' My heart was warmed, but it cooled as she entered and the song continued: she seemed to have rewritten its quizzical sentiments in the interests of upbeat. And so it went; though what with an atrocious sound system, a skilled but overblown orchestra, and a lack of any vocal colouring between smoke and fire, it was not always easy to tell what she was singing.

It works for some people. The audience screamed loudest when the word 'CABARET' appeared in neon; take a bow, Mr Electrician. The Guardian proclaimed her one of those 'like Billy Holiday (sic, and again sic) and Edith Piaf,' who can transform rubbishy material into music and poetry. Actually most of her material is first-rate; and she transforms it into gibberish.

Not always: I respect anyone who can even remember the words of the 'Trolley Song' (they're mainly sound-effects) and when she quietens down and gives herself the chance to be funny or sad, she proves she can still be a fine singing actress: as she was on her first records and in Pookie and Cabaret.

Her writer, Fred Ebb, who has staged the show with some economy and style, has supplied a routine about her getting the Oscar for the latter film, after losing out to Maggie Smith when nominated for the former. I sympathised; it should have gone to Jane Fonda.

At the Cocteau, temporarily beached from a schools tour, there is an inventive workshop Macbeth, on which I hope to enlarge sometime; but I want now to memorialise a neglected death. Pat Smythe is not a theatre name, though he did once appear in a play at the Open Space; he was, though, one of this country's finest jazz pianists, a tolerant and sympathetic accompanist (as I have some cause to know) and a soloist at whom I have seen American virtuosi stand and marvel.
