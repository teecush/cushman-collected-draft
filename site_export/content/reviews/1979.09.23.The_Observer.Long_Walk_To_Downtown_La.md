Long walk to downtown LA

ROBERT CUSHMAN, our theatre critic, reports on a recent visit to California.

THE popular idea of Los Angeles as an endless series of suburbs is not quite accurate. It does have a downtown, which is unlike any other metropolitan centre on earth. It's very peaceful, for the excellent reason that hardly anybody goes there. And it's equally far away from everything -- from everything, that is, except the theatre.

There is a civic arts complex in the heart of town. You may die of loneliness on the walk there, but it's impressive when you arrive. There is the immense Dorothy Chandler Pavilion, where the Civic Light Opera performs musicals (usually sharing them with San Francisco); it is one of the few theatrical organisations in the Western world to turn an infallible profit.

They were doing, when I was there, a pretty tepid production of Bells are Ringing. But better things were happening in the legitimate house, the Mark Taper Forum, which is a steeply raked and well proportioned thrust theatre. They were offering two linked plays by Lanford Wilson. Mr Wilson is a playwright who was known here in the sixties for a couple of one-acters that were among the earliest successes of the London fringe, but has since vanished from our sight. We should rediscover him.

The present plays concern an old Missouri family coming to terms with the outside world. In the first, Talley's Folly, set in 1944, the daughter of the house is wooed and won by a Jewish accountant. The scene is a boathouse: a set-up for atmospheric magic as the suitor, speaking for the author, brazenly admits. Self-conscious though it may be, the device works, and the mating-dance sparkles.

The Fifth of July, a more elaborate play, moves us on to 1977. The girl of the previous piece is now a widow, cherishing her husband's ashes, but preparing to part with them as the younger generation -- the Berkeley radicals and Vietnam casualties of the sixties -- try to find some use for the past in the present. The family wrangle is not my favourite kind of drama, but Wilson -- a playwright firmly in the American mainstream -- comes closer to revivifying it than any playwright I can think of. He has a winningly light touch, and his humour is always surprising. These plays, already successes at New York's admirable Circus Rep, surpass almost any of the American plays we have recently imported.

* * *

The end of the seventies has meant a reappraisal of the sixties. Topping all is Coppola's Apocalypse Now, playing in LA to five packed houses a day. The film is less about what America did to Vietnam, than what it did to its own youth. That was, if not a greater horror, a more immediately frightening one, and it was reflected in the protest movement. 'Hell, no, we won't go' was a more effective slogan than 'Ho, Ho, Ho Chi Minh.' At least that's how it sounds now.

At the Odyssey, a fringe theatre in Santa Monica, I saw The Chicago Conspiracy Trial, a reconstruction of those infallibly theatrical proceedings in which Black Panther Bobby Seale was gagged and bound to his chair, Yippie leader Abbie Hoffman drolly promised the court 'a very exciting cross-examination,' and over which Julius Hoffman (no relation, of course, but how beautiful the coincidence) presided as a judge Dickens might have written and Olivier acted. George Murdock's performance was actually on that scale; outrageously detailed in its pique and self-importance, tragic in the inevitability of its increasing tyranny once the first step was taken.

The trial was abridged with a careful eye to the juicy bits (I remember the brief apparition of Mayor Daley complacently regarding his fingernails while the judge commended him as an ideally co-operative witness), though with little regard for what the cases for prosecution and defence actually were. Two statements struck home. One, unexpectedly, was the prosecutor's allegation that the defendants undoubtedly had come to the Democratic Convention looking for trouble; against that was Abbie's avowal that he and the other supposed conspirators 'couldn't agree on lunch.' Only conclusion: the accused were guilty as charged, but they shouldn't have been charged.

The Chicago trial now seems at once supremely important and supremely ridiculous. This show, with a cast of 48 (thank Hollywood unemployment and fringe salaries), was breathlessly exciting, painfully nostalgic, and plain useful.
