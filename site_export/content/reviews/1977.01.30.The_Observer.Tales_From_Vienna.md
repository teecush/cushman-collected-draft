Tales from Vienna

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

Shamefully few of us had heard, until very recently, of Odon von Horvath, and most of us must still be in some doubt how to pronounce him. After the triumphant opening of Tales from the Vienna Woods (Olivier, National Theatre) we should have plenty of opportunity to learn. I advise you to go down to the woods at the earliest opportunity (there may be crowds but persevere); you will, though, be sure of more than the regulation big surprise.

As a German play of the thirties it is part of a still-growing legend. Horvath was actually born in Hungary, and he died in exile in Paris (a tree fell on his head in the Champs Elysées: an ironically impersonal end for a playwright concerned, on this evidence, with strictly human weakness and malevolence). But his career was in Berlin, and he was driven out by the Nazis.

Hindsight makes this play (staged in 1931) look at moments like a prophecy of specific cataclysm. But hindsight can of course do that job far more thoroughly itself; there are the expected sinister nightclub scenes in Tales from the Vienna Woods, but as intimations of Fascism they hardly compare with those in Cabaret. And though suitably ominous headlines illustrating the rise of Hitler are projected on to a screen, they seem to have little direct connection with what is happening on stage.

It is significant that Horvath, writing for a Berlin audience, set his play at a discreet but hardly flattering distance, in Austria. The title reveals the strategy; Strauss waltzes are constantly in our ears and Maximilian Schell's production sets the actors and the stage itself whirling in sympathy. Stephen Rea as Alfred, our hero, is a particularly nifty mover, continually doubling back on himself, always akimbo.

Actually Alfred is distinctly shabby, a small-time horse-player and gigolo; he is in himself enough to give the lie to the schmalz of old Vienna. Maybe Horvath overworks the irony, but the Danube in this production is so delectably evoked (intending bathers hold their noses, jump into the orchestra pit, and are seen no more) that he is worth indulging. A picnic by the river, all balloons and amateur photography and horrible children, is a tour de force. It is here that Alfred falls, so to speak, in love with Marianne, who works in a toyshop, and she genuinely in love with him. They have a child; Alfred runs out. Marianne, too tender-hearted for a career on the streets, winds up as a nude cabaret dancer: another story of old Vienna. This one, however, being squalid rather than glamorous, is for real.

At every step Marianne is betrayed by the authority of age: by her father, who disowns her in an ecstasy of sentimental horror, by the Church, which refuses her absolution unless she surrenders her child, by the Baroness who runs the nightclub, most hideously, by Alfred's grandmother, who allows the baby to die (and on whom Alfred himself is financially dependent). The horrors of the past make possible those of the future; the crown of Kate Nelligan's Marianne is her silent assurance, beyond stoicism, that the worst is yet to come (a point sympathetically made in Christopher Hampton's preface to a translation which reads stiffly but acts a treat).

Miss Nelligan - in bewilderment, anger, desolation or defiance - is superb. She is also believably good. Warren Clarke matches her as her first fiancé, an unromantic butcher (with a pork-pie hat for special occasions), Grosz in outline but sedate in feeling, and at the last crushing her with forgiveness; "I once told you, Marianne, you wouldn't escape my love" is a truly terrifying line, clinching a finale that must be one of the high points of twentieth-century drama. There are a dozen other performances to mention (including the leathery insouciance of Elizabeth Spriggs as Alfred's protectress); for once, everybody on stage looks right.

* * *

Scarborough, many have said and I can now confirm, is the place to see an Alan Ayckbourn play; his new Theatre in the Round (comfortably made over from a secondary school) currently houses his new Ten Times Table, a comedy about the organisation of a small town festival: more accurately perhaps about the organisation of a small town festival committee. The central historical event to be commemorated is a premature peasants' revolt. The meeting splits between the progressives (a Marxist schoolteacher in the van) and reactionaries, the latter heavily hit by apathy, but rallied by the arrival of a dog-breeder with the instincts of a commando and limited reserves of self-control. A minute after saying hello he is liable to scream of the opposition, "they are scum" to somebody. Christopher Godwin makes this a golden moment.

Others belong to a frail old lady whose conversational contributions are erratic (since her hearing is sharper than might be thought, though duller than could be wished), but contain at least three of Mr Ayckbourn's choicest lunacies. I also enjoyed her son, a dedicated committee man with apologetic glasses but terribly eager teeth. The author directs with an impeccable feeling for nuance, as well he might; this may not be his best play (it's a little too easy-going); but the cast give it a solidity that I would not expect the West End to match. This, in Scarborough, is popular theatre (community theatre, if you remember that old thing); the National should add it to their list of regional imports.

* * *

Good wishes to the new regime at the Royal Court, and commiserations to the old who, considering their inheritance (and I don't mean money) have not done as badly as has been trumpeted; there have been many rockier years in Sloane Square than the last, and the recent spectacle of the Court's biggest pots and kettles begriming one another has not been edifying.

As a rule, these days, if you want to buy their wares, follow me and climb to the Theatre Upstairs, treading carefully for fear of Traps, Caryl Churchill's latest, which has wit and much good acting, six characters, several relationships and a stark rural setting, and kept losing me. It ends with each person in turn stripping off and getting into a tin bath; the last is reluctant (he wears a tie, carries a box of chocolates and is repressed generally), but gives in and, though the water is by now an unspeakable colour, is probably the better for it. I don't want to dismiss this play. Actually, I don't think I dare.
