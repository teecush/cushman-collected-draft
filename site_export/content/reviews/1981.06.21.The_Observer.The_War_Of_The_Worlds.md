The war of the worlds

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

TWO days after I had seen The Worlds (New Half Moon) I met a man who told me that Edward Bond had based his play on Timon of Athens. I wished (this is beginning to sound like one of Mr Bond's own parables) that he hadn't spoken. The Timon connection had struck me while I watched the play but I wanted it to be a surprise.

Mr Bond's Timon figure is an industrialist named Trench who is kidnapped by terrorists and who discovers when released that his subordinates have taken advantage of his absence to vote him out of office. Like Timon he invites them to a farewell party in order to insult them, and then disappears from society (although, like Tony Hancock when he became a hermit in one of his radio half-hours, he is careful to choose a wilderness within reach of the shops). In exile he re-encounters his former captors who now have a new prisoner and, in a bored disinterested way, offers them sanctuary. As he says, unlike his supposed friends, "those people told the truth: they said they were my enemies."

Like Shakespeare, Mr Bond runs into trouble finding things for his solitary misanthrope to do and fitting him with a credible opposition, but he does tell a better story than Shakespeare. The play has the customary Bondian virtues of speed, clarity and economy.

All the same Trench's gratified bitterness, as he sees violence conquering on all sides, threatens to take over the play. He manages somehow to welcome the chaos while despising its activators; and he has an important dramatic advantage which the author may not have calculated. He is always alone. He may behave smugly to his colleagues while still in power but they are characterised as creeps who deserve it.

When threatened by the terrorists he stands his ground, and his scorn for his supporters ("One advantage of being chucked out of my organisation is that I don't have to put up with your inane toadying") is completely captivating. One naturally sympathises with a man under the gun; a threatened human life always counts for more than whatever cause is menacing it. In addition, Ian McDiarmid's performance as Trench, one of his best essays in the grotesque-sardonic, dwarfs the rest of a competent cast. Theatre is an undemocratic form, responsive to star power.

To redress the balance Mr McDiarmid doubles as Trench's most articulate opponent (it's unfortunate that the two can never meet) who closes the play by declaring that "we're all terrorists... we live by terror." Mr Bond is at his best in attacking the hypocrisy that condemns one kind of violence while ignoring or justifying another, though he fails to acknowledge that that is a characteristic of the Left as much as of the Right. There are two scenes of confusion in which everybody shouts at once: one takes place among the capitalists, one among the guerrillas. Maybe we are meant to equate them.

The terrorists are acting in support - unsolicited as far as we can tell - of a strike. Second time round, the terrorists get the wrong man: a chauffeur, instead of his employer. He lies on the floor, tied hand and foot and bundled in white, for most of the second act. He is obviously meant to be a powerful image; Trench uses him as a cue for speeches about the human animal.

But he turns the play false; if this mistake were made in real life, the different parties would hardly be able to move fast enough to disentangle themselves. But here the jailers go on holding him (keeping him trussed up apparently for the author's visual convenience); the strikers make no concessions; the bosses do, but only because they realise it will improve their public image.

Mr Bond misses the deeper irony which is that they would probably act out of quite genuine humane feeling, which certainly would bump up the profits and leave things essentially unchanged. Every now and then Mr Bond slides in explicit lectures which, since they are brief and well written, the audience take like lambs; but which neither support nor are supported by the action of the play. You believe them or not, according to the ideas you have brought into the theatre with you; and the author seems to have written them in the same spirit.

However, they are not the whole play; and if Mr Bond sometimes seems to walk into complexities he wishes weren't there, such misadventures are a bonus for the rest of us. The play takes its title from the observation - acknowledged by both sides, though with different emphasis - that there are two worlds, everyday civilised discourse and the brute moneyed reality beneath; and there is power in the collision.

Trench by the way does act in the end: murderously. It occurred to me that his nihilism was meant to reflect that of Mr Bond's bete noire Samuel Beckett. Actually it is impossible to imagine a Beckett character killing anyone, even himself; in his world, talk is sufficient purgation. The wounds Mr Beckett contemplates seem to be his own rather than, as in Mr Bond's case, other people's; which sounds selfish in theory but in dramatic practice isn't.

* * *

However, I don't think Texts (Riverside Studios), derived from Texts For Nothing and How It Is, counts as dramatic practice. The legendary American director Joseph Chaikin is sole performer; fitted out with some Chaplinesque props he proves an accomplished mime, shifting from haunch to haunch in sympathy with the Beckett rhythm of instant self-contradiction, and a sensitive speaker. But he is also disconcertingly ethereal; to make Beckett's non-theatrical texts work on stage you need, I suspect, to go full-tilt for paradox and gallows humour. Probably you need to be Irish.

* * *

In Herbert Achternbusch's Ella (ICA) a man tells his mother's life story - one of poverty, humiliation and confinement - while she slumps in a chair, a zombie intent on watching television which she turns up louder when he collapses. That probably sounds a potent juxtaposition; at moments it is, especially since Bill Paterson, yet another virtuoso actor, gives yet another virtuoso performance, while Janet Henfrey is a picture of pathos.

But wait: Mr Paterson is not merely telling us about her, he is impersonating her; at least he is speaking in the first person and wearing a frock. In addition he is addressing us from behind wire-netting; the set is a chicken-run, infested by real birds. They are too, with their lofty squawks and scrambled feet. Mr Paterson, in many a throwaway, directs our attention to them as well as to his own situation as a Scots actor enacting a play set in Bavaria (not an area any current German playwright recommends). It is, in every sense, a lot to get through.

* * *

Unexpectedly I got more out of The Rocking Chair (at the Fountains Abbey pub in Praed Street), a first play with characters by no means as fascinating as their author, Gregory Day, thinks they are but still building up to an interesting two-edged study of selfishness. It is paired with Adaptations by Elaine May which has all the marks of a Sixties satire on the American Way of Life; some of them are amusing marks.
