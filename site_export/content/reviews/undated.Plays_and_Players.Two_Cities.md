She prophesied that it would end with a song called ‘It’s a Far, Far Better Thing’ and if she reads this I’d like her to know that she was right. But she didn’t foresee that it would begin with a disembodied choir singing ‘It was the best of times, it was the worst of times’ and so on right through the opening paragraph of the novel, ending fortissimo with the un-Dickensian interpolation ‘It was NOW’. This last statement the rest of the show reveals to be a blatant fabrication. It wasn’t now, it was then, and a very timeless sort of then it turns out to be. Paris has not altered much since *The Vagabond King* but I suppose rabble will be rabble whatever the period. Proof positive that this is a Dickensian musical is offered by the fact that hardly has the curtain risen before the air is filled with the cries of street-vendors. Warning that the entertainment will not be wholly wholesome is provided by the attempt of two guards to drag a girl off to satisfy their master’s ‘droit de seigneur’. The mob will have none of this and give them a basinful of the old choreographic menace treatment—two steps forward, two back and a lot of scowling. Defarge, the people’s spokesman, attempts to pass it all off as ‘High spirits, your excellency’ (*whose* excellency?) but with a gloomy bunch like that he doesn’t stand a chance.

So much for atmosphere and now the plot starts. Lucie Manette arrives to collect her father (‘What’s he doing?’ she asks. ‘He’s making shoes,’ replies Defarge, though I think this line really belongs to the audience) and to tell him (in a song called ‘Tender Love and Patience’) that all will be well when they get to England. ‘And you’ll grow strong before too long,’ she chirrups.

We get to England just in time to catch a song-and-dance routine by Sydney Carton and his legal colleague Mr Stryver. Carton says that he’s an independent man. Stryver wants to know what Carton would do without him and they then go through it all again contrapuntally. (This, the programme helpfully explains, is called a Duet.) And then we are at the Old Bailey for the trial of Charles Darnay, whom you might take, unless you’re looking at the two of them, for Sydney Carton’s double. As the chorus remarks, they definitely, definitely, *definitely*, DEFINITELY (and here I lost count) look alike. This, of course, means that Darnay is acquitted. (Don’t ask me why. I have no head for legal subtleties.) He and Carton indulge in another Duet on the subject of our heroine. The burden of Charles’ song is that ‘Lucie is her name’. Carton is fly enough not to dispute that one, merely replying that she is, after all, but a Golden-Haired Doll. Since this is not an American musical I don’t think he means that as a compliment. Carton, you might think, has seen right through Lucie, but left alone he turns out not to have the courage of my convictions.

In Paris things have got worse and Madame Defarge is threatening to organise the French Revolution. ‘What do you want to do then? Attack the Bastille,’ says her husband (sarcastically, I think, but the performance is so impassive it’s difficult to be sure) but she elects to petition the Queen instead. This seems to take her some months, since by the time we see her again we have been witness to the entire courtship of Lucie and Charles, not to mention some stage-stealing displays of irony from Carton, but maybe there’s a double time scheme like in *Othello*. Anyway, Madame Defarge is furious at being told to go and eat cake, though anybody could have told her it was bound to happen, and this time they do all storm the Bastille. Their methods are unorthodox (they involve much brandishing of evil-looking pitchforks in every direction of the compass so that it never becomes very clear where the Bastille actually is) but they certainly work. By the beginning of Act II the mob are in command, and are playing happily with a charming portable guillotine in pillarbox-red.

In Act II Dickens’ infallible narrative (expertly if ruthlessly carpentered by Constance Cox) tends to get the upper hand, but there are still one or two delights for the connoisseur. I particularly relished the Knitting Song, sung by the Knitting Women, who look as if they’re racing with the clock in a faded tour of *The Pajama Game* which somebody has mischievously supplied with the music of the gendarmes’ song from *Orpheus in the Underworld*. And they can cut a Carmagnole, to what sounded to me suspiciously like the strains of ‘America’. Perhaps the *West Side Story* score is now in the public domain, because I’ll swear that ‘Gee Officer Krupke’ turned up last year in *The Beggar’s Opera*.

No actor has the right to fail as Sydney Carton; but even so Edward Woodward’s performance places him firmly among our best romantic actors (an underemployed bunch, incidentally).

It’s amusing to think of *Two Cities* playing just a few blocks away from *Hair*. They deserve each other. Do go and see it. But not on your own; make up a large party.
