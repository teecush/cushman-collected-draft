Revivals and the West End

Pendulums regularly swing, and the cry of the immediate post-war years—that the London theatre's mania for revivals was stifling a new generation of dramatists—is heard again in the land. It may be that our theatre moves in ten-year cycles—in the decade beginning 1955 revivals (Shakespeare doesn't count) were apologetic stop-gaps for the rare occasions when a new dramatist had absent-mindedly failed to get himself delivered. By 1961 he was failing with perturbing regularity; Kenneth Tynan noticed, and trouble was obviously in store. After rebuking the new wave for rejecting bourgeois tradition (you never knew where you were with Ken) he paused to remark that there were only three plays on in London more than ten years old, and what had happened to history? At that moment, perhaps, a Dramaturg was born. Anyway, before the appointed ten years were out, he was in a position to do something about it.

He has, too. Fashions may come and go but the English theatre's sacred mission remains what it has always been: to present the plays I want to see in the way I want to see them. The National Theatre has fulfilled the first half of the prescription to near-perfection. And, though it matters little in comparison, they have obviously been plays that a lot of other people have wanted to see as well; the greedy audiences accumulated by the National and the Royal Shakespeare Company probably contribute the most important theatrical phenomenon of the sixties. The commercial managements certainly think so: what lay back of the “dirty plays” row of 1964 but resentment of the fact? Over the last couple of years, they have taken on the subsidised companies at the game. Hence, the revival boom. Peter Bridge, the manager most prolifically responsible, declared flatly last month that revivals were an economic necessity, and no useful purpose is served by arguing with that. Go out and risk your own money instead.

Here, then, follows no diatribe: I myself am an avid consumer of revivals. And, being as honest as I can, I cannot see this as escapism—as being afraid of what new plays might do to me. Most of the time, they don't do anything to me, though as a rule the critics have promised that I will be lacerated, transported, elated, or irrevocably transformed to the marrow of my being. There are not, really, many plays able to do this to me but I live in hope. Such hope can have no foundation when I already know the text; I go without false expectations, and so cannot be disappointed. Add to this an Eng. Lit. grounding which makes the piecing together in performance of dramatic history a positive, pleasurable experience.

To take an example from Mr Bridge's current list: I knew beforehand that *Justice* was a pretty dead play, and pace Harold Hobson's genuflection to the valiant piety of doing it so as only just to miss the Galsworthy centenary, I knew from the ads (“by the author of *The Forsyte Saga*”) that it had been mounted as the merest cashing-in job. Never mind, it was heartening to find Galsworthy's programmatic humanitarianism still striking the occasional chord, a dim echo of the Dickensian power to send the reader striding round the room foaming at the iniquity of Chancery and framing passionate letters to the papers about it. Galsworthy has of course, nothing of Dickens' ability to transmute the law into an image of the impersonal inhumanity of society; a pity, since this was the effect he was aiming for—he tells us so, repeatedly, but without ever making us feel it. There is a hint of lumbering power in the pathetic victim's final suicide, but the mechanism that has crushed him is less social than theatrical. Richard Briers' performance is mechanical too, but unavoidably so, since the dramatist shies away from the character throughout—so the play turns into one of those lopsided affairs where the commentator becomes the protagonist; here it's the kindly managing clerk (a stock character, but quite healthy stock) whom Eric Portman underplays to great effect, though his high reputation was surely won in bloodier fields than this.

The dead play has been fitted out in a suitably dead production, but it's a solid funeral. The National would probably have done better by it, but I can't pretend to regret that they didn't bother. If they ever get those twin auditoria and that fifty-play repertory, then maybe; for Galsworthy's 150th. The trial scene at the St. Martin's, though ashen, is a mite lovelier than the glass-boothed one which formerly held that stage.

But though *Justice* is nice to have seen, if not to see, some of Mr Bridge's other choices jar. I am sufficiently a child of my generation not to be able to face *Dear Octopus* (largely out of a sneaking suspicion that I would thoroughly enjoy it), and I do not understand the general consensus that it is “brave” to revive *Hay Fever* so soon after the National Theatre triumph. Just shadow-boxing: and a significant indication of how little of Coward's massive output is revivable (four plays, and here we go round again). But *Too True to Be Good*—the production which started the whole thing off—was a genuine piece of creative impresage: obscure play, unlikely casting, and total success; in general, Peter Bridge's productions have escaped the tattiness that is endemic to large-cast plays in the West End. In the West End's recent collected edition of the minor plays of Oscar Wilde, his *Ideal Husband* positively shimmered in comparison with Tennents' erratic *Lady Windermere's Fan* and John Gale's desperate *A Woman of No Importance*—opportunism gone completely to seed. Tennents have of course redeemed themselves with *The Importance of Being Earnest*—had they failed with that play, an era would really have crumbled. I suspect Robert Chetwyn of being a talented director; faced with three out of four less than ideal lovers and a Chasuble and Prism splendidly capable of relegating them all to sub-plot status, he balanced the play impeccably.

Contrary to rumour, the West End has not become a hotbed of all-star classics. The actors have in general been featured second-rankers—married couples are especially popular, possessors of loyaller publics than accrue to most stars in their frantic passage from the film studios to prestige appearances with “the companies”. The plays have been pop favourites angling for the nostalgia of those who caught them first or last time around; the material, when thinned out a bit, of a future classic repertoire but not of ours. The Haymarket's attempt, with *The Rivals* and *The Merchant of Venice* to aim beyond this, was a startling reminder of time passed. I do not, Heaven help us, believe that divine inspiration has descended on the Aldwych and Waterloo Road, but how fussy, how thin, how unnecessary these productions seemed, and how slighting (not, maybe, that he bothered) to the great actor at their centre. To have given the finest Shakespearean performance in the worst Shakespearean production, of the last few years, is a paradox peculiarly befitting Richardson. The death of Donald Wolfit, while an inferior *Volpone* holds court at the Old Vic, should have rung ironic bells in somebody's ears. We still waste talent on a colossal scale.

More so, perhaps, than before. In that first era of revivals, just after the war, the big guns were firing in the West End (Gielgud worked consistently for Tennents and Olivier had a permanent lien on the now defunct St. James's) and, on the whole, at bigger targets. There were even excursions into the hinterland beyond Sheridan—a few Jacobeans and much Restoration, this largely in the hands of John Clements, an actor-manager whose career is a pattern of the whole period. As the Royal Court came in, he went out—in some glory, with a distinguished season of genuine classics at the Saville. During the fervent years, he confined his West End appearances to mainly second-rate modern plays: now he suddenly re-surfaces at Chichester, which under his influence surely counts as an annexe to the West End rather than to the subsidised belt—if only because he too is now concerned mainly with the recent past. Eliot and Wilder are on the bill for this year while last year, *The Beaux' Stratagem*, once his biggest success of all, went ignominiously under.

The inference is plain. Once it mattered that the West End's energies went into revivals: it mattered because the choice and execution of plays showed those energies to be the best there were. Now, the action is elsewhere; when the National or the Royal Shakespeare blunder (they often do, and this last year has been a honey) we are right to be concerned, for where else do we go? But passion forsook Shaftesbury Avenue long ago, leaving only a variegated collection of shows: when they are good, I applaud—when bad, I shrug. They are likelier to be good when old (to put it in instantly graspable form, the best reason for reviving *The Boy Friend* is *The Four Musketeers!*); when the present is arid, one can only cling to the past.

Robert Cushman
