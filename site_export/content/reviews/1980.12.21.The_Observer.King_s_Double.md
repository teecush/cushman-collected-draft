King's double

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

THE King's Head -- I speak with considerable personal bias -- has offered more consistent pleasure this year than almost any other London theatre. Their new double bill is a lovely finale.

The curtain-raiser is Stephen Oliver's brief opera, A Man of Feeling. Based on a story by Schnitzler, it finds a taut, mocking musical style to match the nervous elegance of its author. The title figure is a man who kills himself when he discovers that his mistress, an aspiring singer, is using him on medical advice as a therapeutic means of recovering her lost voice.

The action is flashback within flashback. Oz Clarke slips lightly between hero, doctors and executor, whose lament 'he was so nice, you know nice' -- a rising vocal line -- embodies Mr Oliver's vivacious flair for marrying his own words to his own music. Nichola McAuliffe gives a sportingly funny account of the peremptory canary.

After this, and playing further tricks with time, love and music, comes Shadow Play, one of the playlets Noel Coward wrote in the late Thirties as vehicles for himself and Gertrude Lawrence, mainly on the theme of how irresistibly sophisticatedly louche the two of them were. In many respects the piece is a compressed and softened version of 'Private Lives.' Here are Simon and Victoria Gayforth, obviously made for each other (or at least not noticeably made for anybody else), but on the brink of divorce.

Simon and Victoria do more than Elyot and Amanda to justify their privileged position; they sing and dance (to three of Coward's best songs, including the unanswerably insistent 'You Were There'), giving their impersonators the chance to play at being Fred and Ginger as well as Noel and Gertie.

John Standing and Estelle Kohler take it with communicated relish. The defiant smile, daring disbelief, flashed at the audience by Mr Standing tapping would conquer a coven of critics. His voice taps too. Some jazz singers are said to sing like instruments (horns usually); Mr Standing, urgently insisting 'Play, Orchestra, Play,' gives out like a rhythmic typewriter not much tone but plenty of beat. He is a bit like Rex Harrison, but a lot more like James Cagney.

With his jauntiness and his timing go something I doubt Coward himself could have equalled: a down-to-earth compassion. He guides Victoria back through her memories when she takes a mild overdose; the nostalgic trip -- fuelled by all that potent cheap music, dramatised in time-slips that seem refreshing even now -- saves their marriage. (Well, what else did you expect?)

Both players find a style of their own for the frightfully brittle badinage. I laughed more than I have at any Coward play in years. Miss Kohler, baring snowy teeth between vermilion lips, is a queen bee: sound all honey, sense all sting. The toughest baby-face since Nelson, she also contrives to be languorously vulnerable. Lizzie Romilly and Anthony Hyde are the interlopers; Miss McAuliffe (who acts contralto, though she sings soprano) and Malcolm Mudie are faithful friends. None of them misses a trick. Add to this an unexpected visual sheen (silky settings by Norman Coates) and fun choreography (David Toguri), and Peter Stevenson's production must count as the best Coward revival since the National's fabled 'Hay Fever.'

* * *

Probably only minor classics can receive such definitive productions. The National knocked off several of them in its early years; I propose to sidle into a consideration of the Old Vic's Trelawny of the Wells by recalling Pinero's play as it appeared on the same stage in 1965. That production originated at Chichester, whose open stage dictated that the changes of scene from actors' lodgings to West End drawing-room to theatre be carried out, most un-Victorianly, in full view of the audience. So one dramatic world appeared before our eyes as another vanished.

You could see and feel the movement of history; a play that was always charming became, for a few moments, great. An exchange about the inevitable tides of fashion (the new drama will in time be old) became both moving and healing: tragic in fact. And in this context the actors playing actors, striking as individuals, built up into a dazzling solid group.

Without this underpinning the play goes soft and, what is worse, slow; affectionate detail becomes obstructive clutter. In Timothy West's production the first scene, in which star actress Rose Trelawny leaves her fellow actors to become engaged to a swell, suffers particularly; most of the troupers have their moments, but they don't cohere or leave us wanting more. But the play is sparked by Pinero's nostalgia for the theatre of his youth, which we should be made to share. We should be made to love the jargon.

When Rose falls out with her posh prospective in-laws she returns to acting and finds she can no longer do it. Real life, among other rich, has spoiled her for make-believe (just the message for today's progressive drama with its class bias reversed) and a new kind of play must be written for her. Tom Wrench, downtrodden actor (back half of dragon in panto) obliges with a comedy called 'Life' and also reunites her with her fiance, who has gone off to be an actor himself, and who, Tom prophesies, may one day be as good as Kean; which, despite a perfectly presentable performance by the lad by David Shaughnessy, is coming it a bit strong.

This area of the play works better. Robert Lindsay is a possessed propulsive Wrench, heavily scarved and generally suggesting the school swot blossoming into a football hero; and Lynne Miller, despite some vocal gaucheries (a very modern Ms, Miss Miller) has a mystery and a conviction to her; there is a palpable churning within this Rose. Bill Fraser as the stern Vice-Chancellor chunters too mildly, but melts most touchingly. The scene in which he, who was once Kean-struck, discovers the old boy's sword in a trunk and tries out a few lines of Richard III, is infallible. I don't think it's sentimental. (More exactly, I don't care.)

To enjoy The Revolt (New End) you have to keep reminding yourself that it was written in 1870, and is thus a striking anticipation of modern feminism. A wife who has minded her husband's house, son and business for four years, determines to walk out and tells him why in detail. He reacts with equally voluble incomprehension; and after her departure he goes to pieces. But the author, Villiers de L'Isle Adam (best known for having rented Axel's Castle to Edmund Wilson), has a joker to play; the lady returns; their marriage has been a commercial contract, and she is now too irrevocably the businesswoman to break it.

Susan Hampshire gets a tremulous self-loathing into this revelation; Olivier Pierre, dangerously boyish, plays his solo with similar panache. Together they hardly connect, but Simone Benmussa's production (set by herself with great precision, especially in the matter of a wrought-iron hatstand) wants them to. They recite at each other.
