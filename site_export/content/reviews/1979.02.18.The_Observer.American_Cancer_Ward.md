American cancer ward

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a Pulitzer Prize play.

THERE is a perpetual conspiracy in American drama to wring cosiness out of terror.

Soap opera is part of that story; but not all of it: soap of itself has always seemed to me a reasonably healthy phenomenon, since to simplify emotion is not necessarily to anaesthetise it. Soap knows its place. But Hollywood movies on 'important' or 'sensitive' subjects do not. And neither do the plays or novels from which they derive. Michael Cristofer's The Shadow Box was a Broadway success in 1977 and won the Pulitzer Prize. It has now been given its British premiere at the Arts Theatre, Cambridge.

It is about death. Three terminal cancer patients are living in some kind of medically supervised settlement, each in his or her own chalet. There is a restless-minded author, now living with a young male hustler and forced to cope with the sudden reappearance of his wife; There is a contrastingly uncomplicated working man whose wife has so far withheld the truth of his condition from their guitar-playing son. There is a rasping old woman, shunted round in a wheelchair by one daughter and anxious only for news of another. One faces the inevitable with resentment, another with resignation; and the third oscillates between.

Each has quite enough personal and family problems to be going on with, and these are duly aired. Where British drama generally avoids confrontation, American drama seeks it out (this will do as a generalisation about our private lives as well) so there is much hand-to-hand and heart-to-heart.

As domestic drama this is all conventional and innocuous if a bit dull (there being an overwrought gentility about much of the dialogue that recalls the suffering days of Bette Davis), but Mr Cristofer has of course the built-in gimmick of mortality. He has also -- and this is where my reservations harden into hostility -- a narrator. He introduces us to the sufferers; he is not, he insists, a doctor. Or rather: "We're not your doctors," he says, blandly and infuriatingly assuming the role of audience spokesman.

This device is given little scope -- the characters are informed of our presence, blink, and then retire behind their fourth wall -- but it is enough to bestow on the evening the aura of cutely earnest experimentation. Our fugleman sits there, wise and smiling and impotent, but we are back with the worst of Thornton Wilder -- as it might be, "The Skin of Our Town." "Joe," he says, summoning his first specimen, and you would not have thought a writer could sprinkle so much sugar on a monosyllable. At the end, the company, Jobs and comforters together, give up on action and address us on the wisdom and necessity of acceptance. After everything else, Mr Cristofer throws a tone poem at us.

The play has gained in one signal respect since I saw it in New York. The word "cancer" is now actually mentioned. It also has the benefit of a production by Jonathan Lynn which is -- until the closing stages -- livelier and less reverential than the original, and of a magnificently clean-cut performance by Madge Ryan as the senior patient (an easy role in which to be self-indulgent while still attracting good notices). Dick Wilson and Sheila Allen fill the family chalet with its proper measure of sympathy and bafflement.

The writer's menage is the most clumsily contrived and the fullest of reproaches. Anthony Bate is too restrained and trained for it and June Ritchie too emphatic; James Aubrey as the cuckoo in their nest begins splendidly, goes to pieces when the character does, but retrieves himself in time for the final curtain.

* * *

Tom Kempinski's Flashpoint (Mayfair) returns us to another traditional form: the enclosed army melodrama. Those with long enough memories will recall "The Long and the Short and the Tall," especially when confronted by the central character, the tough, foul-mouthed barrack buffoon who turns out to have the right moral instincts. The place is Ulster and the time "next year." Martial law has been imposed, a hutful of squaddies await the morning on which they are to shoot a convicted deserter. Some regard the prospect with indifference, one at least with positive pleasure, and one cracks up. He in effect hijacks the hut, turning the rifle on his mates and demanding the cancellation of the execution as the price of their lives.

Up to this point the play has dragged at comic-documentary level; now it jumps to life and -- despite some repetitious writing -- stays jumping, even treating us to a couple of prize twists at the end. Nothing much is said about the Northern Ireland situation, except that it is probably insoluble; nothing much about the British Army, except that it is as likely to be brutalised by war as any other. To go one further, and deduce a "troops out" message, requires a considerable talent for unassisted extrapolation. (A soldier after all might well baulk at shooting one of his own, whatever the ethics of the conflict.) The actors take full advantage of both the tensions and the humour, Tony Selby romping home in the plum role.

* * *

The Albany Empire is a community theatre in Deptford, which burned to the ground in July after presenting a cabaret show with an anti-racialist slant. "Controversy," say the company, "rages as to the cause." The theatre has now been rebuilt and I wish I could welcome the reopening production. But One Gun Salute, which purports to be a lament for the welfare state, is the dregs of agit-prop. Not that you could accuse it of unfairness to its opponents: it never keeps its eye on any object long enough to find any attitudes at all. Attempting to chronicle the disillusion of a social security official, it never lets you get a look at him, and its choice of surrounding incidents is arbitrary to the point of factitious non-existence. Messy acting, desperate music and a lamely allegorical structure complete the picture.

* * *

Michael Hastings's Full Frontal (Theatre Upstairs), a black African applies for membership of the National Front. His life is unhappy and he needs scapegoats, so his application does have a bizarre logic. He has no time for West Indians and is rather like Mr Hastings's Meadowlark Warner (from "Gloo Joo") in reverse.

But unlike Meadowlark he has no plot, and not very much wit, to keep him going; the piece is a monologue, Mr Hastings having kept the listening Front official off-stage. He has in fact forfeited the boldness of his conception, tacitly acknowledging that though it sounds a brightly provocative notion, it will not stand the test of actual dramatisation. All we get is another hard-luck story, though it's pleasant to hear it delivered by Winston Ntshona, who was once the plumper half of the "Sizwe Bansi" duo, but now seems pretty slim and twice as fluent.
