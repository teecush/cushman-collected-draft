Exchanges

ROBERT CUSHMAN talks to Michael Elliott, whose production of The Family Reunion reopens this week in the West End.

Michael Elliott's production of his near-namesake T. S.'s Family Reunion has now been seen in three open-stage theatres: in the temporary structure that preceded Manchester's Royal Exchange playhouse, in the Exchange itself, and at the Roundhouse in London. This week it transfers to a West End theatre, the Vaudeville, and gets back again behind its proscenium arch.

It is a long-delayed return not only for the play but for the 48-year-old director. Probably no director in the British theatre commands more respect than Elliott, but he has reached his eminence on a surprisingly small body of work.

While The Family Reunion transfers, The Lady from the Sea runs on at the Roundhouse, completing a trio of Elliott productions that began with The Ordeal of Gilbert Pinfold. He insists, though, that the season was planned not as a showcase for his own work, but for that of the Royal Exchange theatre which has been open since 1976, though its prehistory stretches back to the early fifties.

It is the story of a group, one whose longevity and stability makes even the RSC directorate look tentative. It began in the post-war Old Vic School, run by Michel Saint-Denis, Glen Byam Shaw and George Devine. Among its graduates were a Finnish director, Casper Wrede, an Italian designer Richard Negri, and an anglicised American actor James Maxwell. Elliott himself ('half-English, half-Scots') met them in 1954, after being at Oxford. He and Wrede co-directed a number of plays for BBC Television. He also married (as did Wrede and Maxwell) an actress-member of the group, in his case Rosalind Knight.

The philosophy that held them all together ('through bitter and awful times') set them apart from the values that were already crystallising at the Royal Court and that have dominated the British theatre--including the National and the RSC--ever since. 'I've never believed,' says Elliott, 'that theatre was at its best when political, and I don't believe that social problems are its sole concern, though they are certainly a legitimate one. The prime concern of drama is to show that life has a larger dimension than that of ordinary reality.'

In 1959 a season of plays was staged at the Lyric Theatre, Hammersmith, by a group calling itself the 59 Theatre Company. Wrede was the director, Elliott his associate, and the actors included some of the best and solidest young talent in London. The (unsubsidised) repertoire was phenomenal. Wrede offered the first English production of Buchner's Danton's Death. Elliott, directing his first professional production, tackled Ibsen's supposedly unstageable Brand, and brought it triumphantly off, with Patrick McGoohan in the title role. At least two schoolboy members of the audience found the season indelibly influential. I was one; the other was Braham Murray, now the Royal Exchange's administrative head.

Elliott's next stage production was in 1961: the magical As You Like It that dominated Peter Hall's troubled second season at Stratford. The Rosalind was Vanessa Redgrave, still his leading lady at the Roundhouse and naming him unhesitatingly as her favourite director.

It was, though, the only production Elliott did for them. He left to run the Old Vic for what proved to be its last season before the National Theatre took over. He reassembled many of the 59 team and led off with a Peer Gynt to match his Brand. The logical sequence would have been for him to stay at the Vic as Olivier's lieutenant. This indeed was the plan and Elliott went along with it for four or five months. But I got more and more unhappy. 'I was only 31; I thought I should live more dangerously. So I went into a kind of exile, directing in television, and writing film scripts.'

It was a long exile; the curious thing is that though he had only six London productions behind him, nobody forgot him. He acquired a mythical reputation as a turner-down of key positions. Devine once wanted him to take over the Royal Court; there was a high-level offer from the BBC. 'I didn't turn them down out of Puritanism. They wouldn't have been the real thing for me.'

The real thing came along in 1967, when he directed Tom Courtenay in The Playboy of the Western World in Manchester. From this came the 69 Theatre Company, operating in a temporary home on the university campus, and from that the Royal Exchange. 'In Manchester we're free. We can be a community theatre with a loyal audience; an average 90 per cent of capacity. And we're far enough from London for actors only to come to us for the right reasons.'

My main criticism of the Exchange has been that, below the star line, there have been too many pallid performances. Elliott's defence is that 'one has a reaction against a certain kind of successful acting. We're going for essences, and maybe give that too much priority over technical skill.'

There is one other factor in the Elliott story: his health. In May of 1976, with the opening of the Royal Exchange four months away, he collapsed and was put on a kidney-machine. 'I was born with the condition though I hadn't known it.' He now spends three nights a week on the machine; but he emphasises he can still live 'an absolutely full life.' In fact the period since last autumn has been his fullest ever as a director of plays: 'I'll have had seven openings.'

All the same it's a mournful thing to have happened to him just as he was finally coming into his kingdom. It's ironic too in view of his continuing fascination with The Family Reunion. He identified with its theme of inheritance having had a successful preacher-father (known pre-war as 'the radio parson') to live down or live up to, and his copy of the play, acquired at Oxford, is now scored with the notes for five different productions.

'It's too difficult, its greatest fault is its obscurity, but that doesn't matter, because theatre is really emotional. It kicks you in the guts. It's a great play.'
