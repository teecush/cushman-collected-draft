Second time round

CONSIDER trilogies, and the authors who beget them. Think of Aeschylus, Shakespeare, O'Neill, Arnold Wesker. Now stop frowning and consider Alan Ayckbourn, the latest and, no contest, the funniest of the drama's triple threats.

Most trilogists devote their three evenings to the telling of a solemn story in chronological sequence. Mr Ayckbourn tells an hilarious story (his may not be the only comic trilogy in existence but at the moment it pleases me to think so) three times over. Each play takes us through the same weekend in the same troubled house, inhabited by the same six characters. All that changes is the room. Table Manners, which opened a fortnight ago at Greenwich, takes us into the dining-room. Very soon we are to go Round and Round the Garden; last week's instalment was called Living Together, from which you may draw your own conclusions.

The plays interlock. "They can be seen," says the author kindly, "in any order since, like a three-ended ball of string, it doesn't really matter where you start to unravel." Ever since reading that sentence in the programme I have been torturing myself trying to imagine a three-ended ball of string. The experience of watching the play is, however, quite painless.

It may even be more painless than the author intended. Sprinkled through the first two plays (and, for all I know, the third as well) are references to the unseen presence who rules the household--a selfishly sentimental bedridden matriarch. Somehow I could never believe in her existence, and my scepticism about her spread outwards to embrace all the darker aspects of Mr Ayckbourn's menage. Like John Hopkins in Next of Kin, he shows us a discordant set of brothers and sisters, husbands and wives. And my first response to him was to give thanks for a dramatist who could insinuate that family life could be hell rather than brazenly proclaiming it. But by the end of his second play I had changed my mind.

Mr Hopkins, however laboured and repetitious, writes like someone who believes what he is saying and has probably lived it; Mr Ayckbourn sounds his bass notes as if fulfilling an obligation. His dialogue is weightless; this does not make him a frivolous writer, but it does limit his capacity to convey pain. He is an unexcelled farcical architect; no other current writer could have invented this cycle, let alone ridden it. He has--this is a compliment--the most acutely mechanical mind of any dramatist since Feydeau; but unlike Feydeau, who could casually annihilate a character or a relationship in a line, he is not a natural misanthrope.

Mr Ayckbourn has time, and even charity, for all his characters: even for the bossy and beady-eyed lady played here (to perfection) by Penelope Keith, busying herself uninvited with other people's problems as a desperate means of avoiding facing her own. Asked pointblank whether she is happy she comes brightly back with "Yes, mostly"; amends that to "occasionally"; and finally settles for "now and then." I look forward to re-encountering Miss Keith in the garden though, not--except in terms of plot--in hopes of learning more about her. I want her to stay as she is. And I would be heartbroken were there to be any change at all in Michael Gambon, who plays a country vet, considerably more honest than the day is long and possessed of a sound but slow and excessively literal mind. He is all pause. As late as the last scene of the second play he is still wringing tears of laughter from us while he carefully considers someone's jocular suggestion that, by having made an unexpected entrance through the French windows, he might be a cat-burglar. He finally denies the charge but it is obviously an infinitely reasoned decision.

Gambon is not one of the family. He is on hand as the inactive wooer of Felicity Kendal, who is in danger of being left on the shelf. Though she looks dishevelled and keeps her hands plunged deep in the pockets of a pair of battered jeans, Miss Kendal cannot make this seem a likely prospect. Tom Courtenay, too, is a touch glamorous for his role. He is meant to be an assistant librarian, which may be a case of Mr Ayckbourn's whimsy getting the better of him, and might well benefit from a spot of character-development in the open air. At the moment his personality, meant to be pivotal, is ill-defined.

His name is Norman and the overall title of the series is The Norman Conquests.

* * *

This, since it sounds historical, is the best lead-in I can contrive to The Great Society (Mermaid), which is historical. It tells of how Sir Pierce of Exton broke into Richard II's cell and instead of immediately clobbering him, as in Shakespeare, tried instead to bore him to death. This he does, in Beverley Cross's play, by recalling the events of the Peasants' Revolt in which they had both been concerned 20 years before. The audience gets little out of this except a steady dosage of possibly useful information about the Middle Ages; the actors of Richard and Exton get less, being compelled to give lantern lectures when they obviously want to play at murderer and victim. Richard is allowed to roll about a bit, to denote fear.

* * *

The story and characterisation of Jennifer Phillips's Bodywork (Hampstead) are tenuous in a good light and non-existent in a bad. But it has thematic unity, denoted by the title. In the first act a girl, on a premarital Mediterranean fling, drops her bra as a come-on for any passing male; in the second a young man is dared into posing in the nude for a couple of girl painters, and feels very uncomfortable; in the third the female body is stretched out as a receptacle for impersonal impregnation, but is rescued just in time. The play leaves the slightest of aftertastes, but there is wit in it.
