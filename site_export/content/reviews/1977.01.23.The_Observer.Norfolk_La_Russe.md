Norfolk à la Russe

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Leeds, Manchester and London.

Years ago Arnold Wesker gave us Roots, in which a Norfolk farm working family prepared to feed and welcome a Jewish intellectual who at the last moment chickened out and remained offstage. This was prudent of him on the evidence of The Wedding Feast (Leeds Playhouse).

The parallel with Roots is not exact. The Jew here is a tycoon named Louis Litvinov, very big in shoes; and he is not an expected guest but a gatecrasher. Two workers in his factory - one his secretary's brother - are getting married and he decides to grace the celebration. He is an affectionate employer who claims, more than once, to understand his employees; after all, he started poor himself. He is accused inevitably of paternalism; his reply, "Give everything a name and dismiss it," though not conclusive, is worth a ponder. It rebukes the jargon that is everywhere flung about in place of argument. The language in this play is thoughtful, judicious and finely shaped.

Structurally, however, there are severe flaws. The first act, mainly devoted to filling us in on Louis, is a disaster area and may well have scared managements off. The play is based (how distantly I have no idea) on a Dostoevsky short story, and a narrator, carefully setting the scene à la Russe (the country cottage on the outskirts of Norwich in the county of Norfolk), pays homage to the source. He does most of the preliminary talking, putting such a fine ironic gloss on the conversation of Louis, his colleagues, and his political adversaries to Left and Right, that the arguments become impossible to follow and the attitudes to comprehend. Louis accordingly walks into the central situation a hollow man.

The mannerisms are dropped for the wedding party itself, which extends over Acts Two and Three and constitute a naturalistic tour de force unapproached (indeed, unattempted) by any other recent playwright. The Leeds production by John Harrison and Michael Attenborough stretches its actors across one of those panoramic subsidised stages in all the awkwardness, acrimony and erratic euphoria of actual family festivity, culminating in something called "the shoe game," where Louis's employees, after some understandable initial nervousness, take the opportunity of beating the hell out of him with his own product. This use of ritual is the most successful, believable and suggestive in any of Mr Wesker's plays.

The family, then, are thoroughly convincing (David Troughton is an outstandingly sheepish bridegroom), but Louis himself strains belief, and his secretary annihilates it. She has a lot of work to do - linking two worlds and, as a Marxist who condemns Louis's attempts at rapprochement because they make class hatred harder, representing a third - and no personality to do it on. David Swift plays Louis decently enough within the bounds of the text.

* * *

Across the Pennines, the Manchester Royal Exchange Theatre has two productions in repertory. Orton's What The Butler Saw is far better done than on either of its appearances in London and two of the performances may be definitive: Trevor Peacock's immovable policeman and Michael Feast's extremely movable page-boy, in and out of women's clothing (in every sense) at a second's notice.

Lee Montague as Dr Prentice radiates the professional psychiatric calm that is unnerving. The play, though, rests on his superior Dr Rance, Inspector of Madhouses, dedicated to the discovery and exploitation of mental and physical deviation. Alfred Burke has some joyous moments, sniffing out enormities like the devil in pin-stripes, but he has not the panache to blend the various obsessions - censorious and libidinous, homo and hetero - wished on him by his author.

The play drives against society by mocking orthodox cases of sexuality; intellectually and imaginatively, it is of a piece, but the methods of attack are inclined to be indiscriminate. Mrs Prentice is lustful or repressed, predatress or victim, as Orton's mood takes him. Rosalind Knight, a witty actress with the law of averages and her own carving-knife voice working for her, opts for attack. Braham Murray's direction, though lacking something in frenzy, is well spaced; and a seat above the arena stage affords a god-like view of the human, and particularly the English, race making a delectable fool of itself.

* * *

For The Skin of Our Teeth I sat below and since this allowed me to be chatted up by the no less delectable Marsha Hunt on one of her exits, I cannot complain. Miss Hunt, though, complains a lot; as Sabina, the maid whom everybody ought to have, she complains about her domestic circumstances; but in her own person she grumbles about playing a role which requires her to step out of character and grumble about playing a role.

Thornton Wilder's self-proclaimed "history of the world in comic strip" presents mankind as a plain old family from New Jersey, successively surviving the Ice Age, the Flood, and War. It thus asserts (a) that people never change and (b) that they always get by. Put like that it seems rather cosy and like that, much of the time, Wilder puts it. Only in the third act is there real tension as Mr Antrobus, alias Adam, the builder, confronts his son Henry, whom you may call Cain if you dare. Lee Montague, still powerfully quiet, meets Michael Feast, a riveting young actor whose warrior is as dangerous as his page was perky. But to enjoy this scene you have to survive, not only the deluge (here unaccountably provoked by some perfectly decorous sunbathing on the boardwalk at Atlantic City), but the author's jokiness, bred ostensibly of his desire to remind us that we are in the theatre.

There is a nineteen-fortyish Middle American folksiness here beyond that of the Antrobi. There are stabbing moments, mainly connected with Mr Feast's destructiveness, and the direction by Richard Negri and James Maxwell capitalises on all the opportunities for spectacle. The theatre continues to excite, and the company to charm, but I still hope for more muscle.

* * *

Terence Rattigan's Separate Tables (Apollo) was the last West End prestige hit before the Royal Court era; the second of its two component parts has always been thought superior to the first, but time has narrowed the gap. The sadomasochistic couple of the first have good Strindbergian stuff in them, though their story is told with surprising clumsiness. The repressed pair of the second have compassion and Rattigan's best technique on their side, but their problems now seem enviably cut and dried.

The casting of this revival is not ideal; John Mills, that perpetual cock-sparrow, has the spirit but not the strength for his first role. Into the military posing of his second he slips almost too easily, while Jill Bennett, his partner in frustration, having dispensed virginal mannerisms with excessive generosity for her first 15 minutes, forgets them thereafter - perhaps because her mother (Margaret Courtney) is too comic-opera a monster to furnish convincing opposition. The small-hotel ambiance is as infallibly beguiling as ever; the supporting roles are all what are known as "little gifts" to their interpreters, who receive them graciously but with an undeniable air of self-satisfaction as each little laugh goes down. Raymond Huntley's schoolmaster is the most discreet, and hence the most durable.
