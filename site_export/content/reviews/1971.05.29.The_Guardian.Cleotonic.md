Cleotonic

MRS JOHN DANKWORTH talks to ROBERT CUSHMAN about her blossoming career

CLEO LAINE opened her act at the Ronnie Scott club this year with "On a Clear Day You Can See Forever," as overexposed a song as you could hope not to hear again. By the time the first chorus had been swung I could have written a letter of apology to Burton Lane, the fine and underrated composer who penned it. The song belongs in his collected works after all.

It is customary for singers to make the initial grab for their audience's attention with an up-tempo number. But Cleo perhaps needs it less than most since she customarily follows half a dozen sonorous complex instrumentals from whatever group her husband is currently fronting (this time it's the John Dankworth Tenfold--"because there are ten in the band and when the gig is over they fold"--R. Scott). I don't think many sleep while they are on, and I have long doubted the infallibility of the traditional opening belter. Even the great Peggy Lee failed to bring it off when she sang at the Albert Hall last year. So Miss Laine had a double set of my prejudices to overcome. But she made it. Hers was the clearest day ever embellished in the second chorus with additional lyrics that would, I suspected, have been as surprising to Alan Jay Lerner as they were to me.

Backstage between sets the lady confirmed my suspicions. "I ad-lib on the melody when working on a song. JD writes it down if he thinks it's any good, and adds lyrics to fit what I've sung."

"Clear Day" might reasonably be described as a modern standard. Traditionally, Cleo includes a number of the more ancient ones in her repertory as well, but they are no longer the staples they once were. She firmly states that "I still like the things I've always done," but also admits that "I may have drained them dry. I try when I sing standards to present them in a different light but there does come a moment when every song you sing starts to resemble every other song."

This is partly true of some of the oldies she does, but only partly. She gets exhilarating mileage out of a piece of vintage Cole Porter euphoria, "Ridin' High," and "I'm Gonna Sit Right Down and Write Myself a Letter" now belongs to her as inalienably as ever it did to Fats Waller. She and Dankworth have been trading opening phrases on this for as long as I remember, and the routine has now taken on an aspect of self-parody which actually augments its joyousness. On the other hand she finds little fresh to do for "I Could Write a Book." I think it might be better slower since it depends, like many of Lorenz Hart's lyrics, on self-mockery, and no vocalist can project this better than Cleo when she gives herself the breathing space. And though I hope there will always be some Gershwin in her performances she curiously fails to endow "But Not for Me" with its proper razor edge. But then I don't think I've heard anybody get it really right.

She started her career as a low contralto with a very sinewy range. She can now do upper register acrobatics that challenge Ella Fitzgerald's. Some of the credit goes to Dankworth who would take her arrangements progressively up half a tone without telling her, so that she had to sing higher to survive. "John hates to hear a run of songs in one key--of course it presupposes that you can sing in more than one." There is a danger, which she recognises, of doing vocal acrobatics for their own sake, but she now wants to sing as simply as she can: "I've done my soaring. The old devil still breaks out sometimes as in 'Clear Day' to delightful effect."

But she's more satisfying when she concentrates on delivering lyrics. Her current showpiece is Noel Coward's "Mad About the Boy." I've always thought it his best song, but I hardly knew the half of it. Cleo has restored the full text, sung in three voices--a sophisticate (giving the familiar words her most longing and languorous treatment), a schoolgirl ("I'm sure that Housman wrote the 'Shropshire Lad' about the boy") and a char. At another extreme, she stills the room with Joni Mitchell's "Both Sides Now." Everybody has sung this one but Cleo's control is consummate. She can half-talk a few lines and suddenly come down true and moving on a last note you hardly hoped to hear. And I treasure her frequent quizzical moods--a slightly aggrieved stressing "Why should I have spring fever" in "It Might as Well be Spring" and a sense of wonder carried through to an enraptured top note at the end of Michel Legrand's beautifully simple and dauntingly complex "What Are You Doing The Rest Of Your Life."

If you missed her at Ronnie Scott's you could try the Wavendon All Music Plan. Wavendon is the Dankworths' Buckinghamshire home, and home also of a festival (this year May 28-June 6) where advanced students can meet other musicians, in different spheres, talk, play each other's music, be taught by each other. Musicians should know a good deal more of other kinds of music--they can't afford to specialise.

She practises what she preaches--as everybody knows, she can now sing just about anything, including John Donne (actually, it may be just the Perry Como associations, but "Go and Catch a Falling Star" has always seemed to me like a natural song-lyric anyway. It now has a Dankworth setting). She also, from time to time, acts; last year she played Hedda Gabler at Canterbury "and I don't think I made a fool of myself." Later this year she turns to musicals as the mulatto Julie ("At least I'm ethnically right for the part") in a West End revival of "Showboat."

For her closer at Scott's she revived "It's a Pity to Say Goodnight," complete, as they say, with verse, and with a coda--"If you gotta go home, you gotta go home"--mischievously repeated as she gradually retreated from the mike and the stage. When she reached the final "Give me a good-night kiss" there could have been few in the audience who would have failed to oblige. By the end she had converted the room into a four-sided commune: herself, John Dankworth and company, Ronnie Scott, and the audience. I left feeling surpassingly happy.
