Strangulating the vowels

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Decadence,' 'Into Europe,' 'Hosanna' and 'Angels of War.'

SOME readers have complained that I was too violent in declaring that anyone thinking The Hypochondriac Moliere's greatest play should be locked up. I'm sorry. I ought of course to have said that anyone thinking The Hypochondriac Moliere's best play should see a doctor.

Television viewers will know that the upper classes have been getting a good deal recently. Some incidental criticisms -- implicit or overt -- of their manners or habits by Evelyn Waugh, Trevor Griffiths, and whoever writes The Borgias -- but not enough to dislodge the traditional dramatic picture of them as people who say witty things while leading active and fascinating sex lives in attractive surroundings.

I was sure that Steven Berkoff -- as author, star and director of Decadence (Arts) -- would not fall into this trap. Nor has he. When his characters are working-class, as in East, Mr Berkoff will occasionally lend them his own self-pity, but that is as far as he usually goes with affection. There are two proles in Decadence and they are credited with all the grace and delicacy of Arthur Mullard.

Mr Berkoff and Linda Marlowe, who appear briefly in these roles, also play the more important, and only other, roles in the piece: a toff and his mistress. "Decadence," says Mr Berkoff in a programme note, "is a study of the ruling classes or upper classes, so called by virtue of strangulated vowel tones rather than any real achievement." Both the syntax and the logic of that sentence are hard to follow, but the emotional import is clear enough.

In order to function in the theatre Mr Berkoff needs a hate-object, so he has invented one. He has taken Bertie Wooster in full soup-and-fish, endowed him with genitals and a mouthful of dirty words and treated him as if he were (a) real and (b) a social menace. All the fellow actually does is talk a lot, screw his girl-friend, go to the theatre, and then proceed to a restaurant where he gets horribly sick, thereby hurting no-one but himself.

Mr Griffiths's TV play Country was far shrewder when it showed the brewery nobs adapting to progress by investing heavily in fizzy beer, even if it meant turning a revolutionary analysis into a commercial for CAMRA.

(A parenthesis on Griffiths before going back to Berkoff. He has said, "my plays are never about the battle between socialism and capitalism, I take that as being decisively won by socialism." But that can leave him with surprisingly little to write about. Linking the brewing Carlion dynasty to the Corleones of The Godfather didn't work. In the first place The Godfather is too recent a work to have acquired mythic associations. In the second, Francis Coppola's film had brilliantly likened the Mafia to "legitimate" business families, so there seemed little point now in likening a legitimate business family to the Mafia. The play, in Richard Eyre's direction, was still very watchable; and the same team's Cherry Orchard the previous week was excellent, though not as different from other excellent Cherry Orchards as they seemed to think.)

Decadence shows Mr Berkoff as a performer at his best. The strangulated vowels are funny, the timing is fine; the body twists itself as ever into remarkable contortions, and the teeth are the most expressive in London. Miss Marlowe, playing with and up to him, makes no mistakes. But it shows Mr Berkoff as a writer at his worst, dishing out excruciating rhyming couplets. ("We mustn't be late/you mustn't dip your spoon into the cup of hate"; "you are no further, no, in the exploit/than when I first discovered my painful plight.") Mr Berkoff regularly claims to be taking us beyond static/literary/bourgeois/West End theatre, but here we have a show that is totally hung up on its own text, and whose visuals amount to two fashionably dressed people on a sofa. This is the first time Mr Berkoff has been boring.

* * *

More overwrought overwriting in Ron Hutchinson's Into Europe (Lyric Studio). Here too there is verse, delivered as inter-scene commentary, in the manner of David Edgar's Destiny. Mr Edgar was writing about Fascism. Mr Hutchinson has the smaller subject of football, and the disparity grates still further in the dialogue, whose style is toughness-in-aspic.

The leading character is a groin-kicking, press-bribing manager who is losing his touch and whose directors want to get him out. Mr Hutchinson is a talented writer (The Irish Play was a lot more than its title), but this time I suspect he has come up with something unactable. Some of the cast patter delicately through the text as if expecting it to give way beneath them; others, including such good people as Philip Jackson and Stephanie Fayerman, go at it bull-headed. They topple over, but they may have the right instincts. In the play's best speech the departing hero proclaims, "the con is everything -- making sure they never get your number." The bravado spirit ("please God don't let it be this week I get found out") may or may not be right for football, but nothing else can keep you going in the theatre.

* * *

There have been first nights at both Half Moons. At the New, Michel Tremblay's Hosanna, allowing us to see an early, limited work by a playwright who has already moved on. This is his diploma duet piece: some enjoyable bitching between a gay couple in Montreal followed by the obligatory sad soliloquy for one of them, in this case a transvestite who went to a party dressed as Elizabeth Taylor and ran into stiff and humiliating competition. Jim Hooper plays him shrilly, bravely and effectively. His bulky biker lover is played by Ian Gelder, who manages to sound really French-Canadian in spots.

The Old Half Moon offers shelter to Mrs Worthington's Daughters, who patrol the country exhuming plays written by women. This one, Angels of War, written in 1935 by Muriel Box, concerns female ambulance drivers in France in 1918. They go heartily to seed, swearing and smoking, sneaking out of the dorm and standing by each other under fire. But real emotion often breaks through, and there is a pacifist ending that now seems positively mordant.

The main problems are that, like many well-made plays of its period, it devotes too much time to a single small incident, and that you can practically see the dialogue coming off the pages of the Samuel French Acting Edition. A couple of performances are strictly Amateur Dramatic, but there is sturdy work from Jill Stanford, aristocratic and shell-shocked, and from Cordelia Ditton bringing a tart touch of the fringe, both Celtic and theatrical.
