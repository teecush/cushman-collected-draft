Caribbean cocktail

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

On a beach in Trinidad, Creator, a master calypsonian, and his apprentice, Bird, are trying to complete a song for the annual calypso competition. Creator is a three-time winner in this contest but his victories were years ago. That he is on the beach now is no accident. Every time a tourist passes (somewhere in the front stalls presumably) the two men leap to their feet and begin 'working for the Yankee dollar.'

That line is the refrain of the calypso that gives its name to Mustapha Matura's play: Rum An Coca-Cola (Royal Court). It forms half of their busking repertoire. The other half is 'Mary Anne' which Creator describes with justified scorn as the only calypso most tourists have ever heard of. For tourist, in Creator's vocabulary, read always mug; he hates them, he hates himself for his dependence on them, and his two standard songs, both obliquely concerned with prostitution, encapsulate the play.

For his current number Creator is trying out some broader double-entendres; it concerns a maid who finds her mistress in bed with the Minister of Works and Communications: 'the works were working and the communications were communicating.' Before they get that far, however, Bird and more particularly Creator go through agonies getting their first couplet right. They run through various adverbial possibilities for Ruby's execution of the housework: 'nicely,' 'tidily,' 'like a lady'; none of them sound to me like good rhymes for Ruby but then calypso is not my style.

In a few minutes, though, Mr Matura had me fascinated; this is the only play I have seen which successfully dramatises the writing process. 'You don't think "decide" is too big a word?' Bird asks at one point, and Creator replies authoritatively that it's a good word; 'decided' would be too big.

The minutiae of other people's lives are not always as fascinating as the Court would have us believe, but song-writers I could watch for hours. Mr Matura complements this scene with another in which we find the couple performing at a night-club. Creator sings a song by a successful rival and realises that it is beyond anything he could write. His relationship with Bird, always uneasy, deteriorates; Bird belongs to the younger generation who have either destroyed Creator's art or are indifferent to it, and he also lacks Creator's freebooting attitude towards vacationers -- more particularly towards women vacationers. He can take his position as a stud (a black musician, how can he lose?) both more seriously and more easily than Creator would wish him to. He takes up with a girl whom Creator eventually robs and murders.

This part of the play has met with cries of disbelief, but I see no reason to echo them. The character of Creator is of a piece, especially as played by Norman Beaton: spectrally smart, charming, cracked, and desperate. 'I'm gonna go on singing calypso' he cries with his arms outstretched; when he draws them in again, the glorious gesture contracts into a defeated shrug. Jocelyn Herbert's set makes beauty out of a couple of inclined planes; the director is Donald Howarth, himself a dramatist of proven wit, feeling and invention, the qualities that continue to distinguish Mr Matura's writing.

* * *

Susan Miller's Flux is the latest in a season of American plays at Square One. It concerns a college instructor trying to teach her class through love. If that sounds sticky I can only refer you to the saltiness of much of the writing, the literalness of the heroine's methods (she writes love-notes on her pupils' compositions when she approves them, and goes to bed with the beefiest 19-year-old, which certainly puts her a few jumps ahead of Jean Brodie) and her final realisation that passion, particularly across generations, is forever out of step.

Back home, waiting with increasing impatience, is a lesbian lover whom Jane Hallaren places in the great American tradition of joking bitterness. Robyn Goodman's heroine wavers more in detail, but has the right indomitable outline. Miss Miller takes technical risks whose success is difficult to judge in Annabel Leventon's hole-and-corner production; it is frequently difficult to tell what is going on. The straightforward bitching or teaching sessions work best; choric interludes and dream sequences worst. There is more snap in the dialogue than in its delivery.

* * *

For the second time in two weeks Goldoni goes down. The Artful Widow (Greenwich) is a slight improvement on the National's Il Campiello, mainly by virtue of one scene in which John Kane's Arlecchino furnishes instant interpretations of two misdelivered letters; here at last is the comic set-piece for which both evenings have been straining. Mr Kane's verve is helpful throughout; Greenwich indeed has a good cast or, not quite the same thing, a cast of good actors. Ursula Mohan's French maid is difficult to resist (though I tried) and Diane Cilento and Frank Barrie, in face of a determinedly uproarious production, are a cool pair of lovers whom one is glad to see finally united; believable Italians in everything except their gratuitous accents.

Venice, updated to what seems to be the 1920s, is full of funny voices; Miss Cilento's other suitors are a Frenchman, an Englishman and (substituted for Goldoni's Spaniard) an American, and the evening is a parade of tired nationalist jokes, applied without any apparent forethought; the American, for example, going a-wooing disguised inexplicably as Groucho Marx, gets a laugh for his make-up and nothing thereafter. It all makes nonsense of the play; if Miss Cilento's foreign lovers are obvious buffoons, she deserves no credit for seeing through them. The production, unconcerned with such things, relegates Goldoni to the gag-writer's position from which he spent his career escaping; judged on a purely statistical basis, it hits far less often than it misses.
