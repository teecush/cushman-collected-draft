The hypnotic Miss Horne

ROBERT CUSHMAN reports on a recent visit to Broadway.

THE most beautiful woman in the world walks on to the stage, dazzling, in a white dress and gold necklace. The overture has been long--all her hits strung together--and we have had plenty of time to ponder and anticipate her entrance. An arm-waving march or a sinuous slink? In fact she just steps on from the side, downstage; it's so uncomplicated (not unassuming) it dazes you.

Lena Horne: The Lady and Her Music is the event of the Broadway season, a club act miraculously blown up to theatre size without bursting. It is, in fact, an extension of the routine Miss Horne has been doing for the last couple of years. She sings, dances a little and talks quite a lot. The chat now gets official status as "book"--for which she is given sole programme credit--so I suppose this counts as a musical.

At 63 she has a solid quantity of past to go over. Her characteristic gait is a rapid prowl, and her words have the same rhythm. They are delivered, often, in a tone of amused anger, especially when she comes to recall her wartime days as Hollywood's first black sex-symbol: not black enough, the bosses decided, so they devised a special make-up for her, afterwards passed on to Ava Gardner when she assumed the role Miss Horne should have played in Show Boat.

She gets away with much that I could not forgive in other performers: with telling the audience how wonderful and important they are and how excited and enthusiastic she is, with singing inspirational songs called "If You Believe" and "That's What Miracles Are All About," with gospel ornamentations of purely secular tunes. There is an uninterrupted flow of passion in her performance that carries all the mannerisms on its back. The prize is the way she can revivify a familiar song by striking directly at its heart: raw, full-throated anguish for "Stormy Weather," dreamy erotic lyricism for "The Surrey With the Fringe On Top," done with only a rhythm section though she was so rapt it took me ages to realise this.

She had noticed that this is really a song about hypnosis, and hypnotism--of herself too--is what she deals in. Between numbers she shakes her head sagely from side to side, with a burning glint in her eyes. It is as if we were not merely her subjects but her creations. That skin-tone MGM commissioned for her was called "light Egyptian," and Mr Factor may have had something of a point; who else could Cleopatra have looked or sounded like?

* * *

Increasingly on Broadway a show runs for a week or for ever. The latest candidate for eternity is Sophisticated Ladies, a nostalgia show like Sugar Babies or 42nd Street, but superior to either of them. It starts with the advantage of Duke Ellington's music; the show was planned as a biographical tribute, but the biography was jettisoned during a calamitous try-out tour. Michael Smuin, whose background is in ballet rather than musicals, was called in and saved the production by turning it into non-stop dance.

The routines would probably be just as exhilarating done to anyone else's tunes, though they reflect one side of Ellington--the raffish, Harlem dandy side--pretty well. The band (under the nominal direction of the Duke's son, Mercer) are irresistible from the first note, and the show strikes a pleasing blow against racism; that is, the white hoofers are as good as the black. The only problem is that, with no story and with every number clearly promising to be immaculate, the evening runs short on surprise.

* * *

It is still unusual for a show to open in the summer; though air-conditioning and tourism combine to sustain the existing hits. The only book (i.e. narrative) musical to achieve any success this past season is Woman of the Year, which has won a few awards and a fair amount of scorn. I can see why; but I had a better time at it than I had anticipated.

Based on a Tracy-Hepburn film, the show has been fashioned as a vehicle for Lauren Bacall, and aims apparently at keeping as close as possible to her previous hit, Applause. Again she is a successful woman who finds that love counts for more than career; or rather, this being 1981 after all, that love counts for just as much as career. Again there is a big number about how popular she is, with the chorus dancing round her, occasionally lifting her up and brandishing her to make it look as if she can dance too.

This time that idea misfires, accenting her limitations rather than concealing them. Her acting lacks range (every line comes out the same) and so, notoriously, does her singing, but this doesn't matter; she makes an attractive, casually sardonic noise, and she communicates an affable, unfazed enthusiasm, to which the house naturally rises. The show is machine-made, staged with expertise rather than inspiration, but it has a better than average score; one of its attractions is that John Kander and Fred Ebb (who also composed Chicago) do not spend the evening trying to prove how tough they are.

* * *

There are no new plays on Broadway (though Lanford Wilson's Fifth of July, which I saw and liked on the West Coast two years ago, has found itself a home) and the most praised piece off Broadway, a three-hander about love and cycling called Key Exchange, is only a nice trifle. Down on Theatre Row (a new development on the far side of the porno district in Forty-second Street) something stirs; something that was once called Four Jews in a Room Bitching is now named March of the Falsettos.

Thus it has two title-numbers; both are very niftily staged though hard to relate to the rest of the action. This concerns Marvin (intended, I think, to be an archetypal name), who leaves his wife and son for another man, but wants to hang on to all of them. Serene chess-playing child ("my father's a homo--my mother's not thrilled") is contrasted with nerve-baring adults.

The author, William Finn, writes effective lines rather than whole lyrics, motifs rather than melodies; but he is on the way to establishing characters and--especially--relationships through song; and in a theatre largely fixated on its insubstantial past that is a hopeful sign.
