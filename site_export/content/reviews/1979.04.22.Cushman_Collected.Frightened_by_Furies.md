Frightened by Furies

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Stratford.

FEW directors these days write programme-notes, but for his production of The Family Reunion (Roundhouse) Michael Elliott--sensitive, articulate and embattled--has broken the rule.

He tells us that the only hope of realising the spiritual dimension of T. S. Eliot's 40-year-old play is to make its realistic ground-base as solid as possible. He hints that his playwright has not given him too much help here and that the passage of time has compounded the inadequacy, making us unwilling to take at all seriously the problems of guilt-racked, verse-spouting landed gentry or of their baffled, dependent relatives.

Even two years ago that latter objection might have held, but we are now just far enough away from the inhabitants of Eliot's Wishwood to re-extend them our sympathy; compared to the unholy abstractions more recent playwrights have expected us to believe in, they are flesh-and-blood itself. That nervous chorus of uncles and aunts was always well observed; perhaps too well--the author seems at times to be pinning them down under glass. One of the chief merits of Mr Elliott's production is that he adds warmth to accuracy, but author and audience are more on his side here than he admits.

His major contention passes unchallenged; nothing in the theatre can work symbolically or metaphorically unless it works concretely first. In this production that principle is carried to the extremest point. In picture-stage versions, the Eumenides--the Furies who according to classical precedent turn to "bright angels"--apparently lurked as embarrassing shadows outside the drawing-room window. Mr Elliott, working in the round, brings them on, to the accompaniment of nerve-wrenching music, as a circle of towering Klansmen. I was scared out of my life.

His direction is in fact superbly tuned throughout. Each area of the play is clearly demarcated, and varied within itself; thus the choral quartet includes virtuoso solos by Constance Chapman and Jeffry Wickham, matched by the kindly intrusion of Esmond Knight's doctor. Combining rigour and humanity, Mr Elliott makes you recognise how much sloppy approximation we are accustomed to accept in the theatre.

However, the play's central problem remains Harry the haunted hero. He believes for most of the play that he has murdered his wife, then comes to realise that he is bearing the burden of his late father who wanted to kill his mother. This uncannily subtle form of guilt (for somebody else's intention only) is difficult to express with dramatic precision; maybe an actor less craggy and more malleable than Edward Fox (who looks authentically racked and sounds like a young uptight Ralph Richardson) might bring it off, but there is no way of disguising the imbalance.

Harry turns out to be a vessel, rather than a figure, of tragedy; the real agony resides with his mother, who has hitherto been kept off-centre and is now dragged forward for a couple of climaxes (one her off-stage death) that only an actress of genius could manage so suddenly; Pauline Jameson supplies talent and a kind of willed hauteur.

But the whole play is dealt to us at one remove; mother and son are even denied a confrontation. Harry's prototype, Orestes, may have grown up in a disturbed household, but he had solid sins of his own to expiate; the Greek and Christian worlds, though equally harsh, do not mesh. Not ethically at any rate; the play does have an atmospheric unity, and this the production brilliantly captures, not just with its technical shocks, but with its careful attention to Eliot's equally careful imagery.

* * *

This may be mannered in its catalogues of bleak domestic horrors (can any poet ever have been so definite about his articles?), but even the mannerism is now effective. Thirties Gothic (the world of a myriad of ghostly black-and-white movies) is now a style. You emerge convinced that something has happened, even if you and the playwright failed to converge on the crucial moment. The play's most moving, as well as its most appealing, moment is Mr Wickham's declaration that he could almost grasp what was going on, if only someone would slow it down and explain.

This belongs with the immortal remark of Constable Dull in Love's Labour's Lost (Aldwych): charged with not having spoken a word, he replies, "nor understood none neither." That always gets the loudest laugh of the play, though in John Barton's production--which will do until perfection comes along--it has stiff competition.

This is as it should be; their predicament--ensnared by their own pretensions, the victims of desire and of mortality--is prototypically comic. If there is no sadder scene in Shakespeare than the message of death that wrecks the accumulated masque, there is none funnier than the lord's sequential realisation--piling farce on irony--that they are all in love and all forsworn.

Richard Griffiths's tubby King, agonisingly shy, is a glorious joke; though to appreciate it fully you may need to know how straight the role is usually played, and how much further down the social scale Mr Griffiths is usually cast. This is in no way to impugn its essential truth, matched by that of Carmen du Sautoy's Princess, whose final transformations--grief, disillusion, anger, charity--are triumphantly done. Tony Church now plays Armado--a shabbier, more mournful Don than he offered in David Jones's production last time round.

* * *

Meanwhile Mr Jones has staged Cymbeline (Stratford). The programme contains enough quotations about dreams for us to believe that this was his governing concept, the dreamer presumably being Griffith Jones's blind Soothsayer, alone on the stage at beginning and end and crumbling Jupiter's prophecy with a gesture I took to be contemptuous. There seems at least to be an acerbic edge to the production throughout. Jeffery Dench's Cymbeline is played as an ineffectual tyrant (married to a wife from North Finchley, judging by the sound of her) and the play's many references to the "gentry" are curtly underlined. (Mind you, the contrastingly noble savages do turn out to be princes in disguise.)

I wondered if Mr Jones had been reading Edward Bond, who sees the play as "an appalling slapstick rehash of King Lear," and whether a class-conscious version of a play obsessed with the differences between "court" and country might not work better in Jacobean dress than in the legendary-nondescript get-up affected here. Even Judi Dench plays Imogen's bad temper (she's always getting at people, often with some provocation) for all it's worth, leaving enchantment to take care of itself--as, with her around, it infallibly does.

Roger Rees digs deeper into Posthumus's jealousy than is customary and Bob Peck rather surprisingly essays the epicene as Cloten. He looks like the Jolly Green Giant and sounds retarded. The company is full of good actors (and, on this showing, at least one terrible one), but they have yet to draw together. Neither does Mr Jones's production really cohere, but I preferred it to Mr Barton's rather bland version, also last time round.

* * *

Talking of blandness, Etherege's She Would if She Could, the seminal Restoration comedy, is being walked through at Greenwich under the direction of Jonathan Miller. The play is full of troubling passions delicately drawn, but you would never guess it.

* * *

What price a really funny young playwright? Jonathan Gems is the man, and of his uproarious The Tax Exile (Bush) more next time.
