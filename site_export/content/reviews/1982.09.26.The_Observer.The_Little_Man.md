The little man

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Brecht and Kyd at the National.

AS I understand it, the character of the Good Soldier Schweyk is a kind of unholy fool. He doesn't defy authority; he is merely incapable of engaging with it. It can't get on his wavelength either, so for much of the time he eludes it.

I may have got it wrong. Certainly that is not the character who appears in Brecht's updated dramatic version, Schweyk in the Second World War; at least not as played by Bill Paterson at the Olivier. Mr Paterson is a very canny actor. He can knot his limbs into apparently impossible positions, but he can always untie them again, swiftly and gracefully. He treats words in much the same way.

One scene finds him in the Prague railway-yard. An SS man mounts guard over a waggonload of weapons destined for Stalingrad. Schweyk, professing to help him remember the waggon-number, reels off a dizzying assortment of mnemonics, blowing his mind. The weapons roll off back to Germany. Maybe Schweyk, idiotically good-natured, was genuinely trying to help. But Mr Paterson leaves you in no doubt that he is a conscious saboteur. He destroys by clowning.

He is the quickest-witted man in town, covering up for his friends at the Tankard tavern when they say something indiscreet. He may say things that sound treasonable, but he can always explain how innocent they are. Defending himself with proverbs and folk-stories, he runs rings round the enemy. 'I don't know why I listen to you,' says Harry Towb's dazed SS lieutenant, but he has to anyway. There is a golden moment when Schweyk offers to take him outside to explain something; Mr Towb takes a hypnotised step in that direction before controlling himself.

Mr Paterson's performance is the greatest piece of virtuoso comedy yet seen on the South Bank. (Well, maybe Dinsdale Landen in 'Plunder'.) The scene in which he wriggles on his belly, dangling a sausage, to catch the dog demanded of him by the lieutenant, is a classic. The dog rather steals the limelight, though.) It does create a problem, however. Schweyk doesn't deliver the dog; he turns it into a meal for his ever-hungry friend Baloun. He thus forfeits his only protection, and is sent to the front. It's impossible to believe that so expert a nose-cleaner would let this happen.

But Brecht was never much interested in (read, was never much good at) plot. He just wanted a way of moving the action to Russia, and didn't care how. The play has the saving grace of being funny, but it's a very lazy piece of work: far inferior to Jack Pulman's television classic, 'Private Schultz', which it probably influenced.

It starts out with Hitler displaying a surprising interest in 'the little man'; then introduces that man, in the unlikely person of Schweyk, and leaves him to get on with it until it's time to ring down the curtain on a rousing hymn of hope. Along the way, though, Brecht suggested some wonderful stage-pictures, and on these Richard Eyre's production joyously seizes.

William Dudley's set wraps the playing-area, and part of the auditorium, in a frieze of Low cartoons, depicting the war-leaders. Dead centre is Hitler, whose portrait lifts to reveal him in conference with his henchmen. They are outsize human puppets, except for Goebbels, who is minute. (Poor old Goebbels.)

This is all in the text, but it's not usually as well realised as here. Nor has any other production I have seen brought on an eight-foot Hitler to confront Schweyk at the end. Life-size, the encounter makes no sense; here it takes on great symbolic force, Hitlerism, rather than Hitler, lumbering in defeat and finally disappearing down a trap-door.

It is difficult to laugh much at Nazism, easy to be impatient with Brecht's cartooning of it, so Mr Eyre points up the violence. At other times the stage is bathed in pure fun; Schweyk's hat hangs in mid-air, awaiting his return. The sets indeed give as polished a comic display as the actors, which is saying something.

Brian Glover puts real feeling into Baloun's gastric cravings; Imelda Staunton and Kevin Williams are strong in the younger element; and Julia McKenzie deals magically with the Eisler music, even getting a hint of the blues into 'The Song of the Gentle Breeze.' 'I've only got to hear you sing,' says an admirer, 'and I've got to get a grip on myself.'

All the Czechs are played north-country; fascists come from London. Susan Davies's translation, which has Hitler promising 'more order than you would have thought humanly possible,' is excellent.

* * *

For a story, try next door. At the Cottesloe, Michael Bogdanov has revived Kyd's Spanish Tragedy, excitingly. This pre-Shakespearean bloodbath influenced Hamlet obviously, plus Macbeth, plus King Lear, plus -- you might assume from Mr Bogdanov's unflattering view of the Watch -- Much Ado About Nothing.

It is a multiple revenge-piece, framed by what might seem a dead mechanism. A ghostly nobleman comes on at intervals to plead for retribution on his murderers; he is attended by the spirit of Revenge, sent from hell. Mr Bogdanov's master-stroke is to keep them on stage throughout: the late Don Andrea mutely lamenting while his enemies temporarily prosper; Revenge (a very cool performance by Peter Needham, smoking a cigarillo, hell being a city much like Seville) languidly placing weapons or fatal letters where they are likely to do most harm.

It might not work for a greater play, but it fits Kyd's determinist universe (where death only heralds greater torment) perfectly. Mr Bogdanov even makes a gratuitous sub-plot seem relevant; revenge rules here, as well, and it also inaugurates a spectacular series of executions, requiring only a chair, a steel frame and actors unafraid of hanging upside down.

The main avenger is old Hieronimo, whose son is killed. He hesitates, goes mad, finally produces a play in which he kills four people. (One of them seems innocent; maybe Kyd thought he'd made him a villain: a pardonable lapse in the circumstances.) Michael Bryant is not a tearer of cats, and his insanity sounds more like sarcasm, but his wit, his determination and his stoicism are unchallengeable.

The rest include a spruce Machiavel from Greg Hicks, a sturdy double (General and Hangman) from James Hayes, and a king from Nicholas Selby as authoritative as the 17 or so other lordlings he has played for this company. Patti Love, confronted with some taxingly regular verse, sometimes sounds as if she is spelling the words rather than saying them, and there are some rough-edged performances further down the cast.

In a posh Olivier production they might be fatal, but here they do no serious harm. The cast and the audience are with the play; Mr Bogdanov's interpolated joke delights, and leaves the tension quite unimpaired.

* * *

Miss Margarida's Way (Hampstead), hailing from Brazil via New York, is a monologue for a teacher. It is obvious after 10 minutes that she is a crazy lady, but the author, Roberto Athayde, continues with his parable of authority none the less. If we are not bored it is because Estelle Parsons plays her with consummate timing and great attack (don't be late, or she'll make you regret it) and because, while her claim that 'everyone wants to be Miss Margarida' may not be true, everyone has had a teacher who resembled her.
