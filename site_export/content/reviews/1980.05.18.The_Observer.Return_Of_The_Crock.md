Return of the Crock

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Rattigan double-bill at the National and `The Maid's Tragedy' at Stratford.

IF Terence Rattigan's The Browning Version (Lyttelton) were done in an ordinary West End house, it would probably seem a decent little play: a respectable exemplar of a dead tradition. On the sweeping width of the Lyttelton stage, facing a clinically challenging auditorium, it must expand or die.

In Michael Rudman's cool, passionate production it does the former. But there are no stretch-marks; the play, in one long act, remains a classically tight piece of work. It is, after all, patterned after the `Agamemnon'; and when Andrew Crocker-Harris, the unpopular Classics master forced into premature retirement, describes Aeschylus's play as the greatest ever written, you may be sure that Rattigan means it.

Mr Rudman uses his space like a battlefield. When he has Alec McCowen and Geraldine McEwan, as Andrew and his desperately embittered wife, sit at opposite sides of the stage, the air is dead between them. The marital poison is Strindbergian; so is the casual vitriol with which the Crocker-Harrises speak of their similarly afflicted acquaintances in between mounting more concentrated attacks on each other.

Millie's cruellest act is to destroy Andrew's belief in the present given him by his one receptive pupil: the `Browning version' of the Agamemnon. Miss McEwan lets you see exactly why she does it. `I would rather have had this present,' Andrew says, `than almost anything I can think of.' That presumably includes the pension of which he has been meanly deprived, to her quite justifiable fury. `Why,' as she says, `should he be allowed his comforting little illusions? I'm not.'

There is a kind of alliance between them, two failures against the world, and he has ratted on it. As he says, she is really quite as much to be pitied as he is. More, perhaps; he has his books, she has only a string of unloving lovers. In a later play Rattigan would probably have treated Millie's sexual dependency in more detail; here he lets Andrew tell us about it, rather than letting us see it for ourselves. Whatever his good intentions, however fine the work of Mr Rudman and Miss McEwan, it is the man's play, and it comes nearer to being a downbeat `Goodbye, Mr Chips' (Rattigan, with typical self-deprecating defensiveness, plants the reference) than might be comfortable.

When the play was first produced, making the protagonist so desiccated a figure must have seemed breathtakingly audacious. Now, when the Crock has passed into myth, we enter the theatre expecting to sympathise with him. Mr McCowen puts us brilliantly off the scent. There is no self-pity about him. His speech is brisk and level; he will remain the soulless, conscientious pedagogue to the end. Even the revelation of his unfortunate nickname hardly seems to disconcert him: `The Himmler of the Lower Fifth,' he muses, savouring the words like a lexicographer.

The piercing of the shell, so carefully built, is devastating--partly perhaps because it violates the actor's mask as well as the character's. (When did Mr McCowen last cry on stage?) But it is also momentary; what replaces it is a new bitterness, now willed and aggressive instead of a protective reflex, a schoolmaster's joke. His final muted defiance leaves the spectator torn between tears and cheers. It is a triumph for public-school understatement, a vein also skilfully and sympathetically mined by Nicky Henson as Millie's lover who doubles as the voice of decency--an awkward voice as might be expected in a world that deprecates explicit passion, even (or perhaps especially) in translation of the classics.

* * *

Mr Henson is the hero of Rattigan's afterpiece, Harlequinade, playing a harried stage manager on a subsidised tour of `Romeo and Juliet' (`I didn't know it was social purpose that brought us here--I thought it was the Arts Council'). Like Hildy Johnson in `The Front Page,' he is torn between a maniac milieu (Mr McCowen and Miss McEwan as a pair of empty-headed, fluffily forgetful stars) and the demands of a sensible fiancee.

As with Hildy, madness wins out; we leave him standing in for an absent actor in a brawl-scene, going devoutly through the one-two-three duelling motions, with an imaginary sword in one hand and a prompt-book in the other. It's a beguiling sight. Carl Toms's set delightfully recalls the days when Elizabethan plays were always set in a trim wilderness of steps and balustrades, wreathed in rosy-pink lights.

* * *

Today of course it's liable to be a sawn-off jungle--or else decadent within the decent limits of austerity. Judith Bland's set for The Maid's Tragedy (Other Place, Stratford-on-Avon) consists of a white pile carpet, dotted with garden furniture. It's a wickered world, and a dazzling one, the ladies of the cast strolling or languishing in primary colours.

Barry Kyle has engineered one of the most exciting of recent RSC productions. Beaumont and Fletcher's play takes as easily to scaling down as Rattigan's to blowing up. Concentration flowers for both actors and audience; every character is individualised, every line tells--especially the libidinous exchanges of a court mainly populated by panders.

A vaguely modern setting sharpens the all-pervading aroma of sado-masochism; with the king as its fountainhead. He is well enough played by John Carlisle as a disposable dictator; though this is hardly the ambience to inspire reverence for divine right. Unfortunately that is the governing force in the life of the hero, Amintor, married off to the royal mistress Evadne, but denied her bed; a more seasoned actor than Rob Edwards might reveal him as one of the more interesting Jacobean neurotics.

Tom Wilkinson gives a forceful account of his buddy Melantius, a war-hero turned terrorist--and, true to form, making a safe conduct his first demand; Raymond Westwell is his aged butt, drawn in considerable detail; Sinead Cusack is fittingly provocative as the bad girl who goes to the good by pinioning the king down (he just thinks she's into bondage) before stabbing him; and Domini Blythe makes something strong and disturbing of the suicidal virgin (who on paper looks wetter than Ophelia) for whom the play is named. Unless of course it's called after Amintor who, through royal caprice, goes virgin to his grave; those playwrights loved a joke.

* * *

Of the unsubsidised fringe groups trading in rare revivals, the best I have seen is the Cherub company, who infiltrate the Young Vic Studio with Barabbas by the Fleming, Michel de Ghelderode: not a likeable play (its Judas, certainly as played here, reeks of anti-Semitism), but a strong and original one, juggling familiar Passion Play characters into new baroque patterns with the released robber himself a bemused and furious thickhead. Andrew Vishnevski's production mostly avoids preciousness like the plague.

The week's one new play, Robert Holman's Estuary (Bush), has rather the look of a revival. What Mr Holman does well is as good as ever: natural, especially liquid, imagery, close male relationships, with Mark Eden especially good as a Tees-side Sunday fisherman, in collusion with his son and at odds with his wife. When the causes of conflict are revealed, they turn out to be as old as Aeschylus, and Mr Holman, though a quiet writer, will have none of Rattigan's pussyfooting. On this occasion, I think it might have helped.
