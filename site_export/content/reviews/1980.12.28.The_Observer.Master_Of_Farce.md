Master of farce

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Ben Travers, who died last week, aged 94.

THOSE of us too young to have seen Ben Travers's farces in their original productions at the Aldwych in the Twenties may never really have seen the plays at all. They were written for a stock company, and part of the joke lay in following the same characters, even if under different names, from one play to another.

Modern revivals have had to start from scratch; it was fascinating to see how the National Theatre's production of 'Plunder' developed as Dinsdale Landen, as the silly ass, began to create his own comic convention. Travers's farces were not tightly wrapped bundles of intrigue like Feydeau's; they were picaresque, comedies of incident rather than plot, and demanding an other-worldly flamboyance in their actors, even when, as in 'Plunder,' the conventions of the genre were stretched to include casual murder.

When the right style was found, they could be delightful; story apart, there was a surreal flair in Travers's dialogue. D'Arcy in 'Plunder' proudly proclaims, "I can phone, I've done it before."

The Travers revival of the Seventies was sparked by his new comedy, 'The Bed Before Yesterday,' a celebration of sex. As he himself freely admitted, its reception was influenced by everyone's amazement that a man of 90 could produce a workable play. It was a good evening, but unlikely to last as well as his previous triumphs. Its real importance was that it set him up as a theatrical mascot: someone we could all love and be proud of. Travers was lively, genial, sensible and a devil with women. No playwright can have enjoyed gayer nineties.
