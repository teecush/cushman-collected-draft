In Celebration

We are still living in the stretch of theatrical history that was inaugurated by the premiere of Look Back in Anger in 1956. That is a sentence I have been employing, more in conversation than in print, over the last ten years and it is one that has usually been greeted with corroborative nods. Last year, however, there was mutiny. Channel 4, in their Voices series of colloquies for the insomniac intelligentsia, mounted a discussion under the heading 'Is Jimmy Porter Dead?" Playwright David Edgar asserts that, as of 1980, he was. This was then debated by Richard Eyre of the National Theatre, Gavin Richards of Belt and Braces, and Michelene Wandor, writer and woman.

Mr Edgar posited that John Osborne had invented a drama that existed to slag off its audiences: a purpose inherited by every subsequent playwright worthy of the name but now displaced by what has been called "celebratory theatre', most prominently represented - as he admitted with a becoming twinkle - by his own adaptation of Nicholas Nickleby. He evinced mixed feelings about this 'sea-change' (he's a scrupulous man) but he found it worrying. The theatre, he said, now colluded with its audience instead of disturbing it. He quoted Hamlet on holding up mirrors, and on guilty creatures. Hamlet, when he made those references, was engaged in mounting a production for a very specialised purpose; and nobody can have heard that passage about showing *scorn her own image without finding it, in its context, strangely reductive.

If that's what Shakespeare thought drama was about (Hamlet also recommends showing 'virtue her own feature', which suggests a blueprint for a particularly sanctimonious theatre of reassurance) then he didn't know his own strength. Macbeth, of course, is a pretty stern warning against regicide, but that cannot be what has kept us interested in it. Mr Edgar asserted that Look Back in Anger took up an adversary position, and that that position was primarily political. I doubt the last part. Mr Osborne's autobiography A Better Class of Person indicates that when he wrote the play he had a lot of bile to work off, but his grievances, in book and play, are strictly personal. They could of course be politically interpreted by critics, and Mr Osborne obviously believed his own publicity enough to go on and write the first metaphor-for-England plays in The Entertainer and The World of Paul Slickey, but after that he went back to the confessional.

Of the playwrights who followed him in the 50s and 60s some, like Arnold Wesker and John Arden, wrote on explicitly political subjects and some, like Harold Pinter, did not; but I can detect in none of their plays the confrontational aggro that Mr Edgar recommends. I can detect it in what was written about them, both the reviews and the in-house proclamations. Indeed I can remember, as a teenager, feeling it myself: the clenched-teeth adrenalin of liking something that other people would hate, of wanting to rub their noses in something they would prefer to ignore. Do you recall the last scene of Beyond the Fringe? Four prophets gather to await the end of the world, a rushing wind that will annihilate everything except the mountain on which they themselves are standing, from which they intend to watch the destruction and have a good giggle.

That is the best image I know for the theatre of shock. The people who advocate it always want it to shock someone else: the perpetual 'other' which for the last century or more we have stigmatised as the bourgeoisie. Shock tactics Jimmy Porter found lots of chums. Ken Tynan, in his myth-making Observer notice, said that all the youth of England would respond and many of them did. At 13 I loved the opening gambit about the two posh Sunday papers. I still love it; the main reason the play has survived is that it is funny, i.e. we recognise the references. The people who didn't share them stayed away, so in the theatre the promised confrontation never happened.

This did not stop it being discussed in what were not then known as the media, but that is another kind of happening. Oddly enough Mr Osborne did develop an adversary stance in his latter plays, and little thanks he got for it from those who are supposed to favour such things. Witness the revolted response to A Sense of Detachment, which ironically is partly about the extreme difficulty of shocking that damnably adaptable thing, an audience. The playwrights of the 70s - Mr Edgar's generation did behave as if they believed the propaganda; they tended, in his own words, 'towards cynicism, nihilism and arrogance.' At least that was part of it; Richard Eyre, who was involved, testified during the debate that they were also looking for an audience that agreed with them.

But, to do them justice, I think they felt uneasy when they found it; scorn was faced with her own image and reacted by getting even more scornful. The discussion, inevitably, rambled. Miss Wandor wandered to feminism, saying that Janey Porter (at least it wasn't Jemima) had hardly had a look in. She was interestingly sceptical about the new female audience; justifiably so, since women have always gone to the theatre more than men. Mr Richards attacked "these managers and people" ', though with no mention of his own long West End run in Accidental Death of an Anarchist. He also said that you should not work "for an audience that you actually despise." I found this cheering; in this situation you should either stop working or - preferably stop despising.

Serious fun How does the despising start? People go into the theatre, as they go into any other public activity - criticism, for example - for only one reason: exhibitionism. This is generally thought to be a discreditable motive, so others are adduced: like political commitment. Besides, a play has to be about something and politics will do as well as any other subject: better than most, probably. But we should not confuse the material of making theatre with the thing itself. There is nothing than can be said in a play that cannot be said in some other form. If this is the one we choose - well, we choose it largely because we believe it is where our talents lie but also, I hope, because we think it an attractive thing in itself.

Because, to put it briefly, we think it is fun. If we think that, we have every reason to do what Mr Richards wants which is to share it with the largest possible audience. It is difficult, though, to reconcile ourselves to so frivolous an endeavour, so we talk instead about reaching the working class with plays that are relevant to them. Mr Richards spoke of doing The Ballygombeen Bequest, which is set in Ireland, at the Bush; and of moving the Irish bar staff to tears. Fine. You can always reach people with subjects that are close to them. If you can do it skilfully you deserve applause; but not, I think, marks for virtue.

There is a myth about the ideal audience that you are not playing to. Last year I sat in the bleachers for another Channel 4 programme, Shakespeare Lives, and heard Michael Bogdanov talk about the untapped audience that was young and vibrant. I decided that youth was nothing special, that I had never met anyone who vibrated, and that Mr Bogdanov should energies to entertaining the audience he actually had. If that can be done - without lies and without condescension - it can be a cure for guilt, a salve for contempt, maybe even an antidote to theatrical megalomania. If that is what Mr Edgar's 'celebratory theatre' is about, I am all for it.
