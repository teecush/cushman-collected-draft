Over the last ten years around 20 plays by Dennis Potter have been seen on television. He is probably the most highly regarded playwright working for the medium, and he has devoted most of his time and talent to it. When he has not been writing for it, he has been writing about it: first for the Sun, now for the New Statesman. He has also written a novel, but though three or four of his plays have been adapted for the stage, he has never, until now, written directly for the theatre. Now, it seems that his career may change direction.

His name has been missing from the screen for about a year - a year in which his work has kept accumulating, with the result that three plays were to be transmitted in quick succession. After I had talked with Potter and written this feature, it was decided that one of those plays would not be shown, but the transmission of the other two has more than a statistical significance; Potter says that the plays may (and he stresses the qualification) be among the last he writes for television. He feels despondent about the current state of television plays, finding they blend increasingly into programmes that surround them. 'More and more one talks of television as a thing, rather than of its constituent parts. I think the days of the television play are numbered; it will soon all be done on film, and it'll be a director's medium like the cinema. It only remains an author's medium at the moment because of British anachronisms. But before it ends I want to make a few defiant noises.'

Potter has been making defiant noises throughout his career. Both his plays and his journalism have been abrasive. (He had a spell as a book critic on The Times, and after he had written scathingly of a volume of military history one reader wrote in to say how interesting it was to hear Mr Potter's unfavourable opinion of the British army and now could The Times please print a review of the book.) So it is a surprise to visit his home, a three-storey house at Ross-on-Wye near the Welsh border, and to find his domestic life to be as charming (in the shapes of a wife, three children and a dog) as anyone could reasonably hope for. He talks quietly, considerately, and with exceptional lucidity.

Only he is ill. He has had arthritis for 14 years and he has often had to write in hospital. At the moment his condition is good but the constant undertow of depression and anxiety still makes itself felt. Obviously it has affected his work, making it darker but also funnier. As he says, 'a sense of the ludicrous emerges very strongly when you're not well.'

He has recently suffered another blow. His father has just died and he is still trying to come to terms with the loss. Equilibrium is harder to attain because his feelings for his father are bound up with his complicated emotions concerning the area in which he lives. Ross is the posh area of the Forest of Dean; Potter was born in the Forest, the son of a miner, and he grew up regarding 'Ross' with a mixture of distrust, amusement and presumably envy. (His wife Margaret comes from the same background; their fathers knew each other though the couple only met when Potter was an undergraduate.)

When he was 14 his parents made an abortive move to London - his mother was a Londoner and his father had wanted to get out of the pit for years - but it didn't work out. Dennis however remained in London, staying with relatives; he was attending St Clement Danes School in Hammersmith, and his 'O' Levels were in sight. (He had also become a Fulham fan; he still comes up to London to see them play.) School was succeeded by National Service, then by Oxford. He continued to spend his holidays in the Forest of Dean but returned to London to work: first for the BBC, then as a journalist.

His life has always been bounded in a small geographical compass; he has never travelled outside Britain, and now he is back nearly where he started: in a region which has served as a background for some of his plays, and for one of his novels. Plainly some of his work engages him in a wrestling-match with his roots. He refers to his home influences as 'soggy, blanket-like things' but adds that the writer's job is to make them concrete. 'As soon as you're compelled to wrestle daily with words you're immediately in contact with your childhood. There is an insight to be gained by living in a different relationship with your past, and one of the most dangerous ways is to return to near where you lived as a child. Everything I write comes out of those first ten to 14 years.' The retreat to the Forest of Dean was partly an escape but partly a search for something positive.

'The Forest of Dean is beginning to change because of the closing of all the pits but it was an extremely tightly-knit, rather weird community, wholly working-class.' He ticks off the characteristics - 'chapel, rugby football, male voice choir, many seemingly Welsh things.' Like many others he found that 'if accident and examination take you up the educational ladder you do lose many of these things along the way to a more general culture. But that cultural change, and all the insecurities accompanying it, was always there, waiting to be assessed by me, looked at again.'

The choir-and-chapel aspect of Potter's background has, in a strange way, caught up with him. From being a convinced atheist he has found himself in his work increasingly veering towards what he calls 'the traditional preoccupations of religious aspiration.' He is careful to put the word 'religious' in inverted commas and to emphasise that he does not mean the Church; he defines the object of his quest as 'the shape of an individual destiny, the uniqueness of an individual if that uniqueness is more than merely bio-chemical - and purpose, in terms of death, love, hate, beyond the merely political sense.'

The early Dennis Potter was very much concerned with 'the merely political'. While still at Oxford he worked on a scathing analysis of the society of the late 50s entitled The Glittering Coffin, which won him some notoriety. The book went beyond party to treat of politics 'in the whole New Leftish sense' and to undertake 'a sort of cultural analysis, a bastardised Marxism' addressed to problems of social mobility in an England which 'appeared to be changing very rapidly' but was as class-divisive as ever, and was inflicting 'casualties in terms of sensibility' on those who were moving. Such as Potter himself, who drew on his own experience both at Oxford and at home.

He in no way repudiates that analysis now; he feels 'very tender' towards the past self who wrote the book, and finds the political responses 'very healthy, very necessary'. One of its components was 'indignation and guilt' about the way in which his father's life had been wasted and misused. He found there were few ways in which British politics could answer such problems, but that the act of writing was in itself 'the first grappling with the permanent problems of human beings living together'. That, he agrees, sounds a Right-wing phrase 'and in a way it is instinctively right-wing to be concerned with those patterns which politics cannot change. In that sense,' he continues, 'I veer between a cast of mind which has many of the characteristics of right-wing thought, and a passion for justice which is, and must remain, intensely radical.' And in that tension lies drama.

His first drama grew almost literally out of that tension. The experience of standing as a Labour candidate in a safe Tory seat produced Vote, Vote, Vote for Nigel Barton and Stand Up, Nigel Barton - two plays about a Labour politician, a graduate from a working-class background, who finally retreats in disillusion from the crunches of the party machine. The plays were produced by the BBC in rapid succession back in 1965 and they established Potter as a television playwright. But already he was in transit, and his next play Emergency Ward Nine denoted a shift of interest. He had been in hospital, seen death for the first time, seen 'how sick bodies were'; and the play - a live Thirty-Minute Theatre planned rather cynically as a formula drama - became concerned with people's fantasies, and their inability to understand the shape of their own lives.

'All the time I've been writing for television I've been trying to break its endemic naturalism.' As he says, this is a very difficult undertaking: 'You can't beat it by, say, putting the camera in shot because all you have is another piece of naturalism in a slightly different convention. The only way you can beat it is by the strength of the play's whole metaphor.' As an example he proffers Follow the Yellow Brick Road which used the disparity between grimy reality and the sunlit world of television commercials as a fight between devils and angels. He adds though that this metaphor of 'a certain religious striving' was probably lost on the audience, as it certainly was on the critics. He says this without perceptible rancour; of my own disparaging review of the stage version of Only Make Believe he remarks mildly that it spoiled his Sunday, as he in his New Statesman television column, has spoiled other people's Fridays.

He regards his journalism both as a necessary job ('I need to keep in employment') and as a safety-valve: 'I'm saved from sounding off in a sterile way in my plays by being able to do it in my column.' When he sounds off in his plays he wants it to be for real. 'I have a solemn, serious, almost indecent sense of my own importance as a writer and an individual; it's odd that I should wrestle for 11 years in the one medium - television - which tends to modify that seriousness.' He recognises, and uses, the pervasive modern mode of irony but dreads it degenerating into nervous jokiness. 'We ought not to be so diffident that we can't say our plays are about these things. If you do that then you become diffident about everything and I'm damned if I'm going to be diffident or mocking about everything. Today it seems irony exists almost in its own right; it becomes a giggling, chuckling bog.'

As an example of a play which really satisfied him, Potter cites Moonlight On The Highway which used images of nostalgia (particularly records of the 30s crooner Al Bowlly - phrases from popular songs have often furnished Potter with springboards for plays) as a 'kind of fungoid growth' - on a contemporary study of sexual unease and fear. This creative use of nostalgia is a difficult feat; one I would have thought to be impossible.

The 'defiant noises' of the new plays are inherent in both technique and content. The religious concern, coupled with a determined wryness about dogma is evident in Where Adam Stood in which Alan Badel plays a Victorian naturalist who evolves a theory to reconcile Darwin and the Book of Genesis and whose young son learns to turn his father's belief to his own advantage. The characters are historical; the boy grew up to be the literary critic Edmund Gosse. Double Dare (another Al Bowlly title) explores the mind of a playwright by juxtaposing levels of reality in a manner with which Potter experimented in Only Make Believe and the novel Hide and Seek. The idea is not just 'to write about writing' but to define the link between the way a writer looks at the world and what he actually writes.

After reading the scripts, the play I had found the most fascinating of Potter's new work was the untransmitted third play, Brimstone and Treacle. It seemed to be nearest the author's own heart, bringing on a modern devil, working to what appears a perversely good end. The new plays continue on the road begun in Emergency Ward 9: they all concern what Potter calls 'the unconscious abstractions that make up our images of other people'.

In announcing that he may have come to the end of the road, at least as far as television is concerned, he is revolting as much as anything against the ephemerality of the medium. He feels a duty, as well as a desire, to leave a permanent mark, and accordingly he is writing his first stage play. He has long been wary of the theatre; its 'elitism' seems both to attract and repel him. His craftsman's interest in television remains unabated; and it is hard to believe that he will write no more original TV plays. I suppose that he can get on without television and television of course can get on without him. But it possesses few writers of his intelligence and ambition, and I hope it won't have to.
