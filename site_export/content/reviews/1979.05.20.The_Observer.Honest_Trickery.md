Honest trickery

Theatre
ROBERT CUSHMAN in London, Chichester and Salisbury

The reputation among us of Ferenc Molnar, the Budapest boulevardier, rose with 'The Wolf' and sank with 'The Guardsman.' The Greenwich production of The Play's the Thing, which appears to be his masterpiece, should more than reinstate him. It presents us with an evening of superbly honest trickery.

Audiences always like to see actors eating; remarks one of the characters over a breakfast consisting--in the best Central European dramatic and culinary tradition--of 'ham, eggs, cold chicken, smoked salmon, cold beef, bacon, butter, milk, honey, jam and rolls.' Right; but Molnar's charm is that he is not merely indulging us. He is indulging himself. Our luck is his good taste.

This is a play about playwrights: two playwrights and a composer. Also about an actor and actress. The three scribes have come to a castle on the Italian Riviera (they are successful writers, and the period is the 1920s) to recuperate after collaborating on their latest hit. The actress is engaged to the composer and she has the room next door. Arriving unannounced, they hear her apparently engaged in passionate dialogue with the actor.

It is likely to cross the mind of any audience that has entered a theatre before that dialogue is indeed all that they were exchanging; that they were merely rehearsing a scene, and that the heartbroken composer has in fact nothing to be jealous of. Such has been the resolution of many similar dramatic situations. Such, indeed, is the outcome of this one, but only because Sandor, the brains of the playwrighting team, knows that life needs a little help if it is to imitate art. He stays up all night writing a play that will incorporate such a scene, and then gently blackmails the culprits into performing it. He deserves that breakfast.

With charming impudence Molnar, with Sandor as his mouthpiece, teases play and audience along. Musing on how much easier and more pleasant drama would be without the customary rules of exposition he advances to the footlights and explains who, what and where he is, and has his fellows do the same--as if they were in a play. The 'as if' is kept going throughout, and Molnar only breaks his own rules once. At the end of Act Two Sandor asks for ideas as to what constitutes a good second-act curtain. Suggestions are proffered. And, at the climax of each, the curtain duly sweeps in, to be stopped in its tracks by Sandor, who always knows better. This, by making the convention manifest instead of leaving it suspended between stage and auditorium, tips the play into coyness.

But all is redeemed in the last act, where Sandor's play is itself performed and turns out to be full of sweet revenges at the expense of its blustering male protagonist. The effect, as immediate verbal comedy (much enhanced, I suspect, in this adaptation by P. G. Wodehouse), cascades through a situation of highly charged artifice, is altogether exhilarating. There is the requisite amount of bitterness in the happy ending (happiness, we are again reminded, is a state of being well deceived), but our main response--one of the primary legitimate theatrical pleasures--is of joy in the author's cleverness.

Alan Strachan's production might be speedier in its earlier stages, but it is knife-edged and splendidly cast. John Moffatt, a master of the languishing comic art of flicking off a line without ever losing it, may be giving the performance of his life as Sandor; and he has excellent, bumbling assistance from James Cossins. There are also contrasting displays of temperament from Julia McKenzie and Richard Coleman as the players; and a perfectly judged display of primness by Stephen Boswell as an impromptu prompter.

* * *

The Roundhouse, for all its renovations, is still a less-exciting arena than Manchester's Royal Exchange and it has had a particularly cramping effect on Laurie Dennett's marine set for The Lady from the Sea; we seem now to be in an aquarium rather than on a fjord. The sound effects, too, lack their former spacious stereophony. Enough survives, though, to make Michael Elliott's production the best theatrical vindication I have seen of George Steiner's paramount claim for Ibsen: that he 'invented his own mythology.' One wonders, in fact, why he should have invented it only to discard it; the call of the sea is so powerfully established that Ellida's decision to resist it and stay with her land-animal husband, Dr Wangel, seems to defy not the character's logic so much as the play's.

The development of minor relationships is delicately and humorously done by this company, setting off the serious Wangels' wrangles. It is much the husband's play as the wife's; perhaps more, since she can only make the crucial decision to stay after he has taken hers to let her go. Graham Crowden as the doctor is both enormously gentle and enormously assertive; the conflict within him is vein-bursting. Perhaps he permits himself too many symbolic lunges at the symbolic flagstaff. Mr Elliott's production is commendably unafraid of melodrama.

Vanessa Redgrave is believably, almost unnervingly, a creature of two worlds, equipped even with two modes of speech: drawly and precise when possessed, loose and fresh--sometimes even irritatedly commonsensical--when the demons lose their hold. Physically she is instinct, not just with gracefulness, but with grace.

* * *

The Chichester season began with a raucously efficient production of The Devils' Disciple, which occasionally sinks below mere competence--notably in a performance of the will-reading scene which tackles this theatre's sight-line problems by presenting everybody with a view consisting entirely of the backs of heads--but which rises above it in two performances.

Brian Blessed as Anthony Anderson, the turncoat minister, plays with conquering warmth and, when the military fit comes upon him, with startling violence. (Startling and funny, just as Shaw meant it to be.) He is also supple and sensitive in his scenes with Mel Martin, who plays his wife and matches him, balancing the reality of her emotions against a saving sense of the ridiculous. Ian Ogilvy, meanwhile, makes the title role a pleasantly swashbuckling blur: an approach it and the play can survive, but which diminishes them both. John Clements, who should have topped the whole thing effortlessly off as General Burgoyne, seemed on the first night well below par.

* * *

David Zane Mairowitz's Landscape of Exiles (Half Moon) traces the course of British Socialism from the death of Marx to the birth of the Labour Party. Its central characters are Marx's daughter Eleanor, her husband Edward Aveling and--pre-eminently--Engels. Interesting that an author presumably opposed to the great man theory of history should yet fall back on it as dramaturgy. Engels comes over as a negative great man sure enough--an almost Stoppardian figure, Marx's understudy--but everyone is hung up on what he will think.

The play is distinctly similar to Robert Bolt's State of Revolution, but dealing with British history rather than Russian it is considerably less picturesque. It is, however, well-written in a rather literary way, and an uneven cast is led with marked distinction by John Phillips.

* * *

In Miss Leasing Lady, the enterprising Salisbury Playhouse attempts at once to advance the cause of the British musical and of the rarer and later Restoration comedy (the source is Susannah Centlivre's decorous but efficient A Bold Stroke for a Wife). The two endeavours get fatally in each other's way.
