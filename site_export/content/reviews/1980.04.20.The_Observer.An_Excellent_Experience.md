An excellent Experience

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Cymbeline, Twelfth Night, Private Lives and The Apple Tree.

IT IS reasonable to be wary of theatre companies who have names like the more sanctimonious kind of pop-group. But with Shared Experience, one's defences crumble; they live up to their title, pleasuring their audiences without ever patronising them.

The experience they share is a narrative one. They have already re-created for us the Arabian Nights and Bleak House; they specialise in tall, tangled tales. So it's appropriate that their first Shakespearean venture should be Cymbeline (now at the Lyric Studio, Hammersmith), whose complexities have defeated attempts at conventional large-scale production.

It is, to begin with, almost impossible to find a satisfactory visual style for a story that rambles between a Snow White fairyland, Druidical Britain, Renaissance Italy, and the Roman Empire of Augustus. Shared Experience, working without a set and with just five players in white shirts and slacks, make these odds all even. The inconsistencies and improbabilities are neither heightened nor abolished, but they are levelled down; no one style is more important than any other, and no glib overall solutions, technical or interpretative, are flourished in our faces.

We are not asked to believe, but alienation hardly comes into it since we are not asked to judge either. Indeed we're not really asked to do anything; certainly not, in the hectoring manner with which theoreticians love to bully audiences, to use our imaginations. This company doesn't make demands, which is not only polite but realistic of them. We, after all, are paying them. (Well, I'm not of course, but you are.) All we need to do is to pay attention and enjoy ourselves, not necessarily in that order.

The doubling involved is less hectic than might be expected; in this episodic piece characters tend, until the finale, to move around anyway in separate groups of two or three. Even at the end, their destinies are disposed of in batches, so a particular formation can dissolve and immediately realign as a collection of other identities. To help us through, the actors speak the opening stage-directions of each scene. This becomes an amusing running gag on its own account, but it avoids the coyness that once engulfed the RSC when they used the same device in the same play, apparently as a means of ducking a battle-scene. Shared Experience give us a battle, and a good one, with their own vocal cords as the chief weapons.

If the story works, so do the characters; there is some beautiful, unassertive acting. Raad Rawi's account of Cloten, the play's clownish (as opposed to comic) villain, is so complete that one hardly thinks of characterisation; leading with a thrusting chin and an indignant nose, he lets the princeling's baffled energies spring right out of him. Cloten defeats most actors by never quite settling into stock; he is a bully without being a coward. He manages, for example, an exaggerated but still quite stirring defiance of the Roman invasion, a scene which Mike Alfreds's direction handles with particular care, balancing Mr Rawi's eruptions against the more calculated bellicosity of Ruth Seglow's Queen and the pacific fumblings of John Dicks's Cymbeline.

Mr Dicks makes a sweet old king, though his treatment of Iachimo -- the play's afterthought on Iago -- betrays the feyness which can be this company's Achilles' heel; he isn't dangerous enough. All the same, he and Mr Rawi (who is the imperfect hero Posthumus as well as Cloten) get enough of the play's corroded male sexuality for Pam Ferris's Imogen -- ripe, sharp and appropriately housewifely -- to have something to transcend. Miss Ferris has one of the primal Shakespearean skills; she meets rage, joy or wonder, head-on. Indeed, despite some ill-advised gabbling, very little of the text is shirked by anyone; there are some subtleties the RSC might envy, while the celebratory ending is immaculate and irresistible. I have not seen all this company's work, and I have no intention of holding them up as a model for anyone. I don't believe in models, or -- since my bias has always been towards mainstream work and will probably stay that way -- in overnight conversions. Nor do I want to belabour the RSC, whose non-Shakespearean work has continued excellent.

* * *

Their Twelfth Night (Aldwych) is no atrocity, but -- apart from Cherie Lunghi's Viola, which fizzes with immediacy, and should go straight to the protective heart of every man in the audience -- it all seems jaded. Even John Woodvine's Malvolio has declined into a series of more or less expert gags; Terry Hands has staged the last scene suggestively, the stage gradually filling with seated, disillusioned figures: Andrew nursing his broken head, Toby subsiding beneath alcohol and marriage, Antonio lonely, the lovers apparently more oblivious by the minute. But it remains an idea, and not too interesting since the characters have rarely come to life in the first place.

I liked Willoughby Goddard's last, despondent moments as Toby, Geoffrey Hutchings's renditions of Feste's songs (especially "O mistress mine"), the funny duel, and Norman Tyrrell's Fabian, a role that can be done almost any way, and is here done old and rustic. (On a related note; in the Stratford As You Like It the performance of Corin I praised last week was given by the understudy, Tom Wilkinson.)

* * *

How, in these troubled days, do you ensure West End success? You revive Private Lives (Duchess) which has few characters, will always attract both stars and customers by symbolising the metropolitan entertainment tradition as few other pieces do, and is enough of a classic for aspiring suburban or regional theatres to feel justified in taking the initial plunge for you. It is a wonder the thing is not done more often -- and a blessing, since, good though it is (and possibly getting better -- Someday I'll Find You, for example, is now as much of a symbol for us as for the characters), you do need time to forget where the jokes come from.

As it happens, the best section of Alan Strachan's production (from Greenwich) is the last, in which the piece becomes four-handed. The minor couple, Victor and Sybil, have been strongly cast with Ian Collier engagingly boorish and Jenny Quayle coming shrilly and delightfully out of her shell to justify all her rival, Amanda, has said about her. It is always a relief in Coward when the butts turn out, as bad-mannered (and thus as privileged) as the wits. Maria Aitken swans through Amanda amusingly enough (by which I think I mean not quite amusingly enough); more of a surprise is Michael Jayston's Elyot. He manages to preserve Coward's rhythms (which are vital) without aping his tones, and his delivery of the notorious self-justifying lines about behaving like very small, quite idiotic children at a party is supremely tactful.

* * *

Those, understandably dismayed by Sheldon Harnick's lyrics for The Umbrellas of Cherbourg should rush to the Overground Theatre, Kingston, to hear what he achieved with his former collaborator Jerry Bock. Their 1966 musical The Apple Tree is, in fact, a trio of one-act pieces, derived from Mark Twain, Frank Stockton and Jules Feiffer. This production is happier with the first segment, an all-American Garden of Eden, than with the other two, which need more resources but could manage for the time being with more speed and a greater awareness of the score's beguiling line in self-mockery. Lesley Duff goes from Twain's Eve to the Turkish Delight Princess of Stockton's The Lady or the Tiger to Feiffer's Passionella, who herself goes from chimney-sweep to movie-star. Would you believe she prefers the second? She is dazzlingly assured as every one of them.
