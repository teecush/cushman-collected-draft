Swinging back to words

ROBERT CUSHMAN talks to Peter Brook about his latest Paris production.

AS usual the world is flocking to Peter Brook: not just to the Theatre des Bouffes-du-Nord in Paris, where he has just staged Alfred Jarry's Ubu, but to the man himself. He somehow manages to hold court even when sitting in the cafe next door to the theatre, dining off a glass of wine and a fried egg.

The Bouffes-du-Nord is a peeling dilapidated theatre (and jealously preserved as such) in an unfashionable part of town. Brook, though, has created his own kind of economy chic. At the end of the performance, director, actors and audience cluster together where the stage would be, if the Bouffes-du-Nord actually had a stage these days, and the admiration is as thick as it was in the cafe before the show. It's just as well the work is so good: the atmosphere otherwise would be unbearable.

One of the crowd is an Indian, who's arrived to work on the international group's long-term project, a dramatisation of the Mahabharata, which Brook describes as being to Indian culture "Dante, Homer and the Bible in one. Its themes and elements are relevant to everybody, but at the moment it's closed to us because its only theatre form is the Kathakali, which is too totally Indian for us to understand directly. Our aim is to become a bridge, to find a true non-Indian form for the quality of the work to come across. We've already been working on the basic script for a year."

Ubu is another step on the road, already marked by his Paris productions of Timon and The Ik and by his celebrated excursions to Persepolis (with the invented-language spectacle Orghast) and through the towns and villages of Nigeria; also by some less-publicised adventures: "doing incredibly crude improvisations on the streets of Brooklyn where gangs would throw stones at the group."

Brook still speaks of a synthesis between the two forms he marked out in his book The Empty Space -- the holy theatre (very serious, usually formal, but limited in appeal) and the rough theatre (popular but usually short on content). Like most people in this mind, he refers back to the Elizabethan achievement as the model of what he is after; and, in fact, he is about to have an Elizabethan, or rather Shakespearean year, returning to Stratford to direct Antony and Cleopatra with Glenda Jackson and an Actor Unknown, but before then, with his Paris International Brigade, doing Measure For Measure.

He once staged a reputedly definitive production of this play at Stratford, and it is unusual for him to challenge comparison so directly with his own past. For him it represents "the marriage of all the extremes. It has scenes of extremely bawdy comedy; it's political--it says all there is to say about justice and social order; and it's metaphysical--it deals with the relationship between the spiritual and temporal life. It's a story that can be told in the theatre to children, adolescents, old people simultaneously."

A few years ago Brook had the reputation of being an anti-word man. Probably the rumours were exaggerated; anyway, he's now swinging back the other way. "We've gone about as far as we could in direct off-the-cuff improvisation. We've done thrilling things, but they've only been for ourselves because they're swept away the moment the experience ends." So back to a text, in this case Jarry's piece from the 1890s, a schoolboy prank that set off an aesthetic revolution, what Brook calls "a masterpiece of non-official theatre, a classic anti-classic."

"The value of a text lies not in abstract literary merits but because it's life in concentration; it's a shrivelled essence that expands in performance. Jarry's very special because he provides the raw material for an anarchic explosion but he's also highly precise. There's an astonishing amount of unpredictable stuff in the play that could not be envisaged when you read it. But that doesn't mean that anything goes. You can only realise the new forms if you have a very precise respect for the forms themselves."

Brook's austerest pronouncements often boil down when you consider them to plain commonsense, and his best work often resembles what one has always believed to constitute good theatre anyway. So his Ubu is a classic piece of comedy. His actors have acquired a peculiar shared intensity which enables them to play in a vein of nightmare farce and make it seem the most natural thing in the world.

The young Jarry turned the hated schoolmaster into an archetype of ruthless mediocrity; Brook called Ubu "the eternal middle-class French concierge: narrow mean horizons fired with a passionate egocentricity." It is generally written that after Ubu's first word "Merde" the theatre was never the same again.

Recent productions have tried conscientiously to make it as explosive as possible. Brook, as so often, underplays: Ubu (the hulking Greek-American Andreas Katsulas) comes on at one door, his shrewish wife (Michele Collinson) at another, and they begin an ordinary day. Bad temper is not a means of shocking the audience but just their way of carrying on.

The play concerns Ubu's brief career as King of Poland -- brief, bloody, cowardly and spectacularly avaricious. (All his laws are tax-laws.) He is a cartoon blend of Tamburlaine and Macbeth and this production joyfully travesties all the Elizabethan battle-scenes you have ever seen. A giant wheel keeps appearing, cheerfully rolling over most of the cast and leaving a trail of limp bodies on the floor. Bricks recur as well; Mrs Ubu serves a banquet of them to Poland's rapacious nobility.

In the second half, Ubu Enchained (which was always less successful and still is), the focus changes; Ubu comes to France and decides that in the land of liberty and fraternity the only distinction, the only way to riches, lies in being a slave. Brook's view of this paradox ("he's terrifying but he challenges a society that has to be challenged") seems to me overblown but the production is still full of quiet splendours. When rain falls, seemingly from nowhere, into the Ubus' prison, it forms into the most convincing puddle in the history of world theatre.
