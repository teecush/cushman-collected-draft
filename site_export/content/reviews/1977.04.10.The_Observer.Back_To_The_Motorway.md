Back to the motorway

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

STEPHEN POLIAKOFF has a keener eye and nose than any other playwright for British rubbish, new-style. No scene of his has registered more strongly than the young couple in Hitting Town up-ending the inedible contents of a Wimpy Bar. His latest piece Strawberry Fields (Cottesloe) takes us on a guided tour of the new wastelands.

Three young people make their way by van up the motorway, which got its own theatrical come-uppance some years ago in Lay-By (of which Mr Poliakoff was one of the multiple authors) and does not seem to have improved its image since. We see them picnicking by slag-heaps and tucking painfully into Chicken Maryland, waited on by the cleaner in an all-night cafe whence all but she have fled. They conduct conversations on the plastic orange chairs of a bus-station sun-lounge, in a men's lavatory which the girl of the party proclaims to be the ugliest place she has ever seen, and in a condemned corner of Doncaster, whose principal features are a cinema, closed down, and a hot-dog stand, unmanned. Finally they arrive in the cleaner air of rural Northumberland, and give thanks for an unspoiled bit of old England. The idyll, though, is rendered decidedly ironic by the fact that they are now on the run. On the journey up they killed a man.

Two of the trio, Charlotte and Kevin, represent something called the English People's Party; the third, Nick, conventionally decent, is along for the ride and to argue with them. The EPP appear at first to be ecologists; it then becomes apparent that conservation has led them to conservatism and beyond. They are right-wing fanatics, though they dissociate themselves from the National Front, perhaps because their own prejudices are aesthetic; they regard immigrants as blots on the landscape. It is Charlotte who pulls the trigger in Doncaster, thereby reversing most of our expectations; we are used to plays about left-wing guerrillas.

Much of the play indeed seems an attempt to give the devil his due; to put the opposition into the glamorous fugitive position usually reserved for the author's own side. Kevin dreams that he and Charlotte will achieve Bonnie-and-Clyde immortality, and he gets a tremendous charge when the news of their crime takes over the air-waves on all available stations. He is going blind, once spent all his time in cinemas, and claims that his head is so full of movies that if pointed at a screen he could disgorge a full show.

Kevin might be an orthodox counter-culture hero-victim out of Heathcote Williams. But, while we are dropping names, David Edgar in Destiny and Alan Drury in The Man Himself have delved into the psychology of the new fascist far more persuasively than Mr Poliakoff does here. He relies on big speeches, but keeps us waiting for them a long time; when they arrive so much rides on them that they can hardly help sounding forced.

His feeling for detritus remains as acute as ever and he can make you taste in your own mouth an endless motorway night, with no sleep and only sludge to eat. The odyssey of this play is a logical one for him to take, but his characters seem to have been press-ganged into it. Michael Apted's production is stronger on place (table and fruit machines dotted strategically) and on travel (headlamps raking the auditorium between stops) than on personality; nobody opens up much or even walks very far. You notice that the Cottesloe, though it may be the National Theatre's baby stage, is still a healthy size and not, on this showing, designed for chamber work. The principals--Jane Asher, Stephen Rea and Kenneth Cranham--are conscientious but subdued; though Miss Asher has both the hardness and the pale elegance for Charlotte, and it makes a change to hear Mr Cranham as the voice of reason. Maya Kemp's impromptu waitress is a gem; Mr Poliakoff has always been good at waitresses. This is not a disgraceful play for him to have written or for the National to have staged, but I hope for better.

* * *

For All Those Who Get Despondent (at the Theatre Upstairs last week) is not actually calculated to cheer anyone up. It is described as a cabaret, drawn from the works of Brecht and Wedekind--both performers in the vein themselves as well as writers, though much of the Brecht material dates from later, soberer years. The Wedekind words--and, at one point, the Wedekind music--are agreeably black and anarchic; the Brecht is preachier and the company seem disposed to ram it down our throats.

Myra Love, a smouldering singer, came among us looking not merely daggers but whole armouries; Anna Calder-Marshall, a powerful bleeding-heart actress in the right environment, drained all the slyness from such a question as 'where, the evening that the Great Wall of China was finished, did the masons go' and substituted black accusation. It was good to hear Hanns Eisler's Brecht-settings (particularly The Love Market), which balance harshness with subtlety; later chord-thumpers, take note. Peter Barnes's translations show, for one so steeped in the best of popular song, a surprising indifference to rhyme and metre.
