Chicago chuffer

ROBERT CUSHMAN on theatre in New York and Toronto.

A LONDON director, on whose latest production I had cast some mild aspersions, wrote me a kind and forbearing letter suggesting that I seek theatrical refreshment in New York, where at least I might see some good musicals. Such solicitude towards my tenderest spot touched me deeply. I received his letter on Wednesday 29 November; Thursday I was on the plane. To tell the truth I was going anyway.

* * *

Friday. Three musicals have dominated 1978 on Broadway: Dancin', Ain't Misbehavin' and On the 20th Century. The last of these has been generally dismissed as old-fashioned, being the only one to possess a traditional book, with a plot and all. It derives from the celebrated thirties movie, about an actor-producer trying to lure a former protegee back into his arms and on to his stage, and is admittedly tired.

But it all happens on a train, chuffing from Chicago to New York and representing, as designed by Robin Wagner, both the apotheosis of Art Deco and a mechanical delight. Its freight includes a new star, Judy Kaye, who is a spunkily funny coloratura. The train and Miss Kaye set the tone of Harold Prince's unexpectedly playful staging which reflects the benign showbiz nostalgia of the best MGM musicals -- all written, like the show, by Betty Comden and Adolph Green. I enjoyed it.

* * *

Saturday. The matinee of Eubie, the latest anthology show, celebrating Eubie Blake, now 95, son of slaves, composer of "I'm Just Wild About Harry" and much else. Like most such combinations, it started off-Broadway and moved on. It now boasts an energetic black cast and audience, the latter comparatively new to Broadway, the former traditional. Musically the show moves from rag-time grace to overblown soul (everyone trying to sound like Lou Rawls or Diana Ross); theatrically it is slight, but it holds.

In the evening downtown to Joseph Papp's subsidised power house, the Public Theatre (a complex with more components than the National) for a workshop production of Thomas Babe's latest, Fathers and Sons, a disappointment after A Prayer for my Daughter, since it extends that play's tendency towards verbosity while sacrificing both its movement and its powerful verismo. It's a Western fantasia; Wild Bill Hickock meets and is gunned down by his unacknowledged bastard. That's really all, although Calamity Jane and many others follow in attendance.

The production, however economical, is crowded and handsome, though too many lines (and Mr Babe still writes good ones) are yelled into oblivion. Why does a city whose every waitress and cab driver seems gifted with perfect comic timing support so much heavy-handed acting? Richard Chamberlain's Wild Bill is a stylish and heroic exception.

* * *

Sunday. The Sabbath matinee is a splendid institution, and I went happily to Dancin', Bob Fosse's tribute to his trade, hoping to swallow bile summoned up by Mr Fosse's Chicago. Well, there is one exhilarating set of routines -- done to Benny Goodman's swing classic "Sing, Sing, Sing" -- and those American kids can certainly (but who doubted it?) dance. But the whole is uninspired, and often worse: witness an erotic number that looks like a reject from "Oh! Calcutta!" and linking figures who say there will be no story or dialogue, and say what each scene intends, and go on saying, archly and witlessly. My teeth were constantly on edge.

In the evening, back to the Public for the last night of a sell-out -- in all senses, according to the local scribes. Now when a show gets merely bad reviews, the chances are it is merely bad. When the boys club it to the ground and then jump on its stomach, it usually has something going for it. So it is with E. L. Doctorow's first play, Drinks Before Dinner. It is not a model of dramatic excellence or even of intellectual consistency. It works only through words, but these are witty where the play lampoons Manhattan chic, and terrifying when it veers towards a vision of the apocalypse.

A rich New Yorker, invited to dine with half a dozen of his peers, pulls a gun on the guest of honour, a high Government official. His pretext is that he believes that the end of the world -- at least of his world -- is at hand; his victim corroborates all his suspicions and even elaborates on them. Christopher Plummer, whom I have never seen better, and Josef Sommer, whom I have never seen, play an exquisite game of cat and mouse. Tony Walton provides a set of literally incredible elegance.

* * *

Monday. Neil Simon's Chapter Two has been widely hailed as a bad play, and indeed its first half (particularly the wisecracks) bears the painful stamp of truth. But Mr Simon cannot act as tough towards his central couple -- a hastily remarried widower and divorcee -- as he would obviously like, and so fumbles and pads in the second act. I don't think he is pandering to his audience; he's pandering to himself.

* * *

Tuesday. Off-Broadway, Sam Shepard's new one, Buried Child. Recommended for those who can take or leave him alone. Ghastly black comic revelations in rural America, reminding some of The Homecoming and me of Cold Comfort Farm. I didn't much care about the skeletons, since I never believed in the cupboard, but the incidence of individual laughs and shudders is praiseworthily high.

* * *

Wednesday matinee. Finally make it to Ain't Misbehavin', the town's hottest ticket apart from Annie. Disappointment probably inevitable. This Fats Waller medley, obviously inspired by Eubie, which is less slickly staged but now has the higher performance level. Waller's wry joking has inevitably been coarsened, but the show is still likeable; if I'd come across it unheralded, I would probably have loved it. Both Dancin' and Ain't Misbehavin' are comin' to London, which somehow ain't that excitin'.

* * *

Thursday. A treat. Bondini's, a downtown restaurant, has a mini revue whose title, To the Happiness Of Us All, is the only uninspired thing about it. Peggy Beatty, an aggressively genteel soprano, Norman Rollings, a bald and sinister singer-pianist, and a strange, sultry lady cellist billed as Miss Faun provide a wildly unpredictable entertainment that begins as spoof operetta and sends out tentacles in all directions. Mission fulfilled: this is New York's best musical.

I write this while anticipating Ballroom, Michael Bennett's first show since A Chorus Line and mulling over the profound thoughts (whither America?) that are supposed to occur to critics on their foreign travels. Meanwhile I shelter in Toronto, where the big playhouses offer British or American exports (current attraction, John Gielgud in Half Life), but where, in stark contrast to navel-gazing New York, a bristling fringe is consciously (often self-consciously) nationalistic.

One small theatre, the Tarragon, goes looking for a Canadian identity on the highways with a country music show called 18 Wheels about long-distance truck driving: a marvellous subject but, on this static showing, a difficult one to stage. Another locally respected company, Theatre Passe Muraille, is due to visit England next year, though not, I hope, with Les Maudits Anglais, a clumsy joke about cultural warfare in Quebec.

The same topic is hilariously treated by Toronto's resident revue team, The Second City, as are airlines, the Post Office, TV news (slogan -- "If it happened today, it's news to us") and inevitably the Prime Minister. (Theirs, I mean). Nothing very new about the targets, but the company's elan is immense; I've seen no funnier revue in years.
