Falklands tragedy

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

A YEAR ago today Lieutenant David Tinker, RN, was killed in action on HMS Glamorgan. He was 25. His letters home, to his family and his fiancée, were collected by his father, Hugh Tinker, and published under the title A Message from the Falklands. Selections from them form the first half of Falkland Sound/Voces de Malvinas, the new show at the Theatre Upstairs.

Two actors appear before the window in the theatre's back wall, which gives on to real summer greenery abetted by the contents of cunningly placed flowerpots. It makes a highly affecting image of the England for which David Tinker officially died, and of the part of it from which he actually came. Paul Jesson's schoolmasterly Hugh is almost part of the scenery: all quiet and sensible moderation. David, fresh and enthusiastic, goes from public school to a naval career, with a spell at Birmingham University.

It is of course moving to hear of the hopes and affections of a young man we know to be under sentence of death. It is made almost unbearable by the gradual revelation that he had no belief in the cause for which he was fighting.

His first reaction to the prospect of "wog-bashing" in the Falklands is that it sounds "very exciting," but experience of combat cures him of that. Yes, the ideal of defending the islanders is fine, but he finds the diplomacy inconsistent, the tactics inept, and the politics cynically exploitative. "They shouldn't," he says ruefully, "send naval ratings to university to read History". Perhaps he was lucky to have been spared the general election.

It is fair, I suppose, to point out that David Tinker's disillusion was not widely shared in the Falklands task force, and certainly life can rarely have handed the Royal Court audience so congenial a hero: not one of us, but agreeing with us. There is great dignity, though, in Max Stafford-Clark's production, and Julian Wadham's David is achingly believable.

The sequel consists of five intercut monologues, derived from interviews conducted with people connected with the campaign, but not part of it: Falklands dependants, perhaps. Though the war hangs over everything they say, they offer little actual comment on it; Lieutenant Tinker has given all we are to get in the way of protest, though a reporter with the fleet (Mr Jesson again) does offer a few caustic bemused comments on terminal imperialism.

He also tells us of a book he has "seriously toyed with" (sic), depicting "scenes at the margin of the news," the kind that get "squeezed out" of the usual reports. This speech was not incorporated until the last preview: odd, since it is the key to the piece.

Marion Bailey plays a British teacher who spent 20 unhappily married years in the islands; she vividly sketches the stifling culture of the expatriate establishment, and her own political awakening. It has no bearing on the ethics of the war but it is fascinating.

A South American actress, Stella Maris, plays an Argentine girl living in Britain and amazed at our passivity. An Anglo-Argentine businessman gives Mr Wadham a chance to show he can play oily as well as wide-eyed, but he is the sketchiest presence.

The fullest is Lesley Manville as the CND wife of a task-force sailor: clearly a mésalliance, though we notice that she may marry again, this time a soldier. As much an anomaly in her world as David Tinker in his, she counterbalances him by recalling the xenophobic callousness of her ex-husband and his mates: the sleeping beast that Mrs Thatcher awoke. She is a very recognisable person.

I daresay that a dramatist could have drawn all this material towards a central point: made it centripetal instead of centrifugal. I dare say, but I do not really believe, and I am anyway far from sure that it would be an improvement. Much more than an improvised play like Crystal Clear, this compilation calls in question the necessity of the playwright (though a good one, Louise Page, edited the text).

A television documentary might offer us the same material, but it would be less compelling, since it would deny us the pleasures of impersonation: bold in outline, delicate in shading. I hate making exaggerated claims, and I am sure you never believe them, so let me merely say that this timely and informative entertainment is one of the least boring things in London.

* * *

Willy Russell's Rita, asked in the course of her education to suggest a solution to the problems of staging Peer Gynt, said, "Do it on the radio." It seems a logical way of dealing with all that violent, extravagant action, all those shifting scenes and supernatural personages, but it also seems a cheat. The best thing to do with the unstageable is to stage it, if only for the perversity of the thing; what Dr Johnson should really have said is that a dog walking on its hind legs is excellent showbusiness. (I'm not so sure about a woman preaching.)

The next best, or next worst, thing to radio is a studio theatre such as the Pit, where Ron Daniels solves most of the problems by pretending they do not exist. The opening Norwegian episodes become scenes of Irish peasant life. The African travels are done before a screen bearing sketchy projections of Peer's ship, Memnon's statue and the Sphinx. The last wanderings at death's door are played sternly, in a suggestion of fog.

It is all very efficient, but much of the life of the text has drained out. For all its symbolic complications, the play is a decoration of a fairly simple point about self-realisation; the decorations need to be fun in themselves. Though every scene is played here, many--notably the fight with the Great Boyg--pass by without making any point. It seems rather a short evening; and that is not a compliment.

David Rudkin's curt translation is perfectly good in its own right, but just as it took the couplets of Richard Wilbur and Tony Harrison to bring Molière alive for us, so it will be necessary for somebody to put this mutant folk-satire back into rhyme for its joy to be released in English.

As far as anybody can be miscast as Peer Gynt, Derek Jacobi is. He has some very fine moments--such as his first and last meetings with Solveig--but they are all emotional. As a comedian he is, essentially, knowing: not really at home as the country boy, and too much at home as the rich sophisticate. He looks dashing in his Valentino prophet's robes; Peer should look ridiculous. (The great British Peers--Ralph Richardson, Peter Ustinov, Leo McKern--have all been ungainly: in a plump comic tradition of which the current inheritor is Simon Callow.)

There are some good performances on the circumference: Katy Behean's Solveig, Brenda Peters's Aase (earthy, but underpowered), Sinead Cusack brilliant as the one-night (and one-scene) stand called Ingrid, Josette Simon fine as Anitra and finer still as an amorous herd-girl, Nigel Hawthorne as two messengers of death, one sober and one fantastical.

Speaking of which, two powerful intimations of mortality--Ghelderode's Escorial and Pirandello's The Man with the Flower in His Mouth--are at the Upstream; Tim Woodward is admirable in both, especially the downbeat second.
