Camp and clicket

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Peter Nichols's new play at the Aldwych.

THE Royal Shakespeare Company is back in Singapore. Two years ago, in Charles Wood's Jingo, it showed us how the British in World War Two absent-mindedly succumbed to what one of their number called "the little yellow men." Peter Nichols's Privates on Parade (Aldwych) now takes us on to 1948, His Majesty's Forces determined not to get caught the same way twice.

Particularly determined is the CO, Major Flack, who grimly anticipates a Communist onslaught composed of little yellow cyclists, all going "ting-a-ling, velly solly, no more clicket now." The major is a bit hazy about speech patterns of other races, and indeed of other ranks. He means well, rather like Alan Bennett's earnest vicar in "Beyond the Fringe," acknowledging how often his flock must be tempted to say "Oh, stuff this for a lark." Home for the major is a seventeenth-century mill-house, which he cites as a symbol of the everyday British life for which they are fighting. Nigel Hawthorne's performance, sternly fatuous and decidedly likeable, could hardly be bettered.

As is usual in service comedies, the men are clowns and the officer an uncomprehending stooge. But this time the distinction is more than metaphorical; since we are concerned with an entertainments unit whose contribution to freedom is the production of--in every sense--camp concerts. Double-entendres are the Order of the Day, beginning with the title; though strictly speaking the lubricious reading is here the literal one, for nobody appears on stage below the rank of a corporal.

In charge of operations is a rampantly homosexual captain, doing his bit out East since back home "life under Clementina Attlee wasn't exactly the Roman Empire." Denis Quilley, tempering his usual robust enthusiasm with a certain primness, storms gaily through impersonations of Marlene Dietrich, Vera Lynn and Carmen Miranda before coming to rest as a surprisingly fleshly Noel Coward (actually he looks more like Donald Sinden) icily inquiring, "Could you please inform us how we came to lose the war/That we lost in 1945?" If you can mentally combine "There Are Bad Times Just Around the Corner" with "Could You Please Oblige Us with a Bren Gun" you will have the right idea.

Certainly the author and his composer (Denis King) have it, here and throughout; with so many of our playwrights attempting to turn themselves into lyricists, Mr Nichols is ahead of the field. He remains supreme, too, as a fashioner of one-liners, dead in character and dead funny.

The play is, however, very long and decidedly unwieldy. Mr Quilley, who is its particular glory, has hardly anything to do with the plot and so is often absent. The story comes in two interlocking sections. There is a love-element, played with a nice mixture of passion and bewilderment by Emma Williams as a half-caste (part Indian, part Welsh, imagine the accent) and Ian Gelder, who impersonated the youthful Mr Nichols in Forget-Me-Not-Lane and is here, I imagine, continuing the biography. There is also a sinister bit concerning a villainous sergeant-major, a source of bafflement to author, actor, director and me.

In theory, all these scenes are stylised--dramatic "numbers" in the concert-party to balance the musical ones--but the idea is scuppered by Mr Nichols's frequent recourse to passages of extended naturalism. The framework vanishes, if not from the stage, then certainly from the audience's consciousness. I doubt its necessity anyway; the device, for all its fun, represents a step backwards for Mr Nichols. There are some dead spots, too, in Michael Blakemore's production, though there is also the expected verve in theatrical effects and the accustomed precision in the ranks: note Simon Jones's pugnacious public school boy and the mannish lance-corporal of the invaluable Tim Wylton.

* * *

At the Royal Court there have been premieres upstairs and downstairs. (I shudder to think what is going on in my lady's chamber.) In the attic there is Tunde Ikoli's Short Sleeves in the Summer, a prolonged marital row. Black boy wants out of the factory, to the uncomprehending disgust of pregnant black girl. She begins every complaint with "Well, I'm sorry but..." which is so true to life that one starts to yearn for a little artifice. The play, however accurately observed, remains obstinately simple, its naivety emphasised by Michael Joyce's abrupt direction but belied occasionally by the alert performance of Gregory Munroe.

Down below is Devil's Island, a first stage play by Tony Bicat, brought in by Joint Stock. It is a tripartite view of the sinking British middle class: perturbed in 1937 (post-Spain), disintegrating in 1977 (post-Concho), half-bestial in 1997 (post-holocaust). The same six actors recur in all three sections, portraying roughly similar characters and retaining the same names. Thus in each period there is a man called Kutchevski, always a jump ahead of those less exotically known as Hugh or Bill or Betty, always some kind of agent-provocateur, always a man of the world, though whether of a desirable world is left discreetly in doubt.

Episode One is the best, perhaps because its setting is, in recent theatrical experience, the least familiar. The actors helped to plan, though not actually to write, the play and they seem vulnerable to stereotypes: the Present comes over as a hip Onedin Line, and the Future (in which the survivors eat dog-food if available and dog if not) is anyone's apocalypse, from the fringe of five years ago. Mr Bicat's dialogue, which starts out both elegant and electric, decays along with the rest of civilisation. In terms of performance, actors' involvement certainly pays off (Gillian Barge, Simon Callow and Jane Wood in particular); and David Hare's production, though occasionally mannered, is very clean and very good. A white sheet spread across the stage makes a dazzling tablecloth for a dinner-party scene; the diners, their debate and their reactions stand out in the sharpest relief. Mr Bicat's view of the British class system, though more overtly grim, is not perhaps far different from Mr Nichols's; his jokes, though, are less adroit.

* * *

Joint Stock productions tend to run a gamut from earnest worry to icy disdain; they have turned away from the stentorian commitment of The Sell-Out (Library Theatre, Manchester), whose authors, Roger Smith and Tom Kempinski, are so confident of their cause that they forget to plead it. In arraigning Ramsay MacDonald for betraying Labour by hanging out with the rich (who idle around, as is customary, having sex and things) they suggest, not that real socialist policies might have worked, but that MacDonald should have resigned and let the Tories do the dirty work necessitated by the slump. Baldwin's refusal to let his opponent put this plan into action occasions the only scene in the evening with any political bite; but the show is still a jolly one, and the many musical numbers are handled with unusual efficiency.

* * *

In Edith Piaf Je Vous Aime (King's Head) Libby Morris, producer and star, gives herself little opportunity to do what she does best, i.e., sing very funny very fast. Maureen Scott gets closer to Piaf's chirpy vulnerability, and a new lyric by the admirable Fran Landesman to "La Goulante de Pauvre Jean" should ensure future oblivion for the "Poor People of Paris" in whose name this song has previously been impaled on the honky-tonk pianos of the nation.
