THE PRESS looked a bit sheepish as it filed into Salad Days (Duke of York's). Most of it was weighed down with memories. The Evening Standard had reviewed the original production; had disliked it, and had been regarded - until enlightened opinion caught up with him - as a notorious curmudgeon for his pains.

The Observer also recalled that production, which he had seen when he was about 12. He had loved it then, but he had come to believe that 12 was the optimum age for a spectator of this show and that reaction had long set in. Still, nostalgia is a strange bloom and he was perhaps as likely to be re-enchanted as disgusted. On the whole, it was a test he would rather not face; particularly as it was likely to have no definite outcome and to leave him sitting, dispiritedly as usual, on the fence.

It is in fact from that fence that I now address you. Parts of Salad Days hold up well; others do not. The present production has some pleasing performances, but is not very good.

Salad Days, as you too may remember, is about a boy and girl, just down from three suspiciously idyllic years at what seems to be Cambridge, which they follow up with a very similar month in London. The time is the early fifties, when the sun shone and birds sang in the park and that sort of thing. It is in the park that Jane and Timothy spend their time: he avoiding work, she (a reluctant deb fractionally before her time) avoiding marriage.

The first problem is solved when they take temporary possession of a magic piano, so constituted that all who hear it break into frenzied dance-steps which involve the transfer of money from their pockets into an upturned hat, thereby providing Jane and Timothy with a living wage. The second is settled by having the couple marry in the hope that they will subsequently fall in love; which, as the show proceeds, they unobtrusively do. (With each other.)

So far, you may be surprised to hear, so good: the romance is pleasant and uncomplicated, the magic piano is an inoffensive and efficient piece of whimsy, and the romance uncomplicated and likeable. It is buoyed up by three ballads which, to make no great claim for them, are as beguiling as anything heard in a British musical since the war. Thus far we have a piece of juvenilia as well worth your attention as Very Good Eddie.

But Julian Slade, though we have to look at their subsequent careers for proof of this, was no Jerome Kern. He filled this score out with chorus numbers of jigging, undistinguished cheerfulness; one or two of them (any one or two, really) would be all right, but a batch of them clogs up the evening. Such charm as they have is anyway destroyed in this production by the emergence from the pit of a droning mechanical noise, attributable to what the programme calls a Keyboard.

Something similar happens to the script, which is padded out with a succession of unrevivable revue sketches. These are now mainly entrusted to Elizabeth Seal, who looks alarmed, and Sheila Steafel, who looks dismissive (a more effective strategy). The leads, Adam Bareham and Christina Matthews, are unobjectionable, except on some of her high notes, and if there were still a great future in playing debs' delights, Osmund Bullock, as perfectly poised as named, would surely have one. But the honours on the first night went to Malcolm Rennie and Ian Talbot, playing policemen, for gallantry in the face of an unfunctioning property gramophone.

I trust their difficulties were real: I would hardly know what to think of a production which went to the lengths of faking inefficiency. It was anyway typical of the rest of the show, a rest frequently disturbed by the thudding of a remarkably graceless drop-curtain. The show kept stalling; it lacked flow. (That's a line I recall from the original; the reply was "I'm sorry, sir, it's Flo's day off." Why did they cut that? As bad remains behind.)

I remember the original as being more wittily performed; it had the benefit of a good rep cast. It began as an end-of-term romp in Bristol. But spiritually it was a transplanted Cambridge show; it reeks of the Footlights of the early fifties or even - to judge from their last offering to arrive in London - of the early seventies. Maybe, in this era of laissez-faire desperation in the West End, its time has come again.

* * *

Considering that it deals with a mad German writer of the eighteenth century rather than a sane English grocer of the twentieth, Mike Stott's Lenz (Hampstead) bears a surprising resemblance to his Funny Peculiar. In both, a supposedly adventurous spirit is set in the context of a remote village and found wanting. Mr Stott sets his German community to speaking modern (but never clangorously anachronistic) Yorkshire with remarkable success.

The play possesses abundant local life (my own salad days are receding, but I think I have the Eng. Lit. jargon right), but is gravely deficient in overall structure. Not even Jonathan Pryce, despite a new disturbing gentleness, can hold us merely by loosing off a series of unexplained tirades, though Lenz's frequent quotations from Hamlet afford a useful preview of what will plainly be one of the great inevitable performances of the next few years. There is an excellent sour and sturdy performance by Derek Smith, free of the sing-song mannerisms which have affected his work at Stratford.

* * *

The London theatre having conspired to deny me a chance of ever seeing The Country Wife, I went to the Cambridge Arts to see it done by the company from the Oxford Playhouse. Wycherley's comedy has always struck me in the text as surpassingly moral; none of its rakes, innocents, cuckolds, whores and hypocrites gets less than his due. This production did not exactly destroy this impression for me; indeed it did nothing exactly. It was an evening of approximations. It was a faithful production; unless, as I am inclined to do, you count sloth and dowdiness as betrayals. As Restoration comedies go, this one is beautifully constructed, but if it once slackens its grasp it can be interminable.
