Waiting for Hickey

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on revivals of O'Neill, Chekhov, Coward and Feydeau.

O'NEILL'S The Iceman Cometh (Aldwych) is the fountainhead of postwar American drama, the archetype of all the plays in which characters sit in bars or cafés or roadside diners or junkies' pads, interact and wait. It also has a considerable number of relatives living abroad; Samuel Beckett's bums waiting for Godot are in much the same predicament as O'Neill's waiting for Hickey, the travelling salesman. Beckett, however, let his characters off lightly; Godot never arrives. Hickey does show up and his acolytes barely survive his coming. That finally they do survive it is the cruellest thing in the play.

Iceman represents the underside of pop Americana: of Damon Runyon and of Clap hands, here comes Charlie. See the smile on all those hungry faces. They can tell that he's a buyer from the wide and open spaces. The men in Harry Hope's saloon, New York 1912, have permanently thirsty faces and Harry himself is usually pickled enough for his clients to grab themselves all the booze they want. Hickey's arrival will save them the trouble of scrounging; besides, a fresh face is always welcome if you never go out of doors.

O'Neill, writing in 1939, was again recalling his own youth. The claim most frequently made for him is that having touched bottom himself he was able to portray his fellows without any touch of patronage. Exploitation, though, can take many forms, and however much O'Neill may have respected his characters he had few qualms about moving them abruptly from one convention to another. Having set them up as individuals he turns them in his last act into a chorus; suddenly they start talking in unison. Harry becomes the chorus leader and Norman Rodway, who has kept his nose infallibly to the ground in Howard Davies's production, suddenly starts to intone. Visually Mr Davies manages the transition well; the drained, battered faces - 15 in a row - look like a rotting tapestry. Aurally it grates, and for the first time O'Neill's notorious deficiencies as a writer (generally more noticeable on the page than the stage) begin to obtrude.

The jolt is nasty, but the play survives it. Partly it does so by virtue of its length; it plays for four and a quarter hours. O'Neill gives himself room to stumble and get up again; and while a bad long play is insupportable, a good one is, from the audience's point of view, addictive. And because they give the actors a chance to dig their heels in, extended group pieces like this are almost invariably well performed.

The massive structure gives O'Neill the space to grade his characters. All of them are victims of a single disease; unable to face reality, each protects himself with a pipe-dream. At the centre is Harry, believing that one day he will take a walk outside. Around him are his clients, part refugees, part spongers, each with a past that serves also as an imaginary future.

Regarding them with pity and contempt is Larry, whose lack of illusion is itself an illusion. He is generally taken for the play's commentator, but even he is overseen by the barman, Rocky, the play's nearest approach to a man of the world; after all, he has a job, he can leave when he's not working and if he runs a couple of whores, they are only a sideline. His illusions are the hardest to crack because hardest to pin down. As Rob Hoskins plays him, Rocky is almost the centre of the play; certainly he is its pacemaker.

To them comes Hickey in the guise of an evangelist; he has seen through his own pipe-dream and wishes to cure them of theirs. He persuades each of them to try to realise his dream; predictably they all come back to the saloon, sobbing. They and the world have no use for each other. But this is Hickey's plan; what he has come to spread is not happiness but misery. (He deceives himself about this of course: he has merely traded one pipe-dream for another. O'Neill's philosophy may have been schematic, but its application necessarily involved him in greater subtleties than he may have envisaged.) The ground keeps opening. He has killed his wife. For a moment he even faces the fact that he hated her; then he lies to deny it, and his denial sets the bums free. They can claim that he was mad, that they obeyed him only to humour him, and that everything is as it was. If you like happy endings, hurry to the Aldwych.

* * *

The last developments can be hard to follow; O'Neill seems to regard illusion and reality as solid wedges to be moved around like chessmen. What is more, all the characters seem to know the rules. There is a parallel action involving young Parritt, who has shopped his mother as Hickey has killed his wife, and who comes to confess to Larry as Hickey comes to confess to the bums.

This is the area least well handled in the writing and in this production. Kenneth Cranham, usually an attacking actor, seems ill-at-ease with Parritt's whimpering passivity and plight. Patrick Stewart easily holds the stage; he does not make Larry's mental processes any too interesting. The rest, in Chris Dyer's set, high and wide if not handsome, is splendour. Alan Tilvern's Hickey is a perfect messenger of death, with slicked-down hair, slicked-down voice, and features locked in the permanent sneer bequeathed by years of successful salesmanship. The others - especially John Warner, Gary Bond and Richard Simpson - make their lurchings between the bottle and the door horribly involving. In its detail, more than its theory, the play rubs a universal sore; and in its attitude to hope (it exists in the saloon as in the proprietor's name and it depends on deception) it satirises tragedy, making it the archetypal modern play.

* * *

Not the greatest though: that is Chekhov's Three Sisters, which at the Cambridge is accorded, in Jonathan Miller's production transferred from Guildford, by far its best performance in my experience. There is at least one transcendent sequence: Natasha's proprietorial stalk around the sisters' bedroom in the third act is satirically reprised by Irena. Olga and Masha fall about; the trio are a laughing unity and the harmony prompts Masha to tell, still laughing, of her love for Vershinin.

Janet Suzman is magnificent as Masha; her reactions throughout are pointed but never planted. The flow of her performance is virtually unbroken. Nigel Davenport, partnering her, helps her to make sense; he is fleshily attractive, opportunist, a bit shabby. He philosophises because that is the thing to do, and he covertly laughs at those (like the doomed Trozonbach) who do it seriously.

The play takes shape, too, as an outsiders' revenge; Natasha and Solyony, both snubbed in the first act, naturally cause havoc later. June Ritchie's Natasha, in particular, is excellently rounded. Angela Down's Irena is refreshingly hard; she does not languish for Moscow, she merely wants it to hurry up. Mr Miller sacrifices intermediate climaxes (one or two, to be honest, I rather missed) for the sake of the last outburst, which comes hard and fast. Susan Engel puts more despair into Olga's last speech than I have ever heard before. Sebastian Shaw's doctor and Antony Brown's schoolmaster are particularly distinguished.

* * *

Blithe Spirit (Lyttelton) turns out more ghostly than might have been intended. Harold Pinter's production firmly outlines the marital discomfort at the base of the play, but he has little opportunity to build on it; and both Coward's prose and his artistic snobbery, the central character is a novelist with little good to say of anyone who isn't, seem petty and, what is worse, routine. A great Madame Arcati might save the day, as she saved the film, but Elizabeth Spriggs is no Margaret Rutherford. She has played too many eccentrics to conquer: the part from without, not enough to take it from within.

Maria Aitken has the right style - compounded of a witty drawl and a long, long back - for Elvira; Rowena Cooper deals professionally with Ruth's good lines, but cannot salvage the bad, which predominate; and Richard Johnson is dull. I think the production should be faster, but I imagine Mr Pinter trusts the text more than I do.

* * *

Nobody involved in Signed and Sealed (Comedy) seems to trust it at all, which is understandable. It is the least inventive farce ever to reach our stage under the name of Feydeau. If I had a line or two left, I could even summarise its plot.
