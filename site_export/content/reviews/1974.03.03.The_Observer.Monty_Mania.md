Monty mania

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

IF Monty Python's First Farewell Tour (Drury Lane) were not the funniest show in London, something would be seriously amiss. Well, it is the funniest show in London, and something is still seriously amiss.

Two bits in the show made me weep with laughter. The first was an Olympiad which involved a Marathon race for men with weak bladders and an aquatic event for non-swimmers, who dived into the pool and did nothing else. Ever. The second was also of a sporting nature, being a football match, Ancient Greek philosophers v. modern German. (Rather a dull game, owing to the tendency of both teams to stand around arguing.) Both sequences were on film.

The experience of watching them was therefore not dissimilar to watching "Monty Python" on a very large colour television screen, in the company of a correspondingly large number of devotees. Though I malign them: they were not devotees, they were sycophantic maniacs.

Now, one of the charms of watching these film clips, as of watching television, is that you can mentally phase out your fellow viewers; but you can't ignore the audience during the live sketches. The skits are mostly familiar (though surprisingly few of them would rate inclusion in my personal anthology, the Pith of Python), loved and learned by rote from TV runs and reruns and from the movie. Like pop fans cheering the opening bars of a favourite song, so Tuesday's house howled when Eric Idle stressed, heavily, that he wanted to be a lumberjack or when John Cleese wandered on carrying a cage containing a dead parrot. (To be fair, the first time he does this he wanders straight off again, the celebrated parrot number being reserved for much later; this teaser is the only point at which the show recaptures the joyously eccentric continuity of its TV original.) However brilliant the material, it cannot survive transformation into a rite for initiates only.

Live comedy depends on danger, on contact, and on unpredictability: it demands an alert but critical audience. To have one's defences breached by a great clown is a marvellous experience, but the defences have to be there.

Anyway, with one and a half exceptions these are not really clowns; they are ideas-men who have conquered one medium, but look lost outside it. On stage the pecking order becomes unwontedly (and unwantedly) clear: Mr Cleese is very funny, and Mr Idle is quite funny, and Graham Chapman is a bit funny, and the others are indistinguishable and screech a lot. This last tendency is emphasised, and my antagonism exacerbated, by the murderous amplification and the cavernous incongruity of the theatre itself. Even Mr Cleese's personality loses some of its flavour under these circumstances.

* * *

Messrs Cleese and Idle might be amusingly cast in the opening scene of David Rudkin's No Title (presented by the Birmingham Repertory Theatre's Brum Studio), in which a nervous young man is shown round his new flat by a tall, beaky supercilious caretaker. Once his guide has departed, the tenant, Mr Bisthorpe, sets about altering those features of his lodging which are not to his taste. Principally he is affronted by some very drab lampshades; his ensuing battle with the light-fittings, permanently uncertain whether he has pulled the cord which operates them to the On or Off position, provides a delicious sequence of silent comedy.

The comedy continues, but grows less silent and less delicious. Though Bisthorpe's flat was advertised as being "self-contained," it affords no cooking facilities. The house to which it is attached boasts a communal kitchen, but when he wanders into it four cooks, already at work there, set upon him, apparently with murderous intent. They seem mad; and gradually it dawns upon him and upon us that he is indeed in an asylum, presided over by a formidable, and totally unsympathetic, lady doctor. His four assailants reappear as the Institution's board of governors.

Is Bisthorpe a sane man who, like Peer Gynt, has wandered into a madhouse where the inmates have taken control? Or has he actually been committed and are we seeing events as they appear to him in his mania? Mr Rudkin keeps his options open for a very long time, then reveals that neither is applicable and that we have here a dramatic metaphor for a Britain, out of control and devoted to dispossessing its inhabitants. Bisthorpe may think he has a tenant's rights, but in fact he has none: No Title.

I remember the kitchen scene from a television play some years back; the rest of it has fled my memory so I cannot say how much of the present work is really new. My impression is that Mr Rudkin rather hastily grafted a few topical jabs on to a scheme he had originally found unworkable. The board tell Bisthorpe that it does not matter if he can no longer afford meat; indeed it doesn't really matter if he starves. There is talk of power-cuts and of "the need for a human face."

The satire and the symbolism may be crude, but they are wrenchingly powerful. Mr Rudkin can stage action that lives in its own right and his verbal command is unmatched by any other current playwright. I prefer his punning here to his poeticising in Ashes; it creates a world and gives us an agonising involvement in Bisthorpe's struggles, trapped in a cage of words. "Oh witty . . . witty Dr Kings," he gasps at his chief tormentor, and tries to erect his own pedantic verbal barricade against her. Tormented by Muzak, he refers to it as an "intravenous assault on my auditory privacy." "Who," he asks, when finally reduced, "can have pity for a man who merely wakes up to find his value fallen in the night?" Colin Farrell has the full measure of his desperation, though some other roles could do with stronger playing; more particularly, with older playing. Don't tell me that this is difficult in rep; I know.

* * *

Like Mr Rudkin, Sam Shepard can create potent theatrical images; unlike him he has no gift for suspense; and though Geography of a Horse Dreamer (Theatre Upstairs) is a more accessible work, by word, than some of his previous work, its total import baffles me. It concerns an American prophet of horse-racing results, who is transported blindfold to England by a couple of gangsters and then ignominiously relegated to the dog-tracks. Finally mayhem erupts. Some warning may be intended about the dangers of uprooting a dreamer, or anyone else, from his nourishing native soil. One incompetent performance, and several effortful ones, perhaps render the play more portentous than it need be, but Bob Hoskins shines out as one of the few British actors one would happily cast in Guys and Dolls.
