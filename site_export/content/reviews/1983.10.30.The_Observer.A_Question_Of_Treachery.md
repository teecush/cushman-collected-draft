A question of treachery

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a new play about the Kroger case.

HUGH WHITEMORE is best known as a television playwright and his Pack of Lies (Lyric) apparently originated as a television play. It bears some of the marks. Its action shifts back and forth between the kitchen and living-room of a suburban couple. They, their teenage daughter and their neighbours make up more than half the cast. It would be tempting to describe the environment, and the play itself, as cosy. It would also be terribly wrong.

Mr Whitemore takes us back to 1960, to the Portland spy case: the arrest of Gordon Lonsdale and his American aides, the Krogers. His central characters are the Jacksons, who live opposite the Krogers in Ruislip and — slightly to their own surprise — have been enthusiastically befriended by them.

Their house is commandeered by MI5 for use as an observation post. It is all very politely done; but the Jacksons are given no real choice in the matter, and before they know where they are, they are being reminded of the existence of the Official Secrets Act.

At first they are not told that their friends are the suspects; when they do know, the Krogers are about to be arrested, and they are faced with the fact that it is partly their responsibility. They are impaled on the old Forsterian dilemma — to betray one's friend or one's country — and Mr Whitemore is a lot less glib about the resolution than Forster was.

Amazingly, the play has been interpreted as a naive attack on British intelligence for using the same dirty tricks of which it accuses the Russians. Well, Mr Whitemore does make a few sallies against native officialdom, but his main concern is with a subtler kind of treachery: that indulged in, against their wills, by the Jacksons themselves. It isn't really their fault, but they suffer for it. (Oedipus had the same problem.) He is especially concerned with Mrs Jackson, since it is she who worries and protests and finally cracks.

Here, admittedly, Mr Whitemore has a great advantage. He has Judi Dench to play Mrs Jackson. Other actresses could probably establish cheerful, nervous suburban motherhood as efficiently as Miss Dench does, but I doubt if any could match her for anger and anguish. Her emotions seem to be coming up from her belly and they hang, immovably and disturbingly, in the air of the theatre.

Miss Dench seems an effusive actress, but she is quite ruthless at paring-down. Mr Whitemore gives each of his characters a monologue: a dubious device, but hers is a good one, about her mother-in-law who lived in terror of authority, so that it rode right over her. She plays it not for pity, but for determination (it won't happen to her) and entrapment (it already has). And she has an unforgettable moment at the end of the first act when she learns the worst; she has been painting, and her body slumps over the easel. It is the profile of shock, beautifully held in Clifford Williams's excellent production.

Michael Williams, drilling furrows in his brow and holes in his pockets, makes a fine worried partner, and Eva Griffith is a highly credible schoolgirl. Richard Vernon is the essence of Whitehall, and Barbara Leigh-Hunt is the female Kroger, dangerous even when ingratiating. (Her American accent impressed me until it transpired that she was meant to be Canadian. Then she turns out to be American impersonating Canadian. But Larry Hoodekoff, who plays her husband, really is Canadian. It makes the head spin.)

Last week I castigated David Edgar's Maydays for not bringing on stage the 'human dust' — the politically discounted classes — to which it frequently alludes. Mr Whitemore does bring them on, and he shows the chariot wheels passing over them and then forgetting. Mr Jackson acquiesces, and Mrs Jackson gets no thanks, but they keep some dignity.

The dialogue is somewhat grey. This is not the best play in London, but it is the most moving. And it does what we are always being told popular theatre should do: takes a despised genre — in this case soap-opera — and reactivates it.

* * *

It has of course a lot of fashionable strikes against it. Not only is its author from television, and its characters lower middle-class, but it has opened in the West End. So, the following night, did Charles Dyer's Lovers Dancing, a much more adventurous choice; you have to be adventurous to put on so bizarrely unworkable a play. It comes with heavy acting insurance. Paul Eddington, Colin Blakely, Georgina Hale, Jane Carr. I kept repeating their names to ward off the evil spirits. Paul and Colin and Georgina and Jane. Like Bob and Carol and Ted and Alice.

They play two married couples who, like the aforesaid, once attempted an orgy. From it a child was born, its paternity uncertain. So all four meet every year on its birthday, for the sake of fairness, though Mr Blakely and Miss Hale have done far better out of life, on the surface, than the other two. (The living-quarters Carl Toms has designed for them look baronial.) They reminisce, mainly about sexual failure.

Mr Dyer can turn a phrase, numbing though they become, and Mr Eddington can time almost anything. Miss Hale has so many mannerisms that the actress, never mind the character, almost vanishes, but she can plead self-defence, and she exits with a tirade that brought her some justified applause.

* * *

Compared with this, Hay Fever (Queen's) exudes the pleasant glow of mediocrity. If we are really experiencing a West End revival, as these successive openings indicate, I would like all concerned to take their courage in their hands and try to get through a whole year without doing a single Coward play. It looks too much like trading on past glories, especially since his four revivable comedies must come round in far too rapid rotation.

Penelope Keith as Judith Bliss makes a bossy star rather than a feathery one, but she shares Mr Eddington's ability to make almost any line funny; he does it by keeping us waiting, she by casually unfamiliar emphasis: on, for example, the line about sex and shrimps-nets, not previously one of my favourites. Her supports range from the anonymous to the insupportable, except in the two smallest roles, Abigail McKern being a neatly intimidated flapper and Elizabeth Bradley a dresser-turned-housekeeper who pads about in a ferocious world of her own.

* * *

When I first read Vanbrugh's The Relapse (Lyric, Hammersmith), I thought it was full of misprints. There was Lord Foppington, saying 'praud' instead of proud, and addressing his brother Tom as 'Tam.' When I grew up and became sophisticated, I realised that these were the basis of a full-length portrait of a fop, and a gift to the right actor.

This Simon Callow obviously is (the only disappointment with his performance is that we have seen so much of it already in Edward Bond's Restoration) and he erects a whole Byzantine structure of mispronunciation; on this occasion he is the architect, Vanbrugh a mere bricklayer. He is, as a colleague has noted, much the most likeable character on the stage, partly because he has pace. William Gaskill directs with expected taste and intelligence, but of the others only Nicky Henson really knows how to drive the dialogue.
