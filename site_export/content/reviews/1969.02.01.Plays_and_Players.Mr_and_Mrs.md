Mr and Mrs

I see from the Sunday papers that *Mr and Mrs* is (or, if you like, *Mr and Mrs* are) to be retained for a second week in the West End. Heaven knows to what a pass this lamentable state of affairs may have attained by the time you read this; but, ever an optimist, I shall compose the remainder of this review in the past tense.

These, then, were attempts to musicalise what the programme, with unendearing vagueness, referred to as “the plays by Noël Coward”. *Mr* in fact derived from *Fumed Oak* and *Mrs* from *Still Life*, and there was no point in trying to disguise the fact, since the bones of each were quite clearly visible through the encrusting fat. You may skip the next paragraph, which is a digression on the advisability of turning plays into musicals.

One assumes that it is being done all the time but as a matter of fact it isn't, and for such forbearance the showmen of the world have only my admiration. Assuming the original to be a workable theatrical mechanism, it is going to get in the way. It can, of course, be spoofed—Shakespeare lends itself very well to this treatment as *The Boys from Syracuse* and *Kiss Me Kate* and (I assume) *Your Own Thing* will bear witness. It can be systematically and ruthlessly rebuilt—as we have seen this year in *Cabaret* and *Golden Boy*. But it gets you nowhere merely to punch a few holes in a previously viable structure and plug them with the odd bout of song and dance. *My Fair Lady* admittedly got away with doing just that but then it's never been one of my favourite musicals, possibly because it contravenes all my theories.

At all events it's a loner. *Mr and Mrs* was more representative of the results to be expected from slavish opportunism. *Mr* was the more endurable; at least its central joke—a seemingly docile, downtrodden husband rounding on and forsaking his horrible wife, daughter and mother-in-law—remained recognisable. It went off at half-cock though, for a variety of interlocked reasons.

One was the doughy quality of John Taylor's lyrics which—as is the way of musicals—took over from Mr Coward's original dialogue at crucial and climactic points. It would, of course, take a very brilliant lyricist (Noël Coward for example) to stand the implied comparison, but it's no use whimpering, Mr Taylor should have thought of that in the first place. Another was the acting. Honor Blackman and (surprisingly) Hylda Baker were only half-heartedly awful (or awfully half-hearted), and with such opposition the worm had not far enough to turn. And John Neville is hardly a wormlike actor; he might not have been able to eat that family for breakfast, but he could certainly have disposed of them long before lunch. So his moment of escape by becoming predictable was robbed of some of its glory—not all, since it resolved itself into a sprightly song-and-dance routine which the actor obviously loved doing, and with enough justification to make us enjoy it too. Only the chorus jarred, for their presence was a reminder of the shameless and snobbish inflationary tactics for which they had been utilised earlier; the programme dubbed them “The Faceless Society” and they were meant to illustrate the pitiful universality of the main characters and their drab little lives. They were also, of course, used as padding.

On which cue, enter *Mrs*, a protracted immolation of what was once *Still Life*, then became *Brief Encounter* and here wound up as a classic of shoddiness. Sizeable portions of the original duologues cropped pointlessly up, looking very lonely. It is just possible to see them as the basis of a chamber musical, given the right kind of bitter-sweet (I don't mean *Bitter Sweet*) score, and sensitive period staging. There is no point in dragging it into the 1960s while the memory of the film (which for most people, even those who haven't seen it, symbolises the entire British cinema of the forties) still remains potent. There is certainly no point in encumbering it with lachrymose ballads which are not even wholeheartedly schmaltzy enough to make good Humperdinck fodder. Above all, there is no point, having retained so much of the original, in harnessing it to a couple of other love-stories, thrown in for multiple audience appeal. For the mums and dads, the elderly courtship and eventual union of a station buffet lady and an engine-driver, enacted with many a knees-up. For the kids, and in some desperation, a much younger buffet lady and her teenage boyfriend, pretexts for the show to take a few tottering steps underground and to come up with a light-show of devastating feebleness: Sunday Night at the Palladium Turns On.

Still, all credit to author-composer-lyricist John Taylor and his triple pronged attack; not every show can manage to reach three separate nadirs in only an hour. As an anthology of all that is worst and apparently (despite the Beatles) irreducible in British pop, it had a certain point. Actually there were one or two moments when the score developed an appealing brassiness, and if I attribute these to the orchestrations of Johnnie Spence, one of the best big-band writers in the country, I am probably being unjust.

So I'm unjust.

I have no idea where Mr Taylor's musical heart may lie: possibly in the Billy Cotton Band Show. Certainly Alan Breeze as the engine-driver seemed more at ease than anyone else on stage. John Neville did his sensitive best by such stray gobbets of Coward as came his way. As a singer (rather than, as in the first half, a putter-over of numbers) he proved uneven; having sailed with apparently justified confidence through three-quarters of a phrase he would come up against a note outside his range and retire momentarily disconcerted. Honor Blackman, whose bounds are narrower yet and narrower, stayed at any rate well within them.

Still, *Charlie Girl* and *The Four Musketeers!* have outrun *Cabaret*, so there is obviously a public for good old British stuff. Certainly the creators of *Mr and Mrs* have worked hard to provide something for everybody so may I suggest, relapsing reluctantly into the present, that, whatever your taste in musicals, there is something to disappoint you at the Palace.
