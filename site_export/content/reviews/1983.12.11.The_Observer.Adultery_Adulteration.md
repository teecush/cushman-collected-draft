Adultery, adulteration

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

DENNIS POTTER'S Sufficient Carbohydrate (Hampstead), his first theatrical original, starts, as so many other plays seem in memory to have done, with Dinsdale Landen being witty and hung-over. It is early morning; he is holidaying in a Greek villa. 'The sun is not up yet, so why,' he inquires of another character, 'are you?' Asked 'Is there any juice left?' he replies with a rueful sense of double-entendre. His virility is in question: a necessary condition, the play suggests, of being English. Now where have you heard that before?

Actually we are not in for yet another attack on an undefended home team; rather we are attending the xenophobic school of which John Osborne is the headmaster. Jack, Mr Landen's character, has many weaknesses, but just wait till you meet Eddie. Eddie is American, and both men are high up in a food-manufacturing business, a transatlantic conglomerate that has bought up Jack's smaller concern, while being obliged to keep him on the strength.

They are having this trip, along with their respective wives, on the firm. 'Have you ever wondered,' asks Eddie as the dialogue moves, a little ponderously, from atmospheres to exposition, 'why Greenace should pay for this jaunt?' The reason is that Greenace, as exemplified by Eddie, are heavily into additives and strange vegetable mutations, while Jack is a traditional whole-food man who is to be made to see the error of his ways. Also, Eddie is getting off with Jack's wife: adulteration on the one hand and adultery on the other.

A fifth sunbather is involved: Eddie's teenage boy by his first marriage, gazing with idealistic disgust at the antics of his elders while harbouring a secret passion for his young stepmother. He is a dead ringer for the student son in Smiles of a Summer Night (whom I of course think of as the student son in A Little Night Music). He scores fairly highly in the test that Mr Potter has devised for all the characters to take at the end of the play.

Jack, though smashed in more ways than one, can see a ship, a 'black freighter' -- a mystic sign of hope and continuity on the horizon. Those who can see it with him are saved. (Eddie of course is damned.) This seemed to me an image laboriously manufactured to prove a point that Mr Potter had had in mind before ever creating his characters. The development of the action is not altogether predictable -- Mr Landen has a mid-play outburst of violence that is a surprise at the time and a puzzle afterwards -- but you feel that the people have been programmed, even if only the author knows the code.

My problem was that I was less interested in the characters' souls than in their sex-lives, and more interested in their business problems than in either, while Mr Potter's priorities appeared to be the exact reverse. And though he probably tries to be fair, he really does seem to regard Americans as slimy things that have crawled out from under stones.

I found myself wondering if the food-juggling was a metaphor for what Hollywood had done to Pennies from Heaven; then remembered that Hollywood had actually improved it, and that Mr Potter himself had written the screenplay. Anyway, Eddie is a boor who can't tell Keats from Shakespeare; and is patronised passim.

Jack, a glamorous blend of sinner and seer, gets all the witty remarks, together with a crucial sermon which says that what people really like to eat is other people. He is also allowed to be the audience's point of reference while the other characters pose behind a glass partition. The narrative excuse is that they are taking refuge from the heat, but the dramatic effect is to make them look like insects.

Nancy Meckler's crisp production, perhaps wisely, makes no attempt to suggest the temperature of which the characters keep complaining. She may have erred, though, in casting Mr Landen. He does a spectacularly effective job, but he has now played so many despairing zanies and mid-life madcaps that we find ourselves thinking of Jack just as the Dinsdale Landen role: a disservice both to the play and to the actor who can (vide On the Razzle and Uncle Vanya) do other things.

Nicky Henson as Eddie has to work hard at the age, the insensitivity and the accent. Jill Baker and Jennifer Hilary do creditably with the underwritten ladies, while Rupert Graves glowers and suffers quite admirably as Youth, insufferable one moment, unanswerable the next. There are some really funny lines. Boring the play is not, but it is sanctimonious as heaven.

* * *

At Theatro Technis, Mornington Crescent, a musical version of Aristophanes's The Frogs closes tonight. Aristophanes was never a great one for keeping his eye on the object and the famous frogs' chorus ('brek-ek-ek-ek ko-ax ko-ax,' and if you believe that's what frogs do, even in Greece, you'll believe anything) is gleefully mined for its irrelevance to the main plot, which has to do with Dionysus travelling to Hades in order to bring back a dead playwright: an extreme example of practical criticism.

The candidates are a languid senior common-room Aeschylus and a redbrick Euripides. A comic slave is played by David Shaughnessy with a winning surliness, which he combines with a singing voice far too good for a frog; he should be ideal when they get round to The Birds.

* * *

Currently weighing down my desk are two volumes of financial scrutiny of the Royal Shakespeare Company, commissioned by the Minister for the Arts. It is an intimidatingly thorough document, full of interesting details of people's salaries and even running to a certain amount of dramatic criticism, some of it indulgently enthusiastic, some quite terrifyingly rigorous. There are productions that have got clean past me and my colleagues but which these accounting gentlemen take firmly to task.

Conclusions are that the RSC cannot continue on its present subsidy, that its only means of doing so would be to abandon the Barbican, and that this would be disastrous. Well, the report doesn't actually use the word 'disastrous,' but it does say that 'without a London base the Stratford theatre would lose its "national" company status.' In other words the report confirms what the rest of the world, or those of us enthusiastic about the theatre, or me and my mates, have always known.

The two stages in Stratford, the two in London, the season in Newcastle, the small-scale provincial tour, are all necessary and interdependent. They enable the good work done in a season of Shakespeare to reproduce and extend itself; without which opportunity, it could rapidly cease to be good. Besides, scale has its own attractions. Small may still be beautiful, but big is exciting; medium is tedium.
