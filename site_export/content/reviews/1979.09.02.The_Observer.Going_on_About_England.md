Going on about England

YOU can empathise with a play for quite fortuitous reasons.

I have just returned from California, and was naturally delighted to find that Queenie, one of the four characters in Peter Nichols's Born in the Gardens (Theatre Royal, Bristol), had done so too. Admittedly she had spent far longer in the West Coast sun than I (years as opposed to one week), but our reactions on return seemed encouragingly similar. She, if I read her right, was rocked by the home country's tatty quaintness, and most dispirited by its attempts at modernity.

Asked about her life abroad, she replies among other things that she heard so much there about the wonderful English theatre she had almost begun to believe it. So, the previous night, before setting off for Bristol--which is the scene as well as the venue of Mr Nichols's play--she had gone to see a play in London. (I, of course, had had the same experience, though not for quite the same reasons.)

She doesn't seem to have enjoyed her evening out very much. At some point in every play, she says--generalising with remarkable accuracy from presumably limited experience--the characters will form a straight line and discuss the state of England, no matter how hard the dramatist tries to stop them. Mr Nichols is a formal kidder and he is on this occasion his own director. His actors at this moment are standing in the formation he describes, and talking just so.

Years ago, in The National Health, Mr Nichols gave us the prototype national-microcosm play. Before that, in A Day in the Death of Joe Egg, he established himself as one of our best theatrical domestics. He has more or less alternated the two modes ever since. Born in the Gardens is an attempt to unite them, and if it succeeded it would be a tour de force. It isn't that, though the author's shining mastery of such neglected basics as character and dialogue make it the most entertaining native play to have crossed my path this year.

We are in Tudor Manor, a mullioned mansion (well up to the Bristol Old Vic's usual high standard of set-design) owned by a successful brush-manufacturer, recently deceased. His coffin lies on stage, Loot-like, throughout the first act. Gathered for the funeral are his widow Maud, a ga-ga lady with an overriding fear of insect infestation and a creative line in malapropisms; Mo, her 45-year-old son, who lives with her and for Dixieland jazz; Mo's twin, the aforementioned Queenie, who seems to fancy him something rotten; and their elder brother Hedley, a back-bench Labour MP who is determined to rearrange his family's life on sensible lines even if he can't do the same for the country. This means resettling Maud in a Primrose Hill duplex (or, as she fruitfully puts it, durex), while Queenie is more than willing to seduce Mo to her own chosen side of the Atlantic. Since both Mo and Maud have developed distressing characteristics--he talks to his cat and she to the silent TV screen--the audience have good reason to wish them shaken out of their respective ruts. It is obvious, though, that they are not going to be; the situation becomes frustratingly static, and the characters' individual comic routines overstrained.

The title refers to a gorilla, the pride of Bristol Zoo, born and raised there, and unknown to the outside world. His nearest human equivalent seems to be the cardiganed Mo, resigned to a life of captivity, knowing full well that he would be awfully disillusioned if he ever actually visited New Orleans. He has some smashing speeches on jazz and other things, but for the play to work he needs to be brought more ruthlessly to its forefront.

It is most enjoyably acted, though Jennie Linden bashes Queenie over a bit hard and Beryl Reid relishes Maud's material and her own perfect timing of it rather too obviously for credibility. It is left to Barry Foster to be both grotesque and believable (as well as personally unrecognisable) as Mo, and for Peter Bowles to make Hedley quite the most sympathetic of our stage's recent crop of joke Socialists.

His blinkered good intentions (and the mess that he, like Queenie, has made of his own emotional life--Mr Nichols holds the scales fairly) say a good deal about Labour in disarray. His mother's view of the party is also pretty close to the bone of popular prejudice. She blames them for post-war austerity: "After all we'd been through, they gave us another five years of it, without even the American soldiers to take our minds off it." For lines like that--burnished and rooted--I can forgive Mr Nichols a lot.

The state-of-the-nation speech in Howard Brenton's Sore Throats (Warehouse) is delivered by Sally, come to view a flat of which her privileged position as an Evening Standard telephonist has given her advance information. The speech has little to do with the rest of the play, which could be going on anywhere.

In the flat, which is desolate, Sally discovers Judy, the estranged wife of Jack, a policeman. Jack returns home to beat Judy up, so that she will hand him over half her money, enabling him to go to Canada with his new love and become a Mountie. He knocks her down, kicks her repeatedly in the guts, then invites her out to dinner. I can believe almost anything, but I want to know more about Jack's insensitivity and Judy's previous passivity before I can swallow that sequence.

Sally and Judy shack up together, devote themselves to sexual gratification; then Jack comes back again, still after money and delivering a searing speech about the birth of his child in the Canadian wilds. Mr Brenton is good, too, at startling images, like Judy tearing up bank notes, and shamefaced ripostes, as when Jack voices our guilty materialist thoughts by saying that if he sticks them together they will still be legal tender.

The play ends with Judy looking forward to a future of sexual, personal and financial freedom. Mr Brenton is not usually an ironist so one can only think, bully for you. Other reactions are stifled by the slenderness of his personal documentation. Paola Dionisotti gives some reality to both her suffering and her defiance; Malcolm Storry delivers his oration well, but is otherwise hard pressed to escape monotony. Jack's profession seems arbitrary: Mr Brenton can't believe that all coppers are wife-beaters, or vice versa. Ruby Wax does her bit, which mainly consists of taking some of the heat off Miss Dionisotti, ably. But if Mr Nichols's indecisiveness has led to dramatic obesity, Mr Brenton's single-mindedness has resulted in emaciation.
