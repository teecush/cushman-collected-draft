A magnificent hypocrite

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN
on 'Tartuffe' at the
Edinburgh Festival

MOLIERE took several risks in writing Tartuffe. His satire on religious poseurs and their dupes offended vested interests, which for a time at least succeeded in keeping the play off the boards and the playwright behind bars. To us, though, these liberties are trifling compared to the licence he took in keeping his principal character offstage for the first two of five acts.

Encore l'audace. Peter James, in his production for the Actors Company, keeps Tartuffe in the wings--if the gaunt, earthbound platform stage of the Assembly Hall can be said to have wings--for two acts and a 20-minute interval. For the entire first half of the show we are offered Tartuffe without the Hypocrite and sometimes without very much else either.

It takes nerve, in any case, to offer this play in this environment; not so much because of the place's ecclesiastical associations (God may not be mocked, but what of John Knox?) as because of its frightful acoustics, and yet more dreadful sightlines. (I may have got that the wrong way round.) It may be a great place for pageants and chronicles--the best thing I ever saw there was Richard II--but it bears hard on more civilised pastimes like comedy.

Nor is the situation improved if players and director simply attempt--as might initially seem reasonable--to conquer by sheer vigour. Tartuffe begins with a family row, which in Mr James's production is spread so thin and shouted so loud that I almost despaired of the evening ahead. Only when Sheila Burrell's black-lace Welsh widow came off the rampage, slowed down and summoned her forces for a concentrated lecture did I feel any confidence.

You may be able to bombast out a blank verse but you cannot rant a rhyming couplet. The form, if ingeniously retained in Richard Wilbur's translation which, it is pleasing to note, has attracted fewer sneers this year than when it was aired some time ago by the National Theatre; but it is not, of course, a mode of expression to which most British actors are accustomed. There are a couple of players in this company--one melodious Robert Eddison, or agile Edward Petherbridge--sufficiently smooth-tongued to wrap themselves around Mr Wilbur's phrases and still have energy to spare. But the majority are less nimble and need time to stand still and collect themselves.

When they do, the results are most gratifying. I think of Sharon Duce pleading earnestly with her father not to marry her to Tartuffe, and of Windsor Davies's surly response. Earlier Mr Davies hits a nicely unassertive basso note on his Orgon with his moved reiterated "Poor fellow" as Tartuffe's gluttonies and complacencies are unavailingly recounted to him. This, alas, is all the compliment I can bestow on this actor, whose performance has already, I see, been critically described as "bearish." If I could show you the jottings on my programme, I could prove to you that the same adjective had occurred, quite independently, to me. As it is, you will just have to take my word for it, together with my assurance that it constitutes a monotonous drag on the play.

Mr Davies sounds less Welsh than his supposed mother, Miss Burrell, but Mr James sees the entire family as aspiring provincials in Paris. There being no obvious social difference in this production between the family and their servant Dorine, her domination of them is robbed of its comic point. Hers is, as Mr James says, "the best part ever written for a maid" but Paola Dionisotti makes surprisingly little of it. She, more than most, gets lost in the uproar. Sheila Reid, however, endows Elmire with more wit and poise than she has ever shown before; and rises, red-haired and delectable, to her encounter with Tartuffe.

We have arrived at length--as the play does--at Charles Kay's gleaming, beaming protagonist; and if the production's greatest power lies in its moments of stillness they are multiplied tenfold whenever he is on the stage. He is stillness; one can see why Orgon takes him for a saint. An audience, marvelling at his presence, may feel impelled to offer similar devotion before reflecting that a man so completely in control of himself could only be a charlatan.

I have sometimes imagined a Tartuffe in whom there was no clear demarcation between sincerity and falsehood; who might, like Shakespeare's Angelo, half-believe in his own sanctity and occasionally despise himself for his lust. Maybe this is fanciful; certainly it is not Mr Kay's Tartuffe, whose every passion has been sublimated within his assumed persona. Moliere's strategy here--simple but immensely powerful--was to devise situations increasingly outrageous, while exempting their supposed originator not merely from compunction but from any hint of surprise. Mr Kay, seraphically glowing beneath the gold wig and matching spectacles, meets the challenge magnificently; nothing fazes, nothing throws him.

Awarded the deeds to his benefactor's house, he betrays his satisfaction by no more than a raised eyebrow; his hands stay devotionally clasped. Finally, very swiftly, he surveys the stage (for this scene Geoffrey Scott's handsome outdoor set has unexpectedly sprouted some wrought iron work which it shortly mysteriously sheds). But in that one look he takes possession. Seducing Elmire he glides noiselessly between professions of piety, of love, of contempt for her husband. (Mr Wilbur's couplets gild and undercut his sophistries simultaneously.) Here, Mr James turns the garden setting memorably to account; echoes from Eden sound as Elmire tempts Tartuffe with grapes, and they ring out as he returns to claim her, draped in satanic red. That an actor could be at once so unassuming and so majestic; the paradox persists, exhilaratingly and disturbingly right to the end of the play.

The production is an achievement, if an uneven one. Notice how rapidly--in the wake of the National's Misanthrope--we have come to expect as much proficiency from English Moliere as English Chekhov. The various companies will now please line up and offer us The School for Wives and Don Juan. After that they may proceed to Marivaux, beginning with Les fausses confidences.

* * *

At the Haymarket Icerink the Young Lyceum Company offer Sean McCarthy's The Fantastical Feats of Finn MacCool, he being a mythical celtic giant here cut--in the sturdy and subdued form of Tony Haygarth--very much down to size. The show is sustained by spectacle (Jeananne Crowley gives a magical, darting performance as a faun) and one or two of the jokes are as good as the title; but the overall tone--as of a jeering, pedantic pantomime--often grates.
