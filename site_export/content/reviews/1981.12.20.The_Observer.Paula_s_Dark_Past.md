Paula's dark past

Robert Cushman in London and Nottingham

Pinero's The Second Mrs Tanqueray (Lyttelton) is the archetypal drama of the woman with a past. Aubrey Tanqueray, a late Victorian clubman, takes as his wife a notoriously unchaste young woman named Paula. His acquaintances cut him; most grievously, Ellean, the convent-bred daughter of his first wife, dislikes his second and a respectable neighbour whisks her off to Paris to spare her any evil influence.

Then Ellean falls in love. Her father is pleased and perplexed. 'I thought you took after your poor mother a little,' he says on her return, 'but there's a look on your face that I never saw on hers.' That moment, the most explicitly sexual in the play, comes late in the action and throws it into sharp relief. It paves the way for a rapprochement between Ellean and Paula, and it tells us all we need to know about Aubrey's first marriage.

I don't think it is prurient to want to know more than Pinero is prepared or able to tell about his second. It is quite easy to guess: to build up a mental picture of Aubrey as a long-repressed man bowled over by his first real experience of sex. But he sees himself as the noble rescuer of a fallen woman, and the text gives him little opportunity to play anything else.

The play's catastrophe comes when Ellean's romance turns out to be an old lover of Paula's. She tells on him. She has been blamed for this, most recently by the critic of The Guardian, who points out that all she had to do was shut up and that by making trouble she disqualifies herself as a tragic heroine. Well, if Hamlet had decided that he didn't believe in ghosts, or if Oedipus had taken his wife's advice and stopped investigating the past, they too might have lived happily ever after.

They became tragic by insisting on the truth, thereby collaborating in their own destruction. Paula also tells the truth, partly under the influence of the Rosmersholm Complex which forces abnormally high codes of behaviour on girls who enter ancestral homes. (Pinero used the same motif comically, and therefore more felicitously, in Trelawny of the Wells.) Pinero has planted additional clues to Paula's behaviour. She says that she has felt furiously jealous twice in her life. Once is when she loses Ellean to Paris. She keeps mum about the other time, but it must have been when she lost the man who now reappears as her prospective son-in-law. Her two jealousies converge, and it's no wonder she snaps. You may perhaps blame Pinero for hedging his bets: nobility or revenge?

The suicide that ends the play (I haven't spoiled it for you, honest) is suspect. The drama has not burned hot enough to justify it. But don't accuse Pinero of propitiating Victorian morality; he is arraigning it for demanding such a sacrifice. A few minutes before, he has stirred and surprised us by letting Aubrey say all the things that have been staring him and his cronies in the face about the double standard.

All the same the play is infected with Victorian pieties. It is taken for granted that the pure Ellean will instinctively abhor her tainted stepmother; and however much we may be counselled against throwing stones, there is no suggestion that Paula's experiences could be a source of anything but shame. There is no hypocrisy in the piece, but there is myopia; and certainly Paula herself is not seen clearly. She is less a character than an alternating vehicle for anger and remorse: doubtless a great role for a barnstorming actress, but Felicity Kendal is hardly that. Faced with a major row, she resorts to sustained shouting that starts you worrying about the effect on her throat. Momentary outbursts she handles with stabbing effectiveness. Leigh Lawson, looking 10 years too young to be the father of his daughter or the contemporary of his friends, hardly saves Aubrey from dullness.

Harold Innocent rejoices in the mother-hen confidant role, but few of the other actors are at ease with the style (which is the plummy side of rich) and some are downright wooden. Michael Rudman's production is otherwise efficient, especially when evoking the imprisonments of convention, though Carl Toms's sets take this theme a bit far, surrounding the Tanqueray homestead with pitchy darkness while the text speaks of morning sunshine.

* * *

Lady in the Dark (Nottingham Playhouse) is a fairly famous musical, 1941 vintage, never before done in Britain, and confining its songs to a series of dream sequences. The dreamer is a New York fashion magazine editor suffering from depressions. With the help of a shrink she realises that she has always been nervous about her looks and has compensated in ruthless devotion to her job. She also learns to choose between the three men in her life; it is hinted that she will eventually devote herself to one of them, and give up unwomanly pursuits like work.

In Crispin Thomas's production the show plods; everything has to be framed within the analyst's office, which in Hugh Durrant's design is a white nightmare on its own account. Moss Hart's script alternates between awed sessions on the couch and in the office, lengthy banter that requires a cast of Eve Ardens, of both sexes. As for the songs: Ira Gershwin was, of all the major lyricists, the freest with heavy trick-rhymes; this score shows him sometimes charming, frequently elephantine. Kurt Weill wrote the music which, like much of his American work, is skilled but bloodless. There are some guts in the final circus dream, which includes the celebrated 'Saga of Jenny,' who would make up her mind ('At seventeen to Vassar it was quite a blow/That in twenty-seven languages she couldn't say no'); and the gentle 'My Ship' is deservedly a classic. Celeste Holm is too subdued for the saga, but she sings beautifully about her ship. She also finds wit and warmth in the dialogue, but she often looks marooned.

* * *

The School for Scandal (Greenwich) is weak on satire, strong on individual character. If there has to be a discrepancy this is the right one, since Sheridan himself plainly lost interest in his ostensible subject after the first two acts. The Teazles are here takingly played by Paul Brooke and Nina Thomas, marked out for special sympathy by being dressed in black and white, to match Bernard Culshaw's delicate and delectable sets; drawn rather than painted.

* * *

The People Show Cabaret (Royal Court) has, in Mark Long, the anti-compere of one's dreams: smooth, sinister and insulting. His stories are the ultimate in shaggy-doggedness. The bill he presents reminded me of a cherished Bob Hope throwaway about a musician who got wounded in a Spike Jones recording session; here a running gag about a frustrated tribute to Glenn Miller is reinforced by some fearsome knockabout, most of it executed by Emil Wolk, a small man with an impressive line in forward rolls. He can also, like the rest of the line-up (three horns and a piano), really play. I don't find him very funny, so the show wore me down.
