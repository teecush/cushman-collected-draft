The Prince of Broadway

'The Pajama Game', 'Cabaret', 'The Phantom of the Opera': since the 1950s, Hal Prince has been the great musicals director of our time. Now, he's bringing 'Show Boat' to London. Robert Cushman met him in New York

HAL PRINCE - Harold Prince on official credits, but Hal everywhere else - turned around a photograph on his office desk so that I could get a good look at it. It was of his first grandchild; a second is on the way. "My shows," he said, "are a reflection of what I'm thinking about. Show Boat is about family."

Show Boat, first staged in 1927, is thought by many to be the greatest American musical. It was the first Broadway show to concentrate all the resources of the musical on telling a complex and coherent story. A headily innovative book was matched to an equally adventurous score. Oscar Hammerstein wrote the lyrics to Jerome Kern's music, and an extraordinary number of their songs have gone on to lead independent lives - "Ol' Man River", "Bill", "Can't Help Lovin' Dat Man", "Make Believe", "Why Do I Love You", "Life Upon the Wicked Stage".

But they all fit perfectly into their dramatic context: a considerable breakthrough for 1927, and one not to be rivalled for a decade or more. The narrative spans 40 years of American life - a hugely ambitious dramatic canvas. The "show boat" of the title is the Cotton Blossom, which ploughs the Mississippi River, bringing melodrama to the masses. The principal characters are its owners, actors and crew. They come in pairs: Cap'n Andy, the show boat's stage-struck skipper, and his wife Parthy, who rules it and him with a rod of iron; their daughter Magnolia, who proves herself as an actress, and Gaylord Ravenal, the feckless gambler she loves and improvidently marries; Julie La Verne and her husband Steve, a mixed-race couple who fall foul of the vicious Mississippi race laws; actor Frank and comedienne Ellie, the musical's standard light-relief couple; Joe the black deck hand and his wife Queenie, the cook.

It is Joe who sings the show's anthem, "Ol' Man River", described by Hammerstein as "an implicit protest song", and a magnificently economical depiction of human suffering and endurance ("I'm tired of livin' / And feared of dyin' ..."). The show's engagement with race may seem tentative by later standards, but it was unprecedented in its time, and can still strike sparks even today. The premiere of Prince's production - in Toronto in 1996 - was heralded by allegations that the musical was "racist" and "hate literature", the latter an especially strange accusation to hurl at a show that is demonstrably full of the milk of human kindness - full even to overflowing. It became clear that few of the protesters had seen the show, and once the production had opened, little more was heard of them.

Much revised during the lifetimes of Kern and Hammerstein, incessantly recorded and three times filmed, Show Boat has since rolled on as implacably and unpredictably as the river itself. Each version has been different from its predecessor. The text that Prince has adapted and directed draws on all available variants, including the Edna Ferber novel on which the musical was based. The year after the Toronto premiere, the show opened in New York, and has since toured all over America; indeed, it's still doing so. It has just opened in Australia. And this week, it has its premiere in London.

HAL PRINCE turned 70 in January this year. He started as a producer and is now a director. For a long and fruitful time he was both: nobody else has made that transition so successfully, and few have attempted it at all. The walls of his office in Rockefeller Plaza, New York, are crowded, as you would expect, with posters of his shows: the record stretches back now over 44 years. His first production, The Pajama Game, opened on Broadway in 1954. It was a big success, and established the 26-year-old Prince as, in the words of screenwriter and commentator William Goldman, "the last of the boy-wonder producers".

The "boy wonder" flashes a precocious grin from one of the photographs that have squeezed their way in between the posters. It depicts Prince and his producing partner of those days, Robert E Griffith, in the wake of that first success. Griffith is middle-aged and modestly managerial; Prince, spruce and glossy-haired, looks like an engaging and impossibly confident mixture of salesman and graduate student. He and Griffith had worked together as stage managers for the legendary producer/director George Abbott, and when they mounted The Pajama Game, they hired their former employer to direct. They themselves, producing on a shoestring, stage-managed their show; Prince has said that six months passed before he got to see his hit musical from out front.

PRINCE HAS never, to put it discreetly, had to go back to stage-management since. After The Pajama Game, Griffith and Prince went on to produce such notable things as Damn Yankees, Fiorello! (a little-known title in Britain, but one of the few musicals to win the Pulitzer Prize) and, most august of all, West Side Story. Then, after Griffith's death, Prince went solo and produced A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum, Fiddler on the Roof and Cabaret. Out of his office came in fact a staggering proportion of the best American musicals of the 1950s and 1960s. Cabaret (1966) was an especial watershed. To begin with, Prince himself directed it. He brought off something very rare in the theatre, musical or otherwise; he caught the movement of history. In a show set in Weimar Berlin, he let the onset of Nazism creep up on us scene by scene, number by number. Cabaret also began a movement away from linear narrative and towards fragmentation, montage and musical comment: a style that matured, along with a preference for unconventional subject-matter, in the musicals for which Stephen Sondheim wrote music and lyrics and which Prince both produced and directed, shows that - artistically if not commercially - ruled the 1970s: Company, Follies, A Little Night Music, Pacific Overtures, Sweeney Todd.

Then came the flop that ended the collaboration, Merrily We Roll Along. More flops followed with other writers: in the first half of the 1980s it seemed that Prince could do nothing right, and his producing career virtually ended. Salvation, for the man who once said that he had to direct his own shows because nobody else would hire him, came in working for other people. He had already, during the Sondheim period, taken time off to direct Andrew Lloyd Webber's Evita (1978); now he scored the greatest commercial success of his career with Lloyd Webber's Phantom of the Opera (1986).

The Phantom of the Opera, in Prince's luscious staging, has now gone around the world. One of the most enduring satellite productions has been in Toronto, initiating another fruitful association between Prince and the Canadian-based producing company Livent Inc. It led to Livent's producing Kiss of the Spider Woman, one of the most visually compelling and thematically adventurous of all Prince's shows. After this Garth Drabinsky, the company's presiding impresario, suggested a revival of Show Boat. (I have here to declare an interest: I work for Livent. However, my association with the company began only last year.)

Though Prince had generally avoided revivals, this was the one old show he had long wanted to do. Part of the attraction was in the show's scale, and how that connected with his own previous work. Cabaret, Follies, Pacific Overtures might all be called epic musicals, exemplifying Prince's stated belief that "the most effective social commentary is to illuminate one period through another". And Show Boat is America's home-grown epic, depicting life on the Mississippi from the 1880s to the 1920s. The staging scheme evolved by Prince and his designer Eugene Lee emphasises the passage of time "from gaslight to automobile". It peaks in detailed, wonderfully lit genre pictures: a waterfront casino filled with intent gentleman-gamblers, immune to every passion but their own; the Cotton Blossom floating theatre with actors and audience packed in together, feelings running melodramatically high on both sides of the footlights; a Chicago music hall glimpsed in the busy aimlessness of daytime rehearsal and then in the frenzied jollity of New Year's Eve (bodies crowded into a confined space are a repeated motif of the production); the decks, constantly rising and falling, of the show boat itself. Years pass before our eyes in a choreographed montage that is the production's own invention, designed to filling some gaping holes in the original second act.

It's generally conceded that the second act is where Hammerstein's script begins to buckle under the sheer weight of narrative, and this is where Prince's surgery has been most aggressive. Hammerstein's first act, by contrast, is brilliant story-telling, and contains one episode - the "miscegenation scene" - that Prince describes, with obvious conviction, as "the best musical scene I've ever worked on".

"The scene," says Prince, "is rich in apparent drama, and in subtext." The apparent drama comes when Steve cuts Julie's arm and his own in order to mingle their bloods, thereby proving that he has "Negro blood in him" so that his mulatto wife has broken no race laws. The "subtext" belongs to the onlookers, who between them embody every polarity in the story: black and white, old and young, liberal and puritanical, comic and romantic. At the end Cap'n Andy has to find a new leading couple to replace the banished Julie and Steve. To the fury of his monumentally censorious wife, he lights on Magnolia and Gaylord Ravenal. The two share a stage kiss that rapidly develops into the real thing.

"And the scene has always ended," says Prince, "with the two of them kissing, and a spotlight on Parthy having the vapours. Show Boat was originally performed by star personalities who made the roles their own. Then when they all had gone, in revivals they'd take movie stars and put them in the slot, to do their own thing. The tradition with Parthy had been to have fluffy comic ladies: they had the vapours a lot. What I have her do at the end of the miscegenation scene is what any serious actress would do: she sits there stone-faced.

"George Grizzard and Carole Shelley [the London Andy and Parthy] are two serious actors who happen also to have done a lot of comedy in their time; they're approaching the roles with the same reality as John McMartin and Elaine Stritch [who played the roles in Toronto and on Broadway]. They belong together. At the end of the show Carole does something nobody else has done; at the end of the show she shows with her posture that osteoporosis has set in. There can be vastly different performances of Andy and Parthy: the beauty of the material is that there's so much there. The vaudevillians and the young lovers tend to be played the same way, though Ravenal can be more romantic or more seedy older or younger." The London Ravenal is among the young and romantic; he is Hugh Panaro, who recently did time as Raoul, the juvenile lead in Phantom. (The older, seedier Ravenals are often actors who have played the Phantom himself.)

Among the London leads, the only two remaining from the Toronto premiere are the black couple: Michel Bell, who sings Joe with the ringing bass associated with the role since Paul Robeson, and Grethe Boston, who plays Queenie with more edge and less flounce than has ever been customary. "You have," says Prince, "to be so careful not to do caricature." In 1927, the show began with a chorus of black dock-workers singing "Niggers all work on the Mississippi / Niggers all work while the white folks play." The line has been changed, as in previous revivals, to "coloured folks work on the Mississippi ..." Hammerstein meant it to shock, and Prince considered using it, but he now concedes, "I wouldn't ask anyone to utter that word on stage." "Ol' Man River" occasions the fiercest piece of staging in the show; in Prince's words, you watch the characters "tear down a whole back-breaking class system". (It would be unfair to reveal how, except to say that Prince instructed the cast, "if you don't all pull together on the word 'tote' - as in 'tote that barge, lift that bale' - it's all over.")

I SPOKE TO PRINCE in a brief lull between his return from launching the Sydney Show Boat and taking off to do the same thing in London. Weeks before, he had rehearsed one of the American touring companies in Tampa, Florida, knowing that the Australian commitment would prevent him from attending their opening in Fort Lauderdale two weeks later. Phantom has been playing in Hamburg for eight-and-a-half years now: "I try to catch them once a year if I can. I get back to the Vienna production more often. I have a responsibility to keep the shows up - a responsibility, I may say, that rarely occurred to the directors I hired when I was a producer." In fact, when he was a producer, he regularly hired only two directors, but they were two of the best: Jerome Robbins and George Abbott. Robbins apparently never revisited his shows; Abbott would stand in the wings occasionally, with his hand on the stage manager's shoulder, making sotto voce comments on the actors and saying "tell them that ..." And Prince of course would know; for a long time ago, he was that stage manager.

"EVERYTHING'S a long time ago now," Prince says, mindful of his evolution from prodigy to grand old man. I once described him - and I can't stop myself from repeating the description - as an appealing mixture of handyman and sage.

Asked about the future of the musical, he says that it's safe as a form because they can't duplicate it on films and TV, and because there's so much money being made from it. But he despairs that young talents are not being widely nurtured. He tends not to see other people's revivals of his previous shows, but finds himself being nice about them when he does: "Actually, I've not got much to be angry about; even I realise that."

His next musical, Parade, promises to be exceptionally hard-hitting - it's about the lynching of a Jewish man in the American Deep South, just before World War One, and he says it's about his own antecedents - it's where his family settled when they came to America in the first half of the last century. For now, though, he's basking in the softer family glow of Show Boat. (I quizzed him a couple of years ago about his continuing reputation as a director of dark musicals: "Oh no," he said, "These days I'm very benign.")

Show Boat ends with a grey-haired Magnolia pointing proudly to her daughter Kim, now a star in her own right. This is apparently the first Show Boat in which Magnolia and Kim have not been played by the same actress. "So, at the last moment, you had the star, back as Magnolia, saying 'Hey, look, there's Kim,' and pointing at some mute chorus-girl half-hidden in the wings. And that was the final curtain." Here we get three generations on stage at the same time, indissolubly connected but each mentally in their own world. And, like the river, it's troubling and benevolent and irreversible.

Prince Edward Theatre, W1 (0171 447 5400), now previewing, opens Tues.
