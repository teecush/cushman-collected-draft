Grass Roots

An American graduate student once advised me to visit some of her country's small towns instead of just city-hopping. That way, she said, I would understand what, or who, kept Johnson in power. I was irresponsible enough not to take her advice, but I have now been provided with a surrogate. And without having to go any further afield than the stockbroker belt. (And go you and do likewise if the opportunity offers, because it's a very pleasant theatre, easy of access to and from London, with—if this production is representative—a satisfyingly high standard of presentation.)

*Grass Roots*, which is not about marijuana and ran long and happily off-Broadway as *Now Is the Time For All Good Men*, which is indisputable if desperate, is not, in theme, style or content, the most original show you are ever going to see. But it has and makes its points. Its hero breezes in from swinging Philadelphia to take up a position as English teacher in Bloomdale, Indiana (population 973), and immediately sets about shocking the community with his new-fangled ideas. They didn't seem very shocking in Leatherhead or, I would imagine, off-Broadway, and what, I ask you, are the show's chances of being seen in Bloomdale, Indiana? This is the kind of question we should all be pondering but not too deeply, lest all our theatres close down at once.

Once upon a time, though it never trespassed upon ours, there was a musical called *Bloomer Girl* whose emancipated female chorus sang that it was good enough for grandma, that good old girl, but it wasn't good enough for them. In *Grass Roots* the male and female chorus are of the opinion that it was good enough for grandma (those very words) and that they have no quarrel with it either. At this moment in time, that scene is genuinely frightening. History has played into the authors' hands; the election of a bull-headed sports coach as man of the century becomes almost a metaphor for the barnstorming progress of George Wallace. This makes the indomitable right-minded liberal hero a little less difficult to take; the authors have cunningly employed the love-interest as a means of expressing their own doubts concerning his inflexibility. He frightens off the young widow who gets involved with him, and her point of view is stated with a sympathy that is even extended to the square head teacher who also loves her.

I would like to see a straight play about modern small-town America (not, that is, about a divided family who just happen to live there). *Grass Roots* goes about as deep as a musical decently can, and surprisingly enough never becomes portentous. There are no stentorian dreamers of impossible dreams. It is also the first musical I have encountered written entirely by women. (Female lyricists are common enough but composers, in this field, almost unheard of.) This is really about the only history it makes; verbally and musically, the songs are generally no more than a pleasant way of passing the time. Nancy Ford, says the programme, spent some time pounding a piano for *The Fantasticks* and it shows. Her tinkles are less resounding than her model's, though both scores, curiously enough, reach their peak with songs about the rain. This one (“Rain—It's April”) has a haunting title-phrase, and a resourceful structure.

So is the whole show. I don't know where they've been hiding all these years but the entire cast can sing and act. The hero (Stuart Damon) functions admirably in both departments, and the heroine (Eira Heath) even better. (In view of this I felt rather sorry when boy didn't, in the end, get girl. I can't remember when he last did in an American musical. Still, he doesn't die in this one.) Paul Maxwell is excellent as the head, and Bill Kerr, to my delight, plays the coach, and doesn't sound in the least like he used to do with Hancock. The town whore, for whose benefit the score goes mildly rock, is endowed by Marti Webb with a vivacious personality, a demeanour which if not sexy is nothing, and a very loud voice. Anton Rodgers' direction is the height of spruceness, and (I am requested to advertise) can be adjusted for larger halls. I don't think it's by any means sure-fire West End material but seeing it where it was, when it was, I was impressed.
