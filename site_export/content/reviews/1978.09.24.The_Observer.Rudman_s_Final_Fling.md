Rudman's final fling

ROBERT CUSHMAN on "a cracker" of a comedy by Michael Hastings.

MICHAEL HASTINGS'S Gloo Joo is rumoured to be Michael Rudman's last production as director of the Hampstead Theatre.

Though I join with all the world in applauding the friendliness of Mr Rudman's five-year regime at Swiss Cottage, I have generally found his actual choice of plays to be glum.

There have been exceptions, of course: two plays by Michael Frayn, one by Pam Gems, and now, at the last, a cracker. The show-piece of Mr Hastings's play is a Jewish wedding, different from most that have been seen on our stages in that the bride is African, the groom Jamaican and the setting an immigration office at Gatwick Airport, whose two functionaries are currently trebling as witnesses, congregation and upholders of the handy portable canopy brought along (together with a celebratory bottle of sherry) by the officiating rabbi.

The assembly are required of course to wear hats, and here the bridegroom can oblige. What living he has, he makes from selling woolly football caps, dealing impartially with supporters of all teams; he now produces a couple of samples. Very funny they look. Mr Hastings here reveals himself as a superb farcical engineer. His hero, Meadowlark Warner, reveals himself as a superb opportunist.

That is his role throughout. The play occurs on what he calls repeatedly "the greatest day of his life." It happens to be the day decided on by the authorities for his deportation; he is forcibly removed from Brixton to Gatwick and is in imminent danger of further travel. He has, however, provided himself with a pair of fiancees.

The first, who is Irish, he discards as having the wrong-coloured passport. (Meadowlark, I should warn you, is a racist.) The second lady is far more eligible and, her patrial status having been approved to the satisfaction of all parties, the ceremony is performed in the exotic circumstances already described. A necessary (though imperfect) coda shows Meadowlark finally meeting his match. His new wife demands to be supported. "From now on," she says immortally, "you'll line up at the social security like everybody else."

The play makes manifest the racial jokes that underlay Trevor Griffiths's Comedians. Meadowlark has lines (one, for example, about not wanting to go to Jamaica because he can meet quite enough Jamaicans in London) that no white character would be entrusted with. When he needs suddenly to claim Jewishness he pours out a glorious flood of nonsense about something called the Black Temple of Nimrod; but his real appeal is to the brotherhood of the persecuted. Meanwhile the immigration officers look on with fascinating (and subtly interchangeable) degrees of callousness and sympathy.

Mr Rudman's flawlessly zippy production is propelled by Oscar James's bounding, fluently Meadowlark and anchored by Antony Brown and Dave Hill as his guardians. Mr Brown, the more punctilious of the two, is an especial delight during the wedding; permanently desirous of mopping his brow, but hampered by the lack of an available hand.

This is, according to the programme, Mr Hastings's ninth play; having missed all but one of the other eight I cannot guess whether he has it in him to give us more comedies as refreshing as this. But then I don't know whether Snoo Wilson will ever give us another Glad Hand, Bill Morrison another Flying Blind, or Mike Stott (the next Hampstead attraction) another Funny Peculiar. Counting blessings rather than pre-natal chickens, I can only celebrate the existence of a new and penetrating genre of social farce.

* * *

Stephen Poliakoff is not among its practitioners. He does not make many jokes. But neither is he weighed down with solemnity or self-hatred. Shout Across the River (Warehouse) adds to his patented collection of grisly fun-centres: there are scenes in an ice-cream parlour (not such a bad place, actually) and in a multi-barred pub, "the biggest pleasure centre for 20 miles." More importantly he shows an increasing mastery of inner terror.

A mother, only 35, has a problem daughter, suspended from school for every imaginable offence. So much for the first scene; in the second, it transpires the daughter, only 15, has a problem mother, who has hardly left the house for six months. Daughter proposes, in the week left her before removal to an approved school, to take mother in hand. How much of her action is due to concern and how much to malice (for she has to be cruel to be kind and would very likely be cruel anyway) is unclear; and the mother's acquiescence is not too easy to swallow.

There are indeed many unexplained details; we don't know what the family lives on and Mr Poliakoff has inflicted on himself a peculiarly refined version of the eternal exposition problem; characters who live together are continually discovering things they should have known for years. He has yet in fact to find a satisfying connecting path between the interior and exterior landscapes he charts with such acuteness. But the effort of belief is this time worth making; the jumping, twisting dialogue is compulsive.

Besides, the upshot is unprecedented. Daughter screams that they are living at the worst time ever and mother, defying all current theatrical custom, refuses to believe it. Having been lured out into the world she finds she can take it. The shout across the river between the generations (or is it merely between the characters south of the Thames and the audience north of it) is, against all augury, successful. Lynn Farleigh gives a wonderfully illuminating account of the mother. Gwyneth Strong is superbly sharp-edged in opposition and support.

* * *

A pair of similarly damaged lives are depicted, but hardly explored, in David Storey's Sisters (Royal Exchange, Manchester); unlike Mr Poliakoff, Mr Storey shows no strain in his writing, but the result -- wringing wan jokes out of a suburban brothel, family-owned -- slips away as soon as heard.

Trevor Nunn's production of As You Like It (Aldwych) still lacks a final roundness and sureness in performance, but a show that was half-good at Stratford is now three-quarters excellent. Mr Nunn excels both at bringing joy and at criticising it; the Forest of Arden is mocked even while celebrated. Oliver Ford Davies (Duke) and Jeffery Dench (Adam) are prodigies of self-deprecation. Alan David's Touchstone, funny nearly all the time, and Emerys James's Jaques, the prototypal Eeyore, are greatly improved performances, and Charlotte Cornwell's Rosalind is most seriously and charmingly in love.

Other newcomers are Cherie Lunghi, a phenomenally witty Celia, and Jane Carr, whose lumpy schoolgirl act does notably well for Phoebe. The changes in the weather (from "blow, blow, thou winter wind" to "sweet lovers love the spring") are exactly and most musically noted, and much of the play is in them.
