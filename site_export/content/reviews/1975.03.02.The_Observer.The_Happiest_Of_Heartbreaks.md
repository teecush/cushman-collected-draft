The happiest of Heartbreaks

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the National's new Shaw production.

THE National Theatre generally performs best in periods of retrenchment. At present it may be carrying this policy to extremes: the three plays in the current repertory have cast-lists of eight, ten and two, which suggests that the National is doing rather less than its bit to relieve theatrical unemployment. But those actors who are working are, as one would expect, extremely accomplished, and whatever may lie in wait in the new building, the last days at the Old Vic will probably be good ones.

The lavish insecurities of last year have given way to the imposing austerities of John Gabriel Borkman and to the full-scale triumph of Heartbreak House, a practically faultless production. It is the best account of the play that I have seen, and the others have all been good. That last consideration leads me to believe that Heartbreak House, though not an easy play to stage (no play is easy to stage), is not supernally difficult. It demands 10 good performances, but it offers 10 good parts and a prestigious company should be able to cast them. These performances have to be welded into an ensemble; but British actors are famously unselfish, and this -- far more than the plays of Chekhov that inspired it -- is a play that breeds co-operation. Its rhythms are imposed by the text; no sensitive actor could ignore them.

The play is by no means devoid of emotion -- that canard is hardly true of any of Shaw's work and of this least of all -- but all feeling is brought to the surface and carefully, often brilliantly, expressed. Here, of course, is the crucial difference from Chekhov, much of whose impact derives from what we sense behind the lines, but there can be powerful surprises in Shaw's explicitness. When Captain Shotover cries at the betrayal of his mental powers by his 88 years ('I must go in and out . . . I can't keep awake and alert') and goes on to detail the encroachments of sleep and 'the horrors of an old man drinking,' he is getting, quite literally, on his author's nerves.

A well-performed group play is a peculiarly soothing experience; the cohesion communicates itself to the audience. What dialogue and delivery cannot supply in the way of atmosphere may be supplemented by setting and lighting; Shaw was extremely canny in his requests for both. For the first two acts we are in Shotover's ship-shaped house, for the last in the garden on an autumnal night lit by an arc-lamp 'like the moon in its opal globe.' 'What a lovely night,' says Ariadne Utterword, leaving nothing to chance, 'it seems made for us.'

In John Schlesinger's production the night is actually less magical than I have known it, perhaps because Michael Annals, having done the interior up proud in a series of monumental curves interrupted on a journey to infinity, has all but given up out of doors. The ending of the dream is here less powerful than its beginning, though Mr Schlesinger adorns his author by bringing down the garden flagstaff in the final air-raid.

But the most decisive action of the evening comes before the first line has been spoken; when Ellie Dunn drops her volume of Shakespeare and falls asleep. The play is full of references to sleep, dreams, hypnotic trances. Either Mr Schlesinger has emphasised them or I was listening for them; at any rate, the play has a visionary unity that I have never found in it before. Ellie's experiences in its hackneyed; Kate Nelligan, initially ingenuous without being mawkish, goes on to give a heartbreakingly full-blooded account of her disillusion, her rage, and her metallic realism. This is a remarkably authentic performance. Ellie's infatuation for the romantic Hector -- the cause of her 'heartbreak' -- is treated by Shaw as if it had the resonance of a grand passion; this makes her one of the play's main problems, here decisively solved.

Another is the industrialist, Boss Mangan, so baited and condemned by the assembled company that he becomes sympathetic and almost lovable. Paul Rogers does his best to guard against this in the early scenes, playing down the vulnerability and pouring oil into his voice and smile, but when he too has his heart broken we cannot help warming to him. Shaw piled it on with Mangan (even his money is shown to be illusory), so that he hardly seems worth the extermination which his author has thoughtfully provided for him. Colin Blakely's Shotover, pungently timed and alarming in distress, is a major creation; so is Anna Massey's unshakeable Ariadne.

Eileen Atkins, though she lacks the overwhelming force that Irene Worth gave the role (very useful in shaping the last act), has a fine, clear-eyed gaiety as Hesione; Alan MacNaughtan brings to Mazzini his unexcelled gift for distinguishing greyness from greyness; and Graham Crowden is a fantastical Hector. The remainder -- Edward de Souza, Patience Collier, Harry Lomax -- do not miss a beat. This is posh theatre at its best.
