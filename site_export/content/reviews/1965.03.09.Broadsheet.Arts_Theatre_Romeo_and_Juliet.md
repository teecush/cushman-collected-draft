Arts Theatre: Romeo and Juliet

In the programme-note to his Marlowe Society production Gareth Morgan lays great stress on the importance to the play of the Montague-Capulet feud, not merely as a convenient lever to set the love-story in motion, but as a positive fact in the play in its own right. His production brings this out almost too well. Despite an inappropriately chilly set the atmosphere of steamy discontent, erupting into violence, is most powerfully summoned. The small-part actors are uniformly good—they really look dangerous; a repulsively flippant Sampson and Gregory pave the way for a sleek, menacing Tybalt; not quite convincing as the fanatically punctilious duellist described by Mercutio, but admirably epitomising the perversity that turns nice young men into killers.

As a picture of strife-torn Verona the production is completely convincing; in other words, the first half (up to the death of Mercutio) is fine. At the interval I felt elated, by the end I was rather depressed. For the connection between the brawling environment and the love story it frames is never fully established. The droop is partially inherent in the play itself; Romeo offstage in Mantua for an act constitutes a formidable break in continuity which can only be bridged if the early love scenes have conditioned us to accept the protagonists as essentially inseparable. But in this production nobody is really in touch with anybody else. Marlowe tradition is flouted by the inclusion of a curtain call, but it seems curiously inapposite: it is a surprise to realise that all these disparate people have in fact been acting in the same play.

This fragmentary impression is echoed in the shape of individual performances. Juliet, in particular, is patchy. She gets the second half way off the ground with a reading of “Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds” whose unabashed relaxed eagerness engages completely and stamps her as a real actress of exceptional promise. Yet a moment later she is greeting the presumed death of her husband with, at unconvincing best, mild concern; the emotional complexities of the last scenes never get beyond hints. Her best moments—her first sight of Romeo, for example—are magic; the girl is really in love. More than can be said for her Romeo who, despite a certain morning freshness, is unremarkable, never really engaging with his own, or anyone else's character.

The best performance is probably Mercutio, who exudes all the necessary panache and manages to build his relationships with Romeo and Benvolio single-handed. The Nurse, too, after a shaky start which drowns her garrulous reminiscences in a sea of vocal fidgets, settles down into an enjoyable routine character study (though I felt that the same actress got unobtrusively to the heart of a similar character much more successfully in last year's *Galileo*). But the elders in general are not very successful: Lady Capulet pauses in the middle of an otherwise stiff and colourless performance to call down vengeance for Tybalt's death with stunning effect. Her husband fusses and chuckles his way through the play in the all-too-familiar manner. And there is an incredibly prim and platitudinous Friar Laurence whose broad grin at the idea of Juliet shut up in the Capulet tomb suggests disturbing tendencies unmentioned by Shakespeare. Unthinkable for him to belong to the same world as the richly-realised crowd. But that is the paradox of the whole production.

Robert Cushman
