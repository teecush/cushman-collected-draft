Down in the country

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on revivals of Chekhov and Synge.

THE THEATRE, a metropolitan art, is much given to proclaiming the tedium of life in the country. Two highly esteemed studies of what Wilde called agricultural depression turned up in London last week; at the Lyric a play puzzlingly billed as The Sea Gull (by Chek Hov?) took us among the rich rural Russians and the following night The Playboy of the Western World (Old Vic) exposed us to the indigent indigenous Irish.

In both societies we found heroines dreaming of escape and clutching at it in the shape of a possible lover from the outside world. It is possible that Chekhov and Synge did not mean these girls to be the precise centrepieces of their respective plays, but the performances of Helen Mirren as Nina and of Susan Fleetwood as Pegeen Mike make no other judgment possible. Both girls are a credit to the Royal Shakespeare Company, up through which they sprang.

Things in the theatre being as they are, i.e., confused, neither of them is acting in an RSC production; Miss Fleetwood is with the National Theatre, while Miss Mirren is a founder-member of the Lyric Theatre Company, a new attempt by H. M. Tennent to regain respectability if not their old prestige. The Lyric venture is true enough to West End tradition to list its players hierarchically rather than (as at the National or RSC) alphabetically; the star emphasis in advertising and publicity has been so strong that I had no idea who any of the supporting actors were until I opened my programme.

Doubtless the ensemble concept has in recent years been oversold and over-sentimentalised, and it would certainly be foolish to pretend that `The Playboy' is the work of a permanent company; none of the cast has appeared before with the National (two were in `Comedians,' but that was an importation from Nottingham) and few of them, if we look at the matter chauvinistically, have any right to be there at all. This production makes no attempt to test English actors in the disciplines -- if that's the word -- of Irish drama (as did the Olivier production of `Juno and the Paycock' with some success nine years ago), though it seems to have more faith in English actresses. And isn't it a grand thing to see Miss Fleetwood and Margaret Whiting the way they'd be exercisin' their art and thrustin' their voices into strange accents and breath-bereavin' sentences, and they surrounded by more well-thought-on Irish actors than a man could find on all the stages of the Dublin Festival not four weeks past.

The acting level in `The Playboy' -- despite the presence of such revered and authentic seniors as J. G. Devlin, Liam Redmond and Eddie Byrne -- is not in fact staggeringly high; the only outstanding supporting turn is Jim Norton's as Pegeen's hopeless suitor, prim, frightened and blushing to the roots of his sparse fair hair. The chorus of village girls go very prettily through all their motions, but are apt to look lost when called upon merely to stand still and respond. Still, much can be forgiven for our first sight of them, jammed together in the cottage doorway as they strain for a sight of Christy Mahon, playboy, potboy, and presumed parricide.

That is magic; and it is characteristic of Bill Bryden's production. He has the strongest visual sense of any of our young directors, and this is his best work that I have seen. It makes a surprisingly gentle evening; the farce of Christy's masquerade counts for considerably more than its inherent cruelty. Stephen Rea struts and cowers to the limits of the text, but never beyond them; since he never reaches very high, he has not very far to fall.

He lives less in his own right than as a thing in Pegeen's dream, and Miss Fleetwood is a beautiful dreamer. Lying barefoot before the fire at curtain-rise she spells out an order for bridal clothes with the languorous longing of a self-appointed fairy princess; confronted a moment later by a prince who, in Mr Norton's performance, bears definite traces of the frog, she unleashes her tongue to withering effect. She is a romantic scold -- a combination that encompasses her changing moods throughout the play; when it ends on a burst of anger succeeded instantly by grief, it is seen to have described a neat and satisfying circle.

Within it, Miss Whiting as Widow Quin commands considerable attention if not unvarying approval. She needs space; having discovered it -- and it is her first objective whenever she comes through the door -- she secures it by planting herself with her arms, and indeed her voice, akimbo, taking great care to melt and relax when her lines speak of loneliness. A disgracefully self-indulgent display, but not wholly resistible.

The play itself is not resistible at all; it is now the accepted thing to be bored by Synge's rhetoric, but since I was hearing it for the first time I have not had time to progress that far. Whatever the individual failings of the cast, collectively they are enchanting. But it is harder for a Chekhovian whole to be greater than the sum of its parts; and the subsidised team, unfairly confirming everyone's prejudices, make a better group showing than the commercial one.

Mr Bryden can legitimately drench his stage in atmosphere; at the Lyric, Lindsay Anderson, virtuously enough, has no truck with it. The Chekhovian texture is in fact wholly a matter of performance: the stronger the individuals, the better. They are all solitaries, but if they do not interact they at any rate bounce off one another and one weak performance -- as Kevin Stoney's Dorn here -- can dislocate several others. By the time the train of cause and effect has gone through, the whole undertow of life on the Arkadina estate has disappeared.

Only Patricia Healey's Masha, a dark rhapsody of self-pity, is fully realised.

Otherwise, our attention, in approved Shaftesbury Avenue fashion, goes to the stars, particularly -- and even more traditionally -- to the female stars. Of the men, Frank Grimes is an unusually firm Konstantin, a character who usually drowns in a wash of puppyish vulnerability -- as 10 years ago when Peter McEnery played the part just up the road. Mr McEnery is now Trigorin, but though several times the actor he was then he has not ripened into the novelist's full weariness. As Arkadina, however, Joan Plowright erupts into middle age with great spirit; her duel of affections with her son -- an encounter which reduces them to theatrical tears almost simultaneously -- is one of the production's brightest ironic episodes.

As for Miss Mirren, she begins almost as Alice in Wonderland, and is superbly unabashed about it. But along with Alice's wide-eyed hero-worship goes Alice's instinctive practicality. There is nothing distracted about her final scene; the famous antiphony (`I'm a seagull; no, I'm an actress') becomes a balancing of possibilities and there is no doubt that the second will win. She is not going to become a thing in anyone's dream. The part, though Miss Mirren finds a totally different style for it, is analogous to the rock-singer in `Teeth 'n' Smiles': both doomed in theory, both survivors in fact.

* * *

Farjeon Revue (Mermaid) is a tribute to a man announced as the best revue writer of the thirties. There must be some mistake. Caution: you may revive old songs (though these sound frail in the theatre), but old sketches are death. Revue is one form where stars are a necessity. Herbert Farjeon by all accounts had them. The Mermaid, painfully, does not.
