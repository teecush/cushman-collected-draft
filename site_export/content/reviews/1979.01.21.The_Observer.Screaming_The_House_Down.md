Screaming the house down

Theatre

Robert Cushman on a Joe Orton revival

The programme for the revival of Joe Orton's The Erpingham Camp (King's Head) sports a paragraph by the author's biographer, John Lahr, claiming the piece as Orton's great transition farce, paving the way for What the Butler Saw and second to it in merit. This, I think, is a case of biographer's bias. The Erpingham Camp is an aggressive and exuberant work, but is also repetitious.

Erpingham is a holiday camp supremo, whose subjects rebel with bloody, and to him fatal, results. "You've struck a figure of authority," says a scandalised redcoat when his chief comes under fire, approximately, the tenth time the joke has been made with half the play still to go. That Orton should have been locked into it was perhaps unavoidable given the play's premise. The camp represents the connivance of the British in their own humiliation (patronised, ballooned and bingoed into grateful submission) and the image can hardly be quarrelled with.

Orton himself, though, seems just a little too delighted with it, and the self-satisfaction shows through, particularly in a small theatre where the audience gets to share it. There is not after all much overlap between the clientele of the King's Head and that of Butlin's. The play was written for commercial television when it must have seemed far more courageously subversive.

There is still much mileage to be had from the rebellious campers, who talk mainly in headline bromides. The rising comes about when an ambitious redcoat, newly in charge of entertainments, organises a screaming contest, into which one young woman enters so enthusiastically that he has to slap her to quieten her down. Her husband, dressed for other festive purposes as Tarzan of the Apes, takes this to heart; he had married her against the odds ("Our love was banned") and, what is far more, she is pregnant. He leads the mob "for the gratification of those in the family way everywhere"; Orton spins endless verbal variations on this theme and the satire - in a society and a theatre much oppressed by slogan - is extremely satisfying.

Said to derive from The Bacchae (but then, what doesn't?), the play asks for a larger production than Islington can offer; starved of accompanying spectacle, the glibness that Orton himself recognised as his own weakest spot is more noticeable. But there is still plenty of verve and intelligence in Stewart Trotter's staging, and a prime performance of Erpingham himself, domineering and grossly epicene, by Peter Vaughan.

* * *

Jennifer Phillips's Daughters of Men (Hampstead) was once a neat radio script. On stage it seems attenuated. There are large gaps in our acquaintance with the characters, while much of what we are shown is gratuitous - and, in Nancy Meckler's production, remarkably sluggish. The main conflict is between the estranged couple for possession of their daughter. The wife, an artist's agent, seems set to keep her; financially at least she is better equipped than her husband. The issue was left teasingly in doubt for most of the original; expanded for the stage - or rather with the same material gone over more slothfully - it merely drags.

The main story is framed by an interview between the wife and a social security official; stagecraft seems to amount to giving Frances de la Tour time to change her clothes while switching between past and present. Miss de la Tour has a couple of minutes of stylised verbal comedy on the subject of toothpaste; otherwise her gifts are wasted, indeed positively misplaced. With nothing to give her pouting angularity a charge, she just mopes through the evening. (I kept wishing she were playing the self-tormenting heroine of Bedroom Farce; she might bring it off like nobody else.) Two other prominent actresses, Anna Carteret and Annabel Leventon, are spectacularly wasted (Miss Leventon never even moves) and there are a couple of wretched performances further down.

* * *

Dick Vosburgh wrote the lyrics to a very funny song called "I love a film cliche," which has led a flourishing underground life for some years now. It crops up in the first half of his double-headed entertainment A Day in Hollywood, a Night in the Ukraine (New End) and it is the key to the entire enterprise. I quote: "I love the dialogue/like 'Please don't shoot my dog/Rusty couldn't have eaten those sheep'." That, in its mocking affection, is the spirit of film-buffery.

It hangs pretty limp in A Day in Hollywood, where the elements tend to cancel each other out. There are jokes about Nelson Eddy being dumb enough to turn down a Cole Porter song; the song itself, "In the Still of the Night," is then sung perfectly straight, to a beautiful accompaniment provided by Frank Lazarus. Mr Vosburgh's taste in movie music is impeccable, but he has gone to strange lengths to get in on the stage.

There are pleasures: Paddie O'Neil doing faultless imitations of Bette Davis and (especially) Ethel Merman, a performance of "Thanks for the Memory" that stakes a claim for Leo Robin as the most underrated lyricist in America, a Depression medley including a powerful "Remember my Forgotten Man" from Maureen Scott and a charmingly wistful "With Plenty of Money and You" from Mr Lazarus. But it all needs more shape and purpose, firmer direction and a choreographer. Also more stage space, since what there is at the New End is being held in reserve for A Night in the Ukraine.

This is much jollier, being Chekhov's The Bear as it might have been done by the Marx Brothers. There is, I must admit, something self-defeating about it; you can hardly parody the Marxes, you can only compete with them. Mr Vosburgh has hit off the style to perfection and many of his cracks are every bit as good as S. J. Perelman's: Chico of Margaret Dumont - "She's a-more than unhappy, she's a-house-broken"; Groucho of Chico - "I'm afraid this story has an unhappy ending, folks, he doesn't die"; Harpo of Groucho - any number of toots on a motor horn.

The song of self-introduction which Messrs Vosburgh and Lazarus have dreamed up for Groucho as Serge B. Samovar, the crooked lawyer, is quite perfect, and this unimpersonatable performer is actually brought off uncannily well by John Bay. Sheila Steafel's Harpo actually goes beyond imitation, having the delicacy and gusto of a genuine creation. And Mr Lazarus's Chico signals a welcome triple talent: as composer, pianist and Italian. There are two funny lovers as well. That it has all been done before is the evening's limitation, but also its glory.

* * *

The Grand Magic Circus are back in town, this time at the Shaftesbury which barn they almost succeed in humanising. I never tire of listening to their presiding genius, Jerome Savary, bidding us a mournful but obviously genuine welcome in his individually elongated English. ("Tonighter we are performing the entire show in Breetsher.") He looks and sounds like a mad undertaker, though for much of this show he is dressed as a Caliph, this show being 1001 Nights.

Scheherazade is explicitly disowned (things, we are given to understand, never work out as happily as that), but the GMC's technique of stopping and starting stories within the stories can get irritating. Chinese boxes full of shaggy dogs. The music and spectacle are well meant, as are the performances, but the really interesting objects are likely to be mechanical; a giant American eagle, for example, which comes down, I think, to illustrate a fable about capitalism. I would also like to mention a pantomime camel.
