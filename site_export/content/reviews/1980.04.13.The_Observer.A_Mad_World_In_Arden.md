A mad world in Arden

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Stratford, Sheffield and London.

THE Stratford season has begun with As You Like It, very brightly. I have never known the first scenes to go at such a lick.

Any actor of Orlando, faced with the appallingly stiff, expository opening, is liable to devote his energies to persuading you that you have never heard it before; I assumed that this was what John Bowe was up to here, and applauded both the effort and the result. When Jonathan Hyde proceeded to give the full melodramatic works to his big bad brother Oliver, and when even the very minor servant Dennis shot off his two functional lines in a similar ecstasy of bad temper, I took it all merely for an extension of the same kindly process.

I was grateful, too, for a thrilling wrestling match in which Terry Wood's Charles looks so huge and fights so dirty that Sinead Cusack's Celia is provoked into assisting his opponent with some fierce maidenly pummelling. She comes indeed from a formidable family; Bruce Purchase as her usurping father quivers all over when banishing people (his favourite occupation), while Trevor Baxter as the rightful Duke lectures his followers on the virtues of the simple life with an oily self-righteousness that would be scary if it wasn't so entertaining.

By this point the story is more or less over, the real business of the comedy is beginning, and Terry Hands's production might be expected to let up somewhat. Not a bit of it: 'Love,' declares Rosalind, 'is merely a tame madness'; Mr Hands glosses over the adjective and goes straight for the noun. Silvius, for example, goes around with his shepherd's crook behind his neck, and holding on to it with both hands as if crucified.

Mr Hands's approach yields plenty of passing pleasures and some illumination. There is the moment, for example, when Derek Godfrey's Jaques, melancholy cloak outspread, advances on Rosalind in what others believe to be an attempt at rape (and who knows whether he thinks her a boy or girl?), but which I, in my innocence, took for an endeavour to convert her to his own philosophy by enfolding her in it. I liked, too, Joe Melia's professionally funny Touchstone and Raymond Westwell's Corin.

But in the end (and conceivably influenced by memories of gentler productions) I was unmoved. In particular, I found it difficult to get the measure of this Rosalind. Her work at the National established Susan Fleetwood as the most enchanting actress never to crack a smile, and I had looked forward to seeing her happy for once. In this production she can be merry all right, but she can seldom relax; the heart of both the character and the play is bypassed.

* * *

The RSC's Three Sisters (Warehouse) is a virtuoso production, but you are hardly aware of the fact until it is over. Trevor Nunn's direction is a feat of both emotional and spatial geometry; at any given moment three lines of feeling are criss-crossing one another on stage and you can see them all. I have never known a production that made so clearly one of the crucial Chekhovian points: that one person's suffering is another's oblivion.

The confined space helps, of course; putting us in the same room as the characters cuts down on comic amplitude, but makes tragedy horribly immediate. Pathos, though, is not indulged; every character save one is allowed a single moment of heart-break, or the memory of one; each is fully but sparely taken with no wallowing.

The one untouched figure is Susan Tracy's Natasha, harshly humiliated at the beginning and thereafter undemonstratively destructive; this is a much improved performance. Similarly Edward Petherbridge's Vershinin, which I liked the least at Stratford last year, is now almost the best, slipping in and out of relationships with a kind of glazed, well-meaning courtliness. 'Let's philosophise,' he says languidly; and Roger Rees's Tusenbach, infinitely trusting, takes him seriously.

Janet Dale's quietly defeated Olga and Emily Richard's Irina, progressively booby-trapped by existence, flank a Masha from Suzanne Bertish that is absorbing and, in its emphasis on predatory self-pity, strikingly unsentimental, but is sometimes--in its dropping of significant words into careful pauses--too obviously calculated. Bob Peck's Soliony, murderous in his vulnerability, broods over the play and is almost the key-figure. The production's aural elements have been as carefully calibrated as its austere visuals.

* * *

There was once a community drama company that stood outside Tesco's, haranguing housewives. 'Women of Britain, what do you want?' it asked. 'We want to get in to Tesco's,' they replied. I don't know what that anecdote does for you in print, but as delivered by Victoria Wood, who also wrote it, it creased me up. In her new sort-of-musical, Good Fun (Crucible, Sheffield), Miss Wood has consistently provided herself, in the time-honoured role of heroine's best friend, with the funniest lines recently heard on a British stage. Or is it the way she tells them? Either way, I laughed till I cried, Observer.

She also has, on this occasion, a theme. The five female characters in this piece all have pleasure, or the lack of it, on their minds; sometimes they advise one another on how or where it can be found, generally with disastrous results. Miss Wood is quite the most accomplished songwriter we have, and when her people use her nimble, observant lyrics to explain themselves or their situations, life is joyful. When she uses song to move her plot along, her muse clogs up, possibly because it isn't much of a plot to start with.

Annabel Leventon plays (very well) a community arts administrator with a talent for attracting the wrong men, if any. She is eternally hopeful until, at the end, she lets all her bitterness out. But she is not kept firmly enough in sight, and Miss Wood seems to be making up the supporting action as she goes along.

Men are allowed to appear, but not to sing, which in a musical is as sexist (and dramatically unsatisfying) as you can get. The production tries too hard, but the performances are good--or, in the case of Julie Walters's cosmetics saleslady, travelling with a heart-shaped box of samples, rather extraordinary.

* * *

On inspection, all the best songs of the rock 'n' roll era turn out to have been written by Jerry Leiber and Mike Stoller. Apart from Randy Newman, they are the only rock writers whose work, with its tightly inventive rhythms and wryly detailed lyrics, sounds potentially theatrical. That in fact is the way they have gone in the past decade, and the highlight of Only in America (Roundhouse), Ned Sherrin's compilation of their work, is a song that was actually written for a show. It is called 'I Ain't Here' and is sung at an ironing-board by the magnificent Bertice Reading, as a black home-help taking a dreamy revenge on her white employer.

Others of the later, greater Leiber-Stoller songs come off less well; Val Pringle, a large resounding bass, sings a few lines of 'Feeling Too Good Today Blues' and is then silenced; the peerlessly sexy 'Some Cats Know' falls victim to its staging and also to the fact that, unless Miss Reading is singing (and she makes far more sense of 'Hound Dog' than Presley ever did), Mr Leiber's lyrics are only audible when the wind and the sound-system are blowing in the right direction.

The idea of the show--to create a tapestry of song untrammelled by dialogue--is fine, and most of the staging ideas are ingenious. They haven't, though, been very well executed, apart from one orgiastic sequence in the necking-section of a movie theatre. Being young in Brooklyn, 1959, is the theme, and as good as any; but few of the performers make much impact, and the designs are drab far beyond the call of nostalgia.

* * *

Andrei Serban's staging of The Umbrellas of Cherbourg (Phoenix), with the characters slinking round mobile perspex screens in sumptuous lighting by David Hersey, is extremely accomplished. That, however, doesn't save the evening. Michel Legrand's score runs to two pleasant tunes, the makings of a third, and an infinity of thin recitative. Sheldon Harnick's lyrics sound as ludicrous as would anyone's who tried to make dramatic points over so characterless an accompaniment.

The story is of boy losing girl and of both achieving a pained serenity; I might as well admit that their last meeting choked me up a bit. Heroine's mother runs an umbrella shop and goes bankrupt; since, in this Cherbourg, no one is ever seen without an umbrella, it is hard to see how.
