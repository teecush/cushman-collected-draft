Malfi and Merchant

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Paris.

A COUPLE of supporting roles are played with less definition than before. The acoustics at the Roundhouse will never be as good as those at the Royal Exchange. These things apart, on its journey from Manchester to London, Adrian Noble's production of The Duchess of Malfi has done nothing but improve.

It seems to owe little to currently approved trends, since it is neither puritanically simple nor outrageously ornate. Lucid and decorative it is, in a style that I imagine Guthrie or the young Peter Brook might have approved. Sound and light are used with an emphasis that can only be described--with considerable gratitude--as old-fashioned.

As the revenge-plot goes into gear the three villains--Ferdinand, Bosola, the Cardinal--are each seen bathed in a separate spot, apart but complementary. Earlier on Duke, Duchess and Cardinal have frozen under waxen beams, while Antonio, normally lit, delivers his appraisal of their characters. Mr Noble's production cannot merely move easily from one style to another, it can accommodate two simultaneously; which is what Webster's text itself does.

There is a nice discrimination in darkness: a black-out for the imprisoned Duchess's reception of her husband's severed hand, but full visibility for the final massacre, whose confusions pass without a single titter.

If there is a Royal Exchange style, it is rooted in the enclosing use of sound. Here the effects, duly haunting, are supplemented by George Fenton's music, which comes in, cruelly but not sadistically, on every climax. The arena stage, meanwhile, lays the play, its inhabitants and its society, open before us. It is traversed in the first, courtly, half by a royal strip of carpet leading to the ducal throne; after the interval the throne is replaced by a prison chair, almost an instrument of torture; and the carpet is blood-stained.

Images crowd the memory: the Cardinal tied to his bed with silken ropes while his mistress humps him amid clouds of incense; the Duchess's courtiers surrounding her in a tight, sycophantic circle while the honest villain Bosola stands contemptuously outside; the madmen brought for the Duchess's torment in a cage on wheels, which falls open with a hideous clank.

Or there is the embrace bestowed by Mike Gwilym's Ferdinand on his sister, the Duchess, and with which he all but strangles her; his performance is now brilliantly uncompromising in its madness, and is perfectly set off by the suavity of Julian Curry's Cardinal. Peter Postlethwaite carves a real person out of the upright Antonio.

Bob Hoskins brings Bosola earthily to life before your eyes, but his voice is still not supple enough for all the shades the script requires of it. (The mechanical muffling of his bellman's epitaph--'of what is't fools make such vain keeping'--is the only gaffe in the production.)

Helen Mirren starts out a trifle cool, especially when choosing a husband; she is better at giving orders than at giving herself. But she rises to the prison sequences. Her actual execution--with the strangling of her maid as its electric coda--is superbly done.

The production's final achievement is that it duplicates the precarious balancing of Webster's forces, admitting a fascination in cruelty, but never wallowing in it. It makes most modern drama look childish. In its technique and its taste this production is something of a prodigy; I would put money on Mr Noble turning out the best classical director to appear since Trevor Nunn.

* * *

Next to him I would rank Mike Alfreds of Shared Experience; though his Merchant of Venice (Collegiate) is disappointing. (I saw it, though, at a dress rehearsal, when there were obviously few of us to share the experience anyway.) The production is staged in commedia half-masks, with Shylock a hunched Pantaloon. All the characters are seen in a harsh comic light and none of them emerges very creditably.

Maybe it was the masks that activated this approach, but they impede the play it has developed. The play has obviously been considered with great care, the actors driven to explore character and relationships. Portia delivers 'the quality of mercy' with unusual force, right at Shylock, so one wants to see how he reacts to it: not just in his stance and voice, but in his face. The full, direct acting that was always this company's strength has been thrown away and -- despite many passing subtleties in the production -- nothing has replaced it.

There is, of course, another argument for the masks; they are a method of getting through the play with eight actors. So, Philip Osment combines a stonily self-regarding Antonio with a deftly improvising Launcelot Gobbo. Most of these actors are musicians as well, but they are generally below the standard of Mr Alfreds's previous ensembles and try to colour the verse by delivering it in italics with exclamation marks at the end.

* * *

I'm Getting My Act Together and Taking It On the Road (Apollo) is a feminist primer disguised as a musical. The disguise is a thin one; what we get is a nightclub act with comments. Diane Langton plays an American singer whom we see rehearsing her new routine. Rightly tiring of the characterless ballads that have apparently made her famous, she intends to replace them with affirmative musical statements that turn out to be equally nondescript, but which have a surprisingly incendiary effect on Ben Cross, who plays her manager.

He thinks they will upset the audience, though heaven knows why; they sound indistinguishable from the self-pitying epics that have been pouring out of the radio for the last decade. The punters might of course complain that they have heard it all before, and that there is no point in listening again to complaints about a woman's role being predetermined from the cradle unless there is some individuality in the expression.

But this isn't what the manager has in mind. In fact it turns out that what he has in mind is his own marriage: a blackmailing baby-doll, a relationship whose falsity is exposed by Miss Langton's new image. Well, maybe it is; it's hard to say, since we never get a look at it. The contest is rigged.

There is this eternal thing in drama called conflict and, without wanting to lay down any iron-clad rules, I would hazard that the best way of expressing it in a musical is to let the protagonists attack each other with songs. Here Miss Langton sings, Mr Cross doesn't.

Actually the Gretchen Cryer-Nancy Ford songs aren't that terrible, but they leave no mark; as in most sort-of-rock musicals, they begin with a splash and then just jump on the spot for several minutes.

* * *

Balconville (Old Vic) takes us to Canada, and it takes Canada to the 'here is life, look at it' stage of drama. The scene is a Montreal tenement inhabited by two English families and a French one; the area is characterised by rickety housing, mutual suspicion between the races and 40 per cent unemployment.

The author, David Fennario, injects a lot of observation, a respectable amount of humour and a minimum of pathos. Soap-opera it isn't, since it lacks both sentimentality and contrived suspense; drama it isn't quite, at least not at this oceanic distance. One offers thanks for information received rather than for enlightenment.

The acting, by Montreal's Centaur Company, is a credit to whoever wants to claim it, though only one player--a crackling rose called Susan Wright--transcends type.

* * *

I have too little space to do justice to The Cherry Orchard, staged by Peter Brook at his unchanging but protean Bouffes-du-Nord in Paris. Here the peeling walls of the theatre do duty for those of the Ranevsky estate; the upper levels spring into life during the packing and clearing of the last act; every cranny is being searched.

By the end you feel you know the house if not the orchard, and its selling--though no ideological line is pushed--hurts. The final sounds of axe-strokes and twanging string are of Royal Exchange standard; and this production too excels in the atmospheric use of carpets. The third act, the party, is not just a series of dances, but one long dance.

Natasha Parry plays Lioubov and Joseph Blatchley Trofimov, but the actors are predominantly French; Firs, indeed, looks like a Parisian maitre d'hotel, a splendid apparition, though jarring a little with his more xenophobic utterances. Best of a round and rounded dozen is Catherine Frot's maid Douniacha, wide-eyed and vulnerable to everything, from the return of the family to the lecherous assaults of the valet. The acting reaffirms that Mr Brook's virtues are thoroughly traditional.
