Play of the book

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a successful adaptation of Evelyn Waugh.

I DON'T want all plays to be based on novels. I don't even want most plays to be based on novels. But such adaptations have provided some of the finest theatrical evenings of the last few years, the latest being Shared Experience's version of A Handful of Dust at the Lyric, Hammersmith. It has been depressing to find this production, a full two years after Nicholas Nickleby exploded at the Aldwych and with the television version now going full blast, greeted with the same old objections.

The original reviews of Nickleby were the most disgraceful I have ever read. Obviously they were not as vituperative as Clement Scott on Ghosts or the daily prints of 1958 on The Birthday Party. But for those writers there was much excuse; they were reflecting public distaste or bewilderment, and were actually helping the plays to become embattled legends. Where would minority art be without the philistines?

But what happened at Nickleby, not least on the first night, was public acceptance and public joy. The flow of feeling from stage to auditorium and back was as near to being an objective fact as anything in the theatre can be. By ignoring it, or wishing it away, or pettishly denying its right to exist because it carried the wrong identity card, theatre critics were committing the ultimate trahison des clercs. And they're still at it.

A Handful of Dust has not the same scale or intensity, Waugh being in every way a smaller writer than Dickens. But there is a moment at the end, when Tony Last realises he is trapped forever by Mr Todd, when the same kind of fusion takes place. The audience gasps, then smiles; gasps at the cruelty, smiles at the elegance both of the thing itself and of its congruence with all that has gone before.

Of course you can get this from reading the book, but that is hardly an argument against enjoying it in the theatre as well. And of course the comedy translates superbly from page to stage. Most of us, when we read something that makes us laugh, want to share the joke, and this is one of the basic instincts informing Shared Experience. It is overwhelmingly sound. (I assume you would rather laugh in company than alone. I mean, what are you, some kind of pervert?)

And the purists are terribly inconsistent. Nobody objects to novels being turned into films. Come to that, no theatre critics objected to Fanshen or The Ragged Trousered Philanthropists deriving from books. Presumably their political credentials got them through. And nobody has jumped on Brecht for adapting The Good Soldier Schweik or the authors of Guys and Dolls for resorting to Runyon. It must be who you know.

Shared Experience are their own kind of purists. They employ no adaptors, use no additional dialogue. Every word in their four-part Bleak House was by Dickens; who, neatly enough, supplies the only words in A Handful of Dust that are not Waugh's. (When Tony starts reading the complete works to Mr Todd, you half expect the company to begin acting them out.)

Ten actors, grey-suited or grey-gowned, appear before a panelled set, to Ilona Sekacz's haunting distillation of 1930s cocktail music. There is less instantaneous doubling than there was in Bleak House, and the characterisations are necessarily slenderer, but they are as precise and enjoyable as ever. Sam Dale, who has a small moustache, hardly impersonates the exasperating child John Andrew, but he so precisely captures his rhythm that it is a jolt to find him playing adults later on.

Alison Steadman has a high time with the self-dramatising Jenny Abdul Akbar, Philip Voss's unassuming Todd is doom itself, James Smith is fuzzily upstanding as Jock, whose conscientious inquisitiveness about pigs is a charming running gag, and Nick Dunning is all calculating precocity as Beaver, the extra man who becomes the other man. These and other cameos surround John Price's full-length portrait of Tony; aptly named Last, emerging innocent and constrained, from his Gothic pile to be hacked down by savages before he even reaches the jungle.

The actors speak narrative as well as dialogue; the third-person convention is worrying for two minutes, acceptable and enriching thereafter. The production may be too prodigal with group mime; we could listen happily to Mr Tendril's sermon without seeing his congregation. But such devices pay off in, for example, the staging of the hunt. An earlier school of adaptation would have left this offstage, which may be what brought adaptation into disrepute; we were merely offered chunks of conversation, often in paraphrase, which were called scenes.

The new theatrical form is fluent, inclusive, and contrapuntal. It is also faithful to its author's style. This version is fast, but never rushed. It represents another triumph for Mike Alfreds, who is known by most people in the theatre, and by a faithful audience, to be among our best directors, but whom few critics seem disposed to recognise.

* * *

Ducking Out (Greenwich) is a heavily punning title, referring both to avoidance tactics for dealing with life (you act nice like a Christian, bend your energies to constructing a crib, and, when it all gets too much, have a stroke) and for a Christmas bird favoured by those who don't like turkey. It has been furnished by Mike Stott for a comedy by Eduardo de Filippo, whose scene he has transferred to Lancashire.

The origins keep breaking through; the obsessive Catholicism or a family quarrel in which the antagonists pick knives from the table keep turning our thoughts back to Naples. In its own right, though, Mr Stott's adaptation is skilful and funny, and it never sentimentalises. Indeed, by coming closer to us, it hits harder as what looks like a charming, if barbed, family comedy turns steadily grimmer, its protagonist ending up bedridden, misunderstood and misunderstanding.

It is the kind of finale Alan Ayckbourn sometimes strives for, but brought off with less obvious effort. Warren Mitchell in the leading role has a northern accent far gentler and subtler than southerners generally attempt, and his whole characterisation flowers from it. In Mike Ockrent's production he is excellently supported by wife (Gillian Barge), daughter (Diane Bull) and a large, obstreperous contingent of family and friends.

* * *

It has been said that Kevin Elyot's Coming Clean (Bush), about the break-up of a homosexual marriage, would not have been produced had it been--as it could easily be--heterosexual. This may well be the case; but actually a hetero play on, or with, these lines would be quite acceptably entertaining until betrayed set in and the words turned weepy, and the same is true of this gay version.

There is a very good performance of an acerbic American writer-academic by Philip Donaghy, an aptly twitchy account of his spouse by Eamon Boland, and admirable work from C. J. Allen as a compulsive cruiser (the only camp one) and Ian McMurrach as The Threat, an unemployed actor written and played with obvious conviction.
