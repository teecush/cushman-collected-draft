Rescuing Candide

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Festival theatre.

Twenty-five years ago Voltaire's Candide was turned into a musical by a legion of celebrities: Leonard Bernstein wrote the music, Lillian Hellman the book, and Tyrone Guthrie directed. The original lyricist, John Latouche, died on the job and was replaced, very wittily, by Richard Wilbur, with additional contributions from Mr Bernstein, Miss Hellman and Dorothy Parker.

It must, when the show failed, have been difficult to know whom to blame, but Mr Bernstein generally escaped censure. His music paid ironic, but innately tuneful, tribute to all the conventions of light opera; if it sounded pleased with itself, it had good reason. The cast album became a classic, and the show a retrospective cult.

Some 20 years on, Harold Prince examined it, and decided that it had all been too solemn. He engaged Hugh Wheeler to write a script that should be glancingly picaresque like Voltaire's, rather than stately and well-made like Miss Hellman's. This reordering of the book meant a reshuffling of the score, and a fifth lyricist was called in: Stephen Sondheim. (He did a workmanlike job, but no more.) Mr Prince dispensed with conventional staging. He directed the action on a series of islands dotted around the theatre floor, with the audience seated in the midst. It was fun.

Long awaited, this adaptation has been brought to Edinburgh's Assembly Hall by the Birmingham Rep. Peter Farago's production differs from Mr Prince's in confining itself to the platform stage, on which Poppy Mitchell has designed a heavily hilled and castellated set. I felt at first that an opportunity had been missed, especially since the National Theatre showed last year in their promenade 'Passion' how effectively space could be transformed. Compared with the New York production, Mr Farago's lacks inventiveness and pace; there are holes between episodes, orchestral tempi drag, and numbers which should bring down the house tail off to be greeted by polite applause.

But as the show progresses, Mr Farago succeeds where Mr Prince faltered; he maintains, and even increases, our interest in the story. Vocals are excellent: 'Glitter and Be Gay,' the variant Jewel Song, in which the much-ravished Princess Cunegonde decides that if rape is inevitable then rich is better, is a gift to an agile soprano; Mr Bernstein differs from other composers in his realisation that coloratura is intrinsically funny and Rosemary Ashe, with a fine air of impudent innocence, builds on that.

William Relton finds both melody and humour in the guileless hero, while Nichola McAuliffe's Old Lady is a tremendous comic presence, equally adroit at letting off one-liners, telling long hard-luck stories, or just singing. Binding all together is Nickolas Grace, who nonchalantly knocks off five roles including Voltaire as narrator and doddering cynic, and Dr Pangloss as pedant and irresponsible optimist. The folly of his position -- in a world that harbours cannibalism, earthquakes, warfare and the Spanish Inquisition -- is thoroughly, not to say repeatedly, rammed home.

For all the pleasures of script, score and performance, doubts remain. Maybe the original, necessarily one-dimensional, always resists dramatisation. I doubt if Mr Wheeler's book, entertaining though it is, really improves on Miss Hellman's; it loses in spreading the farcical miseries around, rather than concentrating them on Candide and Cunegonde, and the songs never quite fit; they were written, after all, for different situations, and in another, more grandiose, style. (In addition, their wit is static; there is nothing much to do with the songs beyond getting up and singing them.) It remains a distinguished musical rather than a great one, but it's a more festive event than is usually found at festivals and it should come to London. Why indeed should it not reopen the Old Vic?

* * *

Its Edinburgh stable-mate is As You Like It, performed on the same set gone green. Costumes are Regency-to-Watteau, the ladies' hats contriving to obscure large portions of the action. David Rintoul is a virile Orlando; Lynn Dearth as Rosalind begins and ends in ravishing solemnity, but jumps about inordinately between times. Mr Grace performs relentlessly as Touchstone, never allowing the clown a moment off duty, but since the production makes nonsense of his relation with Audrey (Miss McAuliffe amusing again) he may have had no choice. The senior gentlemen are uniformly reliable.

Shakespeare based this play on a novel, which brings it into line with 'Candide,' and with Richard Crane's Brothers Karamazov, played in a low-ceilinged eyrie at the top of the Freemasons Hall. Only the brothers -- four, including Smerdyakov, the bastard -- appear, though each has a go at playing Karamazov pere. Already a point is being made, and a strong one; indeed this production, in less than two hours' playing time, gives an amazing impression of having left nothing out.

There is, if anything, too much story; the inquiries as to Dmitry's whereabouts on the night of the crime become wearisome; and this passionate brother -- theoretically the easiest dramatic meat -- goes for least, though Bruce Alexander certainly acts him well. Ivan, on the other hand, all intellectualising, is excellent value: Alan Rickman, sardonically intelligent as usual, extracts terrific drama from the fable of the Grand Inquisitor and the visitation of the Devil; a devastating commentary on nihilism emerges as the joint creation of novelist and playwright. Stephen Boxer's Alyosha is a bit shadowy but, like his incidental music, haunting; Peter Kelly's Smerdyakov a fawning masterpiece.

* * *

Foreign companies have all been classically inclined. The Theatre de la Salamandre, from Lille, offered in the Moray House Gymnasium Racine's Britannicus. A programme note waggishly suggested that to a modern audience (one that has seen 'I Claudius' in its millions) Ancient Rome means nothing, so we got a Louis XIV Versailles setting, with tell-tale modern traces. A joke in fact on the company's own achievement, which is to give us Racine contemporary but whole: echoing his structural confinement by angling the set to allow the smallest possible acting area, and taking the unity of time literally, so that the first act is played before shuttered windows and the fifth in encroaching dusk. Subfusc staging can never have been so effective, setting off luminous, wonderfully spoken performances by Marief Guitier as a stifling Agrippina, Jacques Bonnaffe as a Nero with a strange air of innocence, and Marie Boitel as an ominous waiting woman.

* * *

Meanwhile at the Lyceum (and this week at the Roundhouse in London) the Amphitheatre of Athens offers Iphigenia in Lixourion, an eighteenth-century comic version of the legend of the sacrificial maiden, here presumed to be performed by locally rude mechanicals such as a candle-lighter (an even more specialised trade than bellows-mending).

The actress in the title-role is characterised as 'a harmless insane young girl'; Leda Tassopoulou plays her with a most expressive twitch of the cheek and a charming air of removing herself from the proceedings when they become too silly. She makes the first half engaging and sometimes touching; the second, straying far from the myth into traditional farce, probably works better if you understand the joke.
