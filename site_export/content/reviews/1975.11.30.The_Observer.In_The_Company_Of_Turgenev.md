In the company of Turgenev

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

SOMEBODY said to me last week that a new group of actors will always seize upon a Russian classic as their initiation rite. Chekhov and Turgenev are not merely playwrights; they signify comradeship, companies, unselfish teamwork and good parts for everybody. Company Theatre began its year's tenancy of Greenwich with "Three Sisters"; Lindsay Anderson's Lyric players launched themselves with "The Seagull"; and now the Prospect Theatre Company has come determinedly to town with A Month in the Country (Albery).

This, so far as its principal casting is concerned, is the same production we saw at Chichester last year. Robin Archer's sets remain, looking, as is the way when open-stage designs are placed behind a proscenium, ingenious but draughty. But there is another kind of nip in the air and it is considerably more bracing.

At Chichester, deprived of curtains, Toby Robertson brought his cast on formally to music, and the artificial note never quite departed the performance thereafter. The characters were all branded as poseurs, which, considering that one or two of them are honest, was both unsubtle and unjust. Now, however, the music is gone and with it the languor. Turgenev's comedy can never have seemed quite so hard, quite so funny, quite so English.

I find this admirable, since it is often complained that the English theatre's way with the Russians is droopy and lachrymose. This view seems to be based on productions of Chekhov that everybody knows about, but nobody has actually seen. Most of the English Chekhov I remember has been spruce and -- as far as the plays allowed -- cheerful. If they were not uproarious this may be because Chekhov was kidding us or himself when he claimed that they were.

The failed doctors of "Uncle Vanya" and "Three Sisters" get bitterly drunk; they do not, like their confrere in "A Month in the Country," blithely admit their incompetence ("If you get ill don't come to me") in the middle of a proposal of marriage. This scene -- which yields practically a laugh a line -- is not so much beyond Chekhov's mature range as irrelevant to it, but it fits easily into the tradition of Middleton, Wycherley and Maugham. The play's world is one of sexual and mercenary intrigue and the doctor is its prime survivor; he begins the play with two objectives -- a wife and a team of horses -- and ends in possession of both. Nearly everybody else has lost something.

Timothy West's medic has grown inward since Chichester; less weight is given to his contempt for others, more to his disgust with himself. Mr West, buoyantly enjoyable, makes his points lightly but firmly. In fact this is just about the best-acted play in the West End. "No Man's Land" is the only competition, and Dorothy Tutin's performance can rank with Gielgud's; it covers a similar range with a similar economy of means. Her Natalya, reaching for something she hopes may be passion and wounding everybody in the process, shelters behind a radiant, teasing, and almost continuous smile: a smile which refuses to budge. The charm which has been her refuge in the end becomes her prison; the mask usurps the face.

She is contrasted with the honest merriment of Jane Lapotaire's Vera, who has not yet learned to lie; Miss Lapotaire's beaky schoolgirl seems an excellent reading (it makes Natalya's jealousy the more ironic for being fundamentally unnecessary), though I imagine that when she proclaims "I'm a woman now" she is attempting an instant transformation which she fails to accomplish. Derek Jacobi, John Turner and Carol Gillies remain and have grown; and the tutor, Michael Howarth, shiny but unkempt, gives the production a new convincing centre, justifying both the women's emotions and his own bewilderment.

Turgenev turns his psychological screws exquisitely and often; too often really -- the middle of the play abounds in scenes that explore the characters without actually developing the drama. Theatrical narrative is always a problem to a novelist like Turgenev; so, it seems, is fictional narrative.

* * *

Oh, dear yes, the novel tells a story, said E. M. Forster, wishing it didn't; and Richard Cottrell's dramatisation of A Room with A View, which is Prospect's second Albery offering, copes as best as it can with this odious necessity.

The characters are sent sprinting (or strolling, or -- through some pleasant stylisation -- bicycling) after the narrative. Catching up with it, they get moral and start "placing" one another, as we used to say in Eng. Lit. This is another example of what cultivated actors are likely to wish upon themselves and their audience; if Bloomsbury lives, it lives in the theatre.

Those who have not read the book may miss much of the fun: may wonder, for example, what Lynn Farleigh is doing in such a strained and unsympathetic role, the guardian of a repressive morality that condemns itself in five minutes of stage time. In fact there is more to Miss Farleigh's character than that, but it is revealed, flatly and belatedly, in a conversation behind her back -- too late to give her performance any help. As the girl who escapes her influence (and, by implication, that of Edwardian polite society), Miss Lapotaire is fresh if oddly monotonous; Mr Jacobi as the suitor she almost accepts copes gamely and deliciously with his creator's disapproval. (There is no scorn like that of an English novelist for a character who transgresses his scheme of values.) Mr West, very kind, and Mr Howarth, very bright, do as well with the hardly less onerous burden of his good-will.

* * *

At the Young Vic two more books have been theatricalised: John Lennon's In His Own Write and A Spaniard in the Works are dished up by Denise Coffey as All Walks of Leg and house a major comic performance from Hugh Hastings as a school-mistress. School, rather than home (as in the more sentimental National Theatre version of a few years ago) is the centre of Miss Coffey's adaptation. But Mr Lennon's aimless punning is still very wearing; and the show, for all its energy, is shapeless. It also threatens to be endless, though the last line when it comes is very good.
