Doubts about Brecht

ONE distinguishing mark of the well-intentioned critic is his tendency to wake in the middle of the night in a cold sweat, full of doubts about Brecht.

It has recently been possible to cast these doubts away on the grounds that we have only been seeing his minor plays. The Good Woman of Setzuan (Royal Court) puts an end to that. It does not, however, sweeten our dreams one whit.

Keith Hack's direction simplifies the play and confuses the audience. The main pitfall of The Good Woman is that its point may well seem to have been made before its story has even begun. Fifteen minutes in, we are well aware that the good nature of Shen Te, the prostitute made rich by a gift from the gods, will expose her to the rapacity of her neighbours; another 15, and we know that she can only be protected by her ruthless male cousin Shui Ta, actually herself in disguise. The interest lies in seeing Brecht spin out variations on his theme; each narrative complication embodies some new paradox. Hence the clear lights and cool playing of Brechtian tradition.

Confined as we have recently been to the earlier Brecht, a new tradition has grown up among us: violent, abrupt and formless. The Good Woman is accordingly performed on a set composed of oil-drums that come in for a fearsome banging during the trial-scene. When the spectators are not being deafened, they are being harangued, less from political passion than as an ego-trip for director and performers. (It is, of course, often difficult to tell these two motives apart.)

The poor people of Setzuan are given a set of sneering creepy-crawly performances. Only two elements of the presentation seem calculated to provide any pleasure: Stephen Oliver's Chinese Palm Court music, and Janet Suzman's pliant and imaginative performance of Shen Te. Miss Suzman's warmth (not really counterbalanced by her outline sketch of Shui Ta) underlines an implicit contradiction in the play; the very existence of a good woman shadows the author's materialism. Brecht himself was aware of the contradiction, and explored it in the scenes involving the gods, here mostly cut. In what remains they are shown, not merely as ineffectual moralists, but as seedy poseurs.

Divinity, all the same, has had a good run this week. The Mermaid have brought us The Fire That Consumes, originally La Ville dont le prince est un enfant, study of a Catholic boys' school between the wars rife with amities particulieres. Special friendships is a less resonant English substitute, and though this production restores the Puddle Dock tradition of putting neglected works on to the Mermaid stage, it also honours the less reputable practice of mugging them once they get there.

When the older schoolboy of the play's twosome proclaims his passion to be higher than that of Racine's Andromache, he clues us in as to feeling and style. They may not be attainable by an English cast; certainly the hearty juveniles of this version get nowhere near. With its ice melted the play looks for much of the time like a protracted and mealy-mouthed restatement of a theme once thought daring.

Only in the last act--a confrontation between the schoolmaster Abbe himself in love with the younger boy, and his Superior who brings the fact home to him--do the actors seem to have an idea what they are about. Even this scene, however, has been misconceived; it is directed not as a duel but as a bear-baiting, with an aggrieved Nigel Hawthorne circling around an impassive David William. And Mr Hawthorne is not really the actor either for the Abbe's profession of faith in human love or for his self-scourging at the end. We get at best the underside of the sympathetic housemaster in an English prep-school story.

Montherlant's play was banned for years by the author's own wish. It is well known that Dennis Potter's television play Brimstone and Treacle has been banned for a shorter period in opposite circumstances. A stage version has now been given an excellent production in the Crucible Studio, Sheffield.

Technically it gives no hint of its origin; the action stays in one room with no rambling. But it still, like most TV scripts in the theatre, lacks texture. Characters and situations, designed to be highlighted by the camera, now look opaque--simple without being clear. Teasing ambiguities turn into bald conundrums.

A diabolic young man, dead keen on house-cleaning (with a duster, indeed, hanging from his waist where his tail should be), invades the home of a teenage girl whom a street-accident has turned into a vegetable. He charms her mother, even for a moment her oppressive, guilt-ridden, Right-leaning father; while they are out of the house he rapes the girl. His action shocks her into recovery; whether this is intentional benevolence or an unexpected by-product we are not told. (I suspect the latter but choose for yourself which would be the more ironic.)

Why a devil should want to rape an unconscious girl is mysterious; he could hardly hope to get at her soul that way. We could--though it makes a less intriguing play that way--dismiss all thoughts of the supernatural, enjoy the dazzling display of soothing platitudes with which Mr Potter has furnished him, and take the Prince of Darkness for a hypocritical young man from the Festival of Light: Housewives' Choice made flesh. He doesn't just dispatch 'mumsy' to the flicks; he adjures her to catch up with The Sound of Music. At moments Mantovani fills the air. Media treacle is made to seem more hellish than traditional brimstone; I would just like to know if one has replaced the other or if they are working in cahoots. In this form the play is interesting without being exciting, frightening without being really gripping.

It is the first offering in a promising season of new plays at the Crucible. Meanwhile the Citizens, Glasgow, continue their hardly less enterprising season of old ones; plays we have never seen, plays we have never heard of. Currently Vautrin by Balzac, entombed since a one-night stand in 1840. Extreme sophistication of design (Philip Prowse offers a glorious Parisian mansion, visibly supported from without by a set of grubby planks, and changing at the billow of a few drapes into a Turkish-delight thieves' kitchen) combines oddly with school-play acting; very good school-play acting in the case of Mark Lewis, whose Vautrin is a brooding outcast master-criminal whose master-stroke is the adoption of his protege and adoptive son into one aristocratic family, and his marriage into a second.

The mixture suits the play; a melodrama that sweeps along with increasing velocity, so that even those who cannot rise to the occasion are swept up in it. Robert David Macdonald, director and translator, pulls all kinds of operatic tricks and, unbelievably, they work.

This rich food, however, is hardly more nourishing than the plain crust of Paul Copley's Pillion (Bush), set in what used to be, but as one character bitterly points out is no longer, the West Riding. This sets off, of course, an attack on bureaucracy wherever it works because the play's values are solidly in front of us.

A motor-bike mechanic's shed (realised in magnificent detail by Grant Hicks whose living-room in Walter at the Traverse was until now the premier realistic design of the year) becomes a workable equivalent of the wheelwright's shop Dr Leavis used to go on about. With its virtues, though, go a certain blinkered obstinacy, fully realised in Edward Peel's performance, the best, in Chris Parr's production, of an admirable bunch.
