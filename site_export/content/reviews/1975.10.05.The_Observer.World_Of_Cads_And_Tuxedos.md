World of cads
and tuxedos

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on
a revival of Noel
Coward's 'The Vortex.'

WANDERERS in an Art Deco wilderness, Florence Lancaster and her son Nicky appear on the stage of the Greenwich Theatre like late arrivals to last year's series of 'Family Romances.' You may remember that that season, under the direction of Jonathan Miller, consisted of 'Hamlet,' 'The Seagull' and 'Ghosts'; and at one time it was rumoured that Noel Coward's The Vortex would be of their company.

It is as well perhaps that the rumour proved false, and that we have had to wait till now to see Coward's play at Greenwich, under the direction of James Roose-Evans. Mr Miller, once he had started work on it, would surely have got irritated by its thinness, and this irritation would have communicated itself to us. Compared with Chekhov's and Ibsen's Hamlet-variations, never mind with the thing itself, Coward's play lacks body. It suffers, too, from a facile ending, curing mother and cadging son happy in each other's arms at the final curtain. Chekhov and Shakespeare placed their equivalent clinches in the middle of the action; by the end of the show such easy reconciliations have been left far behind. In Ibsen the embrace is saved for the finale, but it is a ghastly parody of a happy ending.

'The Vortex,' however, closes with Florence, who has had lovers, and Nicky, who has taken drugs, both promising never to do it again. We can believe them or not as we choose, I found it difficult to care either way. In this respect the play is partly the victim of history; or at least of that small tributary of history called fashion. Its true element is the twenties, the cocktail and tuxedo twenties: the Noel Coward twenties in short, by our lights the cutest period that ever was. Mr Roose-Evans has tried to woo us into the play by initially emphasising twenties charm (so that cloche-hatted Jennifer Hilary, as Florence's confidante, never sits on a chair when she can perch on its arm or drape herself against its back), and subsequently fading it out so that the last scene leaves us with unadorned timeless emotion.

Here he echoes his author, who fills out his first two acts with the bitchy humours of his heroine's entourage before turning relentlessly to business in the last. The result, however, in both writing and production is that manner, or just mannerism, outbalances matter. Having become attuned to a Coward comedy, though not one of his best, we are dashed to find mother and son upbraiding each other in a modern closet scene. (When they start rolling on the bed it's really too much.)

The turning-point has been a moment of sheer melodrama, well-made melodrama, but no more convincing for that. Florence discovers her current gigolo kissing Nicky's fiancee. 'What does this mean?' she asks rhetorically. 'You are an utter cad,' she states, even more rhetorically. The following year Coward himself parodied this kind of thing in 'Hay Fever' where the inveterate performer Judith Bliss ('Is this a game?' 'Yes, and a game that must be played to the finish') makes every blameless intrusion the occasion for a scene. Judith is a more truthful as well as a funnier version of Florence. Each one of them is a Bright Middle-aged Thing, a sad phenomenon, as 'The Vortex' keeps insisting, but not overwhelmingly tragic. Where Judith is an actress, Florence merely moves in a theatrical ambience. We are already within sight of Coward's typical snobbery towards non-thespians, treating them as bizarre objects to be sniffed at, patronised and touched with tongs.

The milieu is too shallow to be worth storming at or preaching over, but Coward does both in 'The Vortex.' He is fair enough to make his mouthpiece Nicky more than slightly suspect, though little of this emerges in the performance of Timothy Dalton, who seems undernourished but thoroughly healthy. Vivien Merchant, all of a flutter as Florence, speaking low, slow and breathy like an enervated Fenella Fielding, pays the same price as the play and production, livening the early scenes at the expense of the conclusion. The freshest acting comes from Gabrielle Drake, the fiancee who acts as foil to Nicky's grand filial passion.

* * *

Toby Robins, a leading Canadian actress over two decades, looks startlingly youthful as the recidivist heroine of The Ecstasy of Rita Joe. She is a better advertisement for Canadian theatre than George Ryga's play (at the fallible Hampstead Theatre Club), which treats of the dispossessed Indian in a manner stolid, documentary at best and leadenly fanciful otherwise. (Red man speak with ornamental tongue; often he trip over it.) It must have seemed more urgent in its own time (1967) as well as its own place. Technically it plods in a manner we have outgrown on both sides of the Atlantic; its avoidance of character confrontation strains both the patience and the nerves. Its original production probably owed much to the presence of genuine Indians. (One staging featured Chief Dan George, the indestructible ancient of 'Little Big Man.') At Hampstead it falls an easy victim to a miserably unauthentic production, which closes with blanketed white actors stomping hard, perhaps to ward off the evil spirits of embarrassment. They materialised nonetheless.
