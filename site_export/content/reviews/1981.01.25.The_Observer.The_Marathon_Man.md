The Marathon Man

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Shaw at the National.

Shaw used to patronise Shakespeare by declaring that his sound counted for far more than his sense: 'Tested by the brain it is ridiculous; tested by the ear it is sublime.' Those words, and their music, rebound heavily upon him in the National Theatre's production of Man and Superman (Olivier) in its five-hour entirety.

Two of those five hours are occupied by the play's third act; the one, generally cut, in which Jack Tanner, fleeing from London to Granada and points beyond in order to escape the marital clutches of Ann Whitefield, is held up by bandits in the Sierra Nevada and dreams of himself as a Don Juan governed by intellectual passions consigned to a sybaritic Hell ruled over by a Devil who greatly resembles the brigand chief, not least in being a good talker.

He is allotted one of Shaw's longest, wittiest and most persuasive speeches, on man's essential destructiveness. It goes right against the official Shavian ethic (the play was written in his most buoyant period) and is immediately countered by Juan, who proclaims that humanity is obsessed not by death but by ideals, and that what all these amount to is the drive to create ever more intelligent forms of life. He goes on arguing this for the rest of the scene, and gets only token rebuttals; for once Shaw seems disinclined to risk giving the Devil the best tunes.

The cliche is apposite; since what holds us in this scene--and it works, in Christopher Morahan's production, astonishingly well--are the melodies and rhythms of the prose. (There are also bursts of real music from, naturally, 'Don Giovanni': putting this play on alongside Amadeus suggests that a spirit of economy is beginning to haunt the South Bank.)

As Juan, Daniel Massey's natural musicality stands him in magnificent stead; his speaking, a sensuous pleasure in itself, is also a vehicle of character. He only loses the thread towards the end, when he gives up all pretence of talking to his companions and concentrates uninterruptedly on the audience. Michael Bryant, a Satanic maitre d'hotel, is his deceptive opponent, and the other participants--Penelope Wilton's Dona Ana, respectable even when damned, and Basil Henson as her father, still a man of stone on the outside, but all sentiment within--are likewise impeccable.

Ana says little, but she has the last word. The Devil, after Juan's departure, happens to mention the concept of the Superman leaving Ana, suddenly revealed as eternal woman, determined to give birth to it, which means finding the right father. Juan, who thought he was done with sex, is, to judge from the determined wonder in Miss Wilton's voice, doomed all over again; and by his own philosophy. At which point Tanner is here merely picked up and put down.

Thus we return to the social-sexual comedy generally known as 'Man and Superman' to find it--as Juan found Hell after a taste of heavenly contemplation--less amusing than it might be. Tanner's chat about the Life Force, Ann's compulsive wiles, may make more intellectual sense, but their accustomed comic energy is diminished. Maybe this is a matter of aesthetic disproportion; a sex comedy lasting five hours is excessive, even with overtones. But the production too is at fault; Mr Massey's doomed white-suited gyrations as the trap closes are exquisitely done but he is too helpless a victim from the start. Both he and Mr Henson (unstoned as Ramsden) could do with more Edwardian bluster, as Ralph Koltai's first-act set in battleship grey (limbering up for some glowing abstract transformations as the scene moves outwards) could do, at this stage, with more Edwardian solidity.

Miss Wilton, conversely, gives little sign of the girlish vulnerability that takes in most of the men. The production is, however, exceptionally clear; and the mother-wit of play and players guarantees considerable pleasure. Which reminds me that maybe the most creative performance is given by Antonia Pemberton as Ann's mother, scoffing sweets in the delight of hearing someone insult her child. The subsidiary romance--English ironclad and American innocent--is brightly handled by Anna Carteret and Greg Hicks.

* * *

Stanley Price's Moving (Queens) is a fairly pleasant comedy about the traumas of selling a house, flawed by pretentiousness, itself possibly the result of flagging invention. Maybe if Mr Price had been able to construct a farcically logical chain of events from the inability of his protagonists to decide between rival offers, despite the necessity to reach a quick completion over the house they want, he would not have felt obliged to pad out his second half with reflections on the property market as a reflection of middle-class values: a subject that should be pushed harder if touched at all, but is here merely picked up and put down.

As often happens in West End comedy, the funny lines have no connection to the situation; they are merely whimsical metaphors for whatever happens to have crossed the speaker's mind. Mr Price goes very easy on his characters; of course in real life most people do manage to dispose of their homes without having nervous breakdowns, but there are occasions when real life should be kept away from the theatre.

The standard pair in the middle are Peter Jeffrey, who seems very real, and Penelope Keith, who adds to her familiar skills a surprising capacity for tears. Roger Lloyd Pack is an enlivening presence as a decorator, with a sympathetic whine in his voice and furrows in his cheeks. Paul Scofield must have been like this when he played his dead common role in 'Expresso Bongo.'

* * *

Characters and situations closely related to the above but younger, appear in Jonathan Gems's Naked Robots (Warehouse). In both a young actress appears with an I've-just-had-an-abortion look on her face (defiant in the West End, woebegone on the fringe, which has always been the place for sentiment); Mr Gems's teen and twenties fashion freaks would like a decent place to live instead of one room piled high with mattresses, but it would cost them £40,000.

Mr Gems has written an unarguably terrible play which stubbornly, despite great provocation, refused to bore me. His characters are: a costume designer, a singer, a dope-pusher, a lost girl, and a rock manager; all sliding or slid, all with deeply conventional aspirations which the author gently satirises, but with which he seems to have no basic quarrel. Accordingly he might have given them a firm conventional structure to mirror and support them.

His current stop-go methods are underlined in John Caird's production, which gets no rhythm going and leaves five uneasy actors delivering possibly amusing lines into stony silence. Mr Gems has an excellent ear; you never groan at his dialogue as you do at some currently successful plays. The hero's profession has inspired Ultz to design spectacular costumes, as for a sci-fi panto cast with Michelin men.

* * *

A group called Cheek by Jowl announce a radical new version of 'Tis Pity She's a Whore (Theatre Space, Covent Garden). Well, they've cut a sub-plot, and they play in modern dress; but that's not new. What is, is the quiet conviction with which they invest the sober verse and lurid action, though limitations of space, skill and temperament promote a nagging or plain sulky delivery of potentially incendiary material. They should refrain from fully audible soliloquial ad-libs, which are not only an abomination which may not matter, but silly, which does.
