Masterly Marivaux

THEATRE
by ROBERT CUSHMAN

MARIVAUX, who wrote comedies in Paris in the first half of the eighteenth century, has a fair claim to be the classic playwright most unfairly neglected by the British theatre. Very few of his plays have ever been published in English; though I remember stumbling some 15 years ago on a clumsy translation of his 'Fausses confidences' and deciding, in its despite, that this was a masterpiece.

I have waited ever since for official confirmation, now finally supplied by Shared Experience at the Lyric Studio, Hammersmith. The play has been translated by Timberlake Wertenbaker under the title False Admissions; and is performed in repertoire with another Marivaux, here rendered as Successful Strategies. They come, in Mike Alfreds's productions, as a pair.

Paul Dart -- responsible for sets, costumes and lighting -- has supplied each with a unified colour-scheme. Successful Strategies is decked out in a dazzling array of whites and creams; False Admissions rings more changes on the colour black than might have been thought possible.

Both are plays of amatory intrigue, with several characters in common. Each cast, for example, includes an Arlequin, wearing the traditional suit of lozenges, bleached up in one play, mired down in the other. He comes, in False Admissions, disconcertingly close to the Watteau idea of a broken-hearted zany. I suspect that Marivaux meant Arlequin's comedy to be rather broader (he is a pushy, discontented domestic) than Mr Alfreds has allowed for. But these possible distortions are forgivable in productions that give each play so strong an identity.

'Amatory intrigue' may be a phrase that slips too easily off the typewriter. These are plays about lovers lying, without ever -- which is where Marivaux gets his peculiar tang -- ceasing to be in love. In False Admissions, Dorante, who is poor, gets a job as steward with his adored Araminte. On his behalf Dubois, his former servant, wages psychological warfare; he tells Araminte that Dorante is crazy about her, and waits to see propinquity, vanity and his master's personal charms do their inevitable work.

All good fun: the pain comes in with Marton, Araminte's paid companion, who is led to believe Dorante loves her and who gradually finds out her mistake. Maggie Wells does some memorable silent suffering in this role, while Holly Wilson is decorous and alluring as Araminte.

A scene between them reminded me of the two women quarrelling over the young tutor in A Month in the Country, or at least of how that was done in Peter Gill's National Theatre production, which placed Turgenev firmly if unexpectedly in the Marivaux tradition. Instead of a matchmaking doctor, we have here a matchmaking solicitor: a good enough fellow in Philip Voss's performance, but a thoroughly practical one who cannot see what business people have marrying for anything other than money. Araminte's mother would agree with him, except that she thinks in terms of rank rather than cash.

Apart from the chessboard manoeuvrings, the fascination lies in the degree to which emotional and monetary desires can coincide. The clarity and the subdued intensity of the acting matches that of the decor, though there are times when the banked-up feelings threaten to outgrow the studio.

If False Admissions sometimes becomes oppressive, Successful Strategies makes amends. Here everything sparkles, not least Miss Wertenbaker's text. This time we have a Comtesse who loves another Dorante, but jilts him in favour of a conceited Chevalier. Dorante makes common cause with the Chevalier's old flame; and their subterfuge carries them almost to the marriage-altar.

The same comedy is played out among their servants; Mr Voss's complacency as the Chevalier is matched by James Smith, superbly supercilious as his valet; while Sam Dale makes an aggrieved Arlequin, fearing forcible separation from his fiancee. Miss Wilson is again the cynosure; serene before, she is here manipulative and flirtatious without ever becoming dislikeable. Her caprice -- her greed, if you like -- and the jealousy she both suffers and inspires are, after all, thoroughly human.

These complex comedies make one wonder whether we have not been misdirecting our energies; we have persevered with Moliere, to be rewarded by powerful sermons on the inadvisability of being a miser, a hypochondriac or a possessive husband: sermons that have left us thinking yes indeed and what else is new.

The depth and ambiguity in Marivaux is far closer to our own tradition, even if the style takes some decoding. This task has been masterfully performed by Mr Alfreds, to whom a final bouquet: for his consistent adventurousness and for the relish with which he has shared his experiences with his excellent actors and they theirs with us.

* * *

Bob Fosse's Dancin' (Drury Lane) begins with a proclamation about how musicals have been destroyed by their sappy and portentous books. I have some sympathy with that position; I certainly think it a welcome confession from the creator of Pippin and Chicago. This show promises amends; no speech, just movement and music. Of course it breaks its own rule even by announcing it; and it goes on doing so.

It is a choreographic revue that feels compelled to explain everything. Mr Fosse is not for the most part at his best (the best of Sweet Charity or How to Succeed) and his American company is less than top-notch; all right in their dancin', but not in their singin' or their talkin'. Just one number burns up the stage: a homage to swing in which the performers are shot across the stage in groups of three (Mr Fosse's favourite formation) before combining for a marathon jitterbug.

The music is the Benny Goodman war-horse Sing, Sing, Sing, and it helps a lot. The insinuating I Want to Be a Dancing Man (by Warren and Mercer) similarly supports a mass tribute to Fred Astaire; you might think that the Fosse tension was at odds with the Astaire cool, but there is an area of nonchalance, the hat tipped rakishly over the brow, at which the extremes meet.

* * *

Michael Abbensetts's Outlaw (Arts) takes us nostalgically back to Rachmanism and the beginnings of Black Power. Another thing it takes is a long time; I was reminded of a favourite Goon Show in which, every two minutes, Peter Sellers would intone, 'this is where the story really starts.' That it never did was fun then, but not now.

Robert Gillespie's production does nothing to sharpen matters, either in staging or editing, but there are lively performances from Raul Newney as a would-be wide-boy turned politician and Joy Richardson in a witty double of his girl and his mother. Jacqueline Pearce, who once did the nude scene in Otherwise Engaged, has come on a lot since.
