Polish apocalypse

Theatre

Robert Cushman in Paris.

I do not know whether Michel Guy, who runs the Paris Autumn Festival, regards himself as a prophet of environmental theatre. It is a fact, though, that the four Festival productions I saw last week derived part of their impact from their relationship to the buildings in which they were housed.

I applaud this approach less for its honesty (a questionable theatrical commodity, even when it is to be had) than for its expediency. I extend this commendation even to Jerzy Grotowski, the hallowed Polish experimentalist, the altitude of whose principles is hardly to be measured, let alone questioned.

When Grotowski's laboratory troupe visited London a few years back those of us permitted to see them were transported in secret coachloads to a City church. In Paris we again find Grotowski in church: in Sainte-Chapelle, celebrated for its stained glass and its tourists. Grotowski has exploited the former and banished the latter.

Seeking to establish what Peter Brook has called a holy theatre, Grotowski very properly latches on to sites with reverential potential. On the same principle, the audience at a musical is conditioned to gaiety by the overture. At Sainte-Chapelle, after a brief sojourn in an antechamber to read our programmes (afterwards removed, lest we succumb to the temptation of perusing them rather than watching the play), we were escorted in small groups up winding stairs, and finally ushered two by two into the chapel itself. There, on a raised wooden platform lit from ground level by a single powerful lamp, crouched the actors. It was an awesome sight, which would have been totally ruined had we been allowed to crowd in en masse.

The play, *Apocalypsis Cum Figuris*, was apparently worked up in improvisations stemming initially from the Book of Revelation, and shaped to lead up to a confrontation between the Christlike figure called the Innocent (played by the remarkable Ryszard Cieslak, Grotowski's superstar, acknowledged as such on the posters) and a Simon Peter who speaks in the voice of Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor. Not understanding the words, one's response must inevitably be an act of trust; feeling the presence of an undeviating line in the production I trusted Grotowski, as I failed earlier this year to trust his compatriot, Tadeusz Kantor, whose *Lovelies and Dowdies* was the buttered toast of the Edinburgh Festival fringe. The two are, as I fear I am not the first to observe, Poles apart: Kantor bullied his audience through an initial cloakroom ceremony, based on the assumption that every time we say goodbye to our coats we die a little. Those of us who are only too glad to shed them for the duration were inevitably suspicious, and the suspicion deepened when the performance proved to be a collection of unrelated and not particularly striking images. I believe that the theatre works through concentration, not dispersal, and it is for his adherence to this principle, as well as his engagingly practical attitude towards his venue and his public, that I salute Grotowski. His work may, as has been alleged, be humourless, but this is not a crippling disadvantage to a man who is not trying to make jokes.

It is on the other hand a grievous handicap to TSE, the Argentinian group now based in Paris, who attempt at the Palace, which certainly has the right ambiance, to burlesque the French music hall in a show called *Luxe*. It sounds, as indeed it is, a pointless exercise; effective parody of a form so effervescent would after all be indistinguishable from the real thing. *Luxe* can be distinguished from its models by the slowness of its performance, the uninventiveness of its routines, and the blankness of its stare; this finding aural equivalent in the dispiriting use of pre-recorded music throughout. I suppose the intention may have been to expose the spurious glamour of the halls, but I hesitate, without further evidence, to accuse TSE of anything so detestably high-minded.

Downstairs at the Palace, a long, mirrored rehearsal-room has been utilised for performances of *Les Quatre Jumelles*, a cheerful fable about four ladies who, for the sake of a whole heap of greenbacks, maim, blind and kill one another repeatedly.

Here, too, the author (Copi) is Argentinian; so is the director, Jorge Lavelli, who has staged the piece, all over the room, with flair and four good uninhibited actresses, in the grimly mischievous style which might be christened Arraballetic.

Grotowski apart, the jewel of the festival has been a reworking of an obscure Marivaux play, *La Dispute*, by the Theatre National Populaire: once, under Jean Vilar, the glory of French theatre and now reconstituted at Lyons under Roger Planchon, maestro of that parish. In the glittering confines of the Theatre de la Musique, Patrice Chereau, a young colleague of M. Planchon, has mounted the play as a mock opera. A burst of romantic music starts us off; smoke belches from the infernal depths of the orchestra pit. ("Hell," said Shaw's Don Juan, "is full of amateur musicians.") The main business of the play, a formal trial of the inconstancy of the sexes, which is also an expose of the myth of the noble savage, is despatched in chiaroscuro light against sumptuous pastoral decor. From his actors, many of them very young, M. Chereau has conjured performances of shattering force, not least in the Prologue, played in evening dress on the rim of the orchestra, in which a sardonic Prince broaches the play's argument to his self-pitying betrothed. Finally, he lures her, by way of a perilous plank flung across the abyss, to the stage where the action unfolds in illustration of his argument.

With productions as electrifying as this, the TNP could regain its former prestige, which has currently passed to the Theatre Municipale Populaire, established by Jean Mercure in the gutted Theatre Sarah Bernhardt, reconstituted and renamed the Theatre de la Ville. Straying from the festival, I visited Monsieur Mercure's production of Peter Nichols's *The National Health*, transported from an English to a French hospital and rather dashingly subtitled *Les Amours Noires d'un Homme en Blanc*. The ward (give a bottle of Lucozade and take one of Evian) is almost identical to the one we saw at the Old Vic; and Mr Nichols's humanity, which I have thought unalterably English, has crossed the Channel undamaged. Certainly a packed house thought so, and the Theatre de la Ville, where a single-tiered auditorium sweeps down a steep rake to confront an open stage, proved an ideal meeting place for audience and play. Puzzlingly the interpolated segments of soap opera (parodies of *Doctor Finlay*, et al.) were left in their English guise; this added to the confusion which overtakes the play in its second half, and which erupts helplessly, and topically, in the royal wedding finale.
