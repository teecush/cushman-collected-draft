HDG
T/C

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

OUT OF the Barbican and into the Adelphi comes Poppy, in which Peter Nichols tells the story of the Chinese opium wars and of the very bad show put up in them by Queen Victoria's English chaps.

They were saying last year that Mr Nichols had written a pantomime, and that Terry Hands had directed a musical. I pass on that, chiefly because the two genres are hopelessly entangled. Doubtless things were different in Victoria's day, or even in Mr Nichols's childhood, but today, from the Palladium to your local Empire if any (and if any, then what a suitable site for this saga), Christmas entertainments have the same production values as a revival at the Apollo Victoria.

The gambolling maidens and grumbling swains who open 'Poppy' might have stepped out of a George Edwardes chorus at the old Gaiety, but so might the merry villagers of your average 'Dick Whittington.'

If I must, I will say, in the teeth of my colleagues, that this West End re-staging has moved away from pantomime and towards musical. The Fairy Queen and Demon King, alias Victoria and the Chinese Emperor, are now denied their traditional entrances at opposite sides of the stage; it is an earnest of the show's sympathies that he comes down on a heavenly cloud while she rises up from an infernal trap.

New choreography, by the American Onna White, smells of Broadway; also, in a naval battle fought under the command of our long-legged principal boy prancing about like Eleanor Powell, of Hollywood. Farrah has designed new sets, simpler than the old and more beautiful. The salient features are a dauntingly tilted floor, on which the lighting throws opalescent patterns, and a series of coloured projections.

Mr Nichols's chosen convention—Dame, Squire, Idle Jack, pantomime horse—remains perfectly clear; and so does his point of view. I doubt that any in the audience will fail to notice his disapproval of imperialism, capitalism, class distinction, and organised religion. Whether they will think this quite enough is another matter.

I can imagine the opium wars being dramatised in strict epic style, dispensing the maximum of information, or through a close focus on the people involved. I know little [about them], but I am positive that they were not called Lady Dodo, Jack Idle and Obadiah Upward, and I grew weary of the increasingly desperate [attempts] to pretend that they were.

The contention that pantomime is itself a jingoistic form, deserving to be up-ended, may have been true in the nineteenth century, but not now. Nor does Mr Nichols play very fair; he gets his Chinese characters giggling when they see women dressed as men and men as women; since they themselves are supposed to be part of the panto, why should they question its methods? And anyway what right have the Chinese—whose classical theatre is founded on transvestitism—to balk at a pantomime dame?

Monty Norman's music is catchy, rather like the common cold; both have simple and familiar germs. I liked best 'They All Look the Same To Us,' the Rodgers and Hammerstein parody, all shuffling feet and tinkling bells, with which the inscrutables greet the unspeakables. Line by line, Mr Nichols's lyrics are admirably turned, but the weight of information they have to carry frequently sinks them as songs. The format allows him to make some excellent jokes, but as the proclaimed swan-song of one of our best playwrights this is thin stuff.

Similarly, Mr Hands's direction, though smart, hardly caps his imposing triptych of 'Much Ado,' 'Arden' and 'Cyrano.' Geoffrey Hutchings repeats his forceful Dame, but the other leads (Alfred Marks, Antonia Ellis, David Firth, Janet Shaw) are muffled. The best newcomers are Ken Wynne as the Emperor and Nichola McAuliffe who, with a wicked grin and a noble voice, doubles as queen and missionary and is able to put over not only a number, but the thought behind it.

Athol Fugard's Master Harold and the Boys (Cottesloe) is about a white male, growing up in South Africa in 1950, and crumbling under family and social pressures. His mother runs a Port Elizabeth tea-room, his father is an invalid, his only friends are two black waiters. He ends up humiliating the more authoritative of them, Sam, whom he has previously treated as a substitute parent.

The play is an acknowledged piece of autobiography, and its remembered pain is indisputable, as is the author's desire and ability to see right round the situation. The action can easily be taken as a metaphor for the shocks that await anyone living in a prejudiced (not just a segregated) society. But its dialogue is too laborious, its images too calculated. The waiters are ballroom dancers, and dance is extensively discussed as a metaphor for life; its grace and its collisions.

You know that the lights will go down on a foxtrotting couple; the moment, when it arrives, is nice but no more. There is a sort of visual poetry in the readying of the shop for the next day's custom, but it relates to nothing more than itself. (You want trends? I'll give you trends. With this and 'Glengarry Glen Ross' the Cottesloe is mounting a season of restaurant plays.)

The play was given a better, or at least a better-cast, production (directed, like this one, by the author) in New York last year. There I saw the towering James Earl Jones play Sam. Here he is John Kani, the brilliant improviser of 'Sizwe Bansi is Dead,' but a flyweight actor not easily acceptable as anybody's father-figure. So the piece lacks a dimension.

Theatro Technis, in Mornington Crescent, is doing Aristophanes's The Frogs as a musical, which is much how Aristophanes himself must have done it. The score (John Gould music, David Dearlove lyrics) has a collegiate charm and joyfully points up the irrelevance of the famous froggy chorus ('brek-ek-ek-ek-ex, koax koax,' that one) to what the play is supposed to be about.

Aristophanes was never a great one for keeping his eye on the object. The god Dionysus, a drastic but conscientious theatre critic, descends to Hell to bring back a playwright; he has to choose between Aeschylus, whom Aristophanes worshipped, and Euripides, whom he didn't.

Ted Craig's production puts up a lively case for the play if not a conclusive one. Guy Siner's Dionysus is an amusingly discomfited cad, like a thin Simon Callow; Keith Varnier doubles a donnish Aeschylus with one of Hades' heavies; and David Shaughnessy as the comic slave combines a winning surliness with a singing voice that lifts him clear above 'The Frogs' and right up to 'The Birds.'
