England's a prison?

Theatre

by Robert Cushman

The late great songwriter Johnny Mercer once hit on the unfortunate idea that love was like a game of poker, and pursued the image through 32 whole bars. The dramatist's equivalent of the "Love is like" routine is the "England is like" game. Howard Barker's The Hang of the Gaol (Warehouse) is the latest round.

I spent a large part of the evening trying to pretend that this wasn't the case, and that Mr Barker was interested in the burnt-out moorland prison he calls Middenhurst for its own sweet sake. The jig was up, though, when a character referred to England and his ability to get the hang of the place, thus referring us back to the title and telling us what it meant.

More specific guidance is provided by the only prisoner to appear in the play who says in effect that the British Empire will endure for as long as there are prisons in the land. Since there are jails in every country in the world, and since even in this country they predate the existence of empire by some centuries, this seems to me an extremely shaky argument.

I will grant you that imperialist habits of thought still permeate the way we run our institutions, and that it is in prisons that we are likely to find those habits at their nastiest. That, though, seems a disappointingly small point. A spectacular set-designer might help to give the image the magnitude it seeks, but that is obviously off the cards at the Warehouse.

The play centres on the investigations that follow the fire. On hand to be questioned are the governor and his wife, a pair of warders (one mild, one bitter) and that solitary trusty, keeping the governor's dogs and feigning madness. From their combined testimony we gather that Middenhurst has been on the verge of disruption, owing to the governor's controversial methods and the contempt this has aroused in all hands. The cons, if I got it right, felt patronised, and the screws of course felt betrayed.

The inquiry scenes themselves swing along nicely in good judicial fashion, reaching a climax when the youngest member of the tribunal of three finds himself taking a brutal pleasure in interrogation and, conscience-stricken, begins comparing himself to Himmler. His chief, in the best line in the play, tells him that it does all men good to confront the insect in themselves so long as they do not, like Himmler, grow to love it. But the consolation proves ineffective, and junior rushes off to live like a wild man, thus dangerously upping the number of moorland madmen at large in the play, and demonstrating how a spark of dramatic life may be snuffed out by inflation.

Meanwhile his mentor maintains a pose of alcoholic crustiness (unpredictable but incorruptible like a committed Dr Cameron) and lusts in a forlorn way after his remaining colleague (a woman). There are also two self-satisfied arson experts - a comic duo responsible for the play's few laughs - and, controlling all, a Labour Home Secretary who naturally occupies the lowest place of all in the author's sympathies. The governor and his wife may be ridiculous, and their privilege questionable, but Mr Barker allows them integrity and even style; their just-left-of-centre opponent is merely grasping. You will, if you go to the theatre much, have met him before.

There are on offer some pithy thoughts on the immorality of punishment, and the play's imagery is certainly unified. Scatology is the link; begin with the name Middenhurst, and go on from there. But a play like this, where neither character nor dialogue are believable or pretend to be, desperately needs a strong fable; and this Mr Barker has not supplied. He brings his actors on in varying combinations, but the configurations seem arbitrary. There is, to put it at its most basic, nobody whom one is particularly glad to see. I don't mean they aren't credible or likeable; I mean they have no energy.

This is true even of the senior inquirer, invested by Fulton Mackay with his usual measure of frazzled Caledonian insistence. Mr Mackay is always a compelling actor but I liked him best in that other prison drama, Porridge, where the demands of TV comedy caused him to hurry on a bit. Left to himself he is apt to add 10 minutes to any play's running time. Nicholas Le Prevost elaborates on his particular speciality, the smart but nervous grammar-school boy fallen among bullies; while Nigel Terry, darkly mumbling, has another go at his. Gaye Brown, on the other hand, is cast rewardingly against type; the lady she plays could never be described as a good-time girl.

* * *

It would obviously be unfair to judge Ken Campbell's projected 10-part sci-fi epic The Warp (ICA) by the first night of its first instalment. At least I hope it would be unfair, since what I saw was mostly terrible. The acting, even by Mr Campbell's informal standards, was sloppy; I was relieved when a first-night cry of "Speak up for God's sake" turned out to have come from the maestro himself. (It worked too. Instant direction.)

Neil Oram's script, as so far revealed, plays like a pale copy of Illuminatus: conspiracy theories are adumbrated and the questing hero, a would-be poet, is passed on from one baffling guru to another; one grew to dread the announcement that someone had an interesting friend they wanted him to meet. There are also beddable girls for him to enjoy and policemen to stop him doing it (the number of arrests in this play must constitute an all-time record).

Only Jim Broadbent among the actors seemed to me to match Mr Campbell's exalted standards of frenzy. But the staging offers some promise of fun to come; the play is performed on a series of stages placed end-to-end around the walls of the hall. It chases you clockwise round the room; in fact I must confess to wondering what happens next. (Will I succumb? Watch this space.)

* * *

The heroine of Shaw's very late play The Millionairess (Haymarket) is required to be simultaneously bossy and enchanting. Penelope Keith can be bossy and hilarious (as in The Norman Conquests) or vulnerable and enchanting (as in Donkey's Years); the new combination is beyond her, though she manages both elements by turns and looks a treat; her giraffe height functions in the production almost as mobile sculpture.

Her colleagues mostly vary from grotesque to dull (Nigel Hawthorne performing needless prodigies of self-effacement), though Charles Kay has a pretty style as her chosen mate, the Egyptian doctor whom he plays, perhaps inevitably, as Peter Sellers' Indian. (Donald Pleasence once did so on television. And some movie producer saw him, and cast Mr Sellers in a film version, opposite Sophia Loren. Goodness gracious both of them.)

The play is a problem since its dialogue has sparkle far in excess of its weight. The piece races along, and the audience are left wondering what it is all for. At its heart, though, is a very powerful conceit; the heroine Epifania is sexy because rich: a lineal descendant of Portia in The Merchant of Venice and encumbered by much the same kind of paternal will. She is cold and challenging; so is the play.
