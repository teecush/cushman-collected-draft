Lessons of a crisp factory

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

JOSEF FRANK, as I learned last week, was one of those hanged in Prague in 1952 after the Slansky show-trials. In his new play, Weapons of Happiness (Lyttelton) Howard Brenton imagines Frank, surviving, somehow, coming to England and working, now in a potato-crisp factory, in South London. It will be seen that, historically speaking, Mr Brenton's Frank is a ghost; dramatically, too. "I don't sleep," he tells us at curtain-rise. "I walk about London."

The crisp business is in turmoil. The factory is small, family owned and running down. Its work-force seems to consist entirely of kids and old men. Prominent among the kids is Janice, whose political education began early with a book called "The Evil That Was Lenin." ("Think it was meant to put me off. 'Stead it put me on.") Discovering that the management has plans to sell out she organises a workers' occupation. She does not organise it very well; and her forces have to decamp through the sewers. Anyway her own politicisation, though fervent, has never seemed very secure ("Couldn't understand a word. But I believed in it") and she has not managed to communicate much in the way of ideology to her followers, who are driven by gut-discontent, gut-hatred, and--at the decisive moment--alcohol. Besides this they have neither the finance nor the contacts to take over the machines and make crisps themselves.

It is Frank who points this out to them; but it needs no ghost to come from the grave to tell us that this attempt at revolt was foredoomed. Mr Brenton's full-length plays are characteristically organised around two disparate blocks of experience; in Magnificence his worlds collide with immense force, in The Churchill Play they remain obstinately apart. In Weapons of Happiness they are brought together but there is no impact. Frank, both in the writing and in Frank Finlay's performance, is carefully opaque. We are shown his past as well as his present; there are flashbacks to his days as a Czech Minister negotiating with Mikoyan and socialising with Stalin; monologues about his torture and interrogation. These of course are harrowing, but ever since Darkness at Noon, never mind The Gulag Archipelago, they have been within the grasp of any competent writer who cared to use them. And Mr Brenton does not use them to any new effect; he leaves us feeling (a) that in the light of experiences like these, English ideas of revolution look childish and (b) that the idea of socialist revolution has anyway been irredeemably tarnished. These conclusions might be reached by any liberal, and I doubt if they are what Mr Brenton had in mind.

When Janice, in the last line of the play, recalls Frank, she merely says of him, "He was a Communist." And that line, coming as and when it does, sounds like a declaration both of love and honour; but it can hardly counter-balance what has gone before. There is more swimming about in the play than has been resolved or made clear. Half of the evening works, though, as a furious lampoon on a decaying corner of capitalism. It is like Mr Brenton to choose a crisp-factory for his area of conflict, and to make the owner the victim of his own ingenuity ("It was the pork chop flavour that finally did for me") in the fight against "the monoliths of the crunch world." Michael Medwin has the best of Mr Brenton's dialogue (brisk, grotesque and concrete as ever) and does well by it; he is pitiable and amusing. Julie Covington, too, is excellent as Janice; compact and composed, frank, exact and enigmatic, she proves herself here (as she promised a couple of years ago in The Pleasure Principle) the most distinctive contemporary young actress we have.

There are some excess characters: a deaf-and-dumb boy who is not used for cheap effect but is not used for much else either, and Mr Medwin's unsatisfied spouse. (If industrialists could make love to their wives, would that solve everything?) But Mr Brenton still takes as much delight in stage images, and his director David Hare takes as much care over them, as in their Portable Theatre days. Operating now at the other extreme, using all of the National Theatre that works, they convey the same cool pleasure in stretching and challenging the available resources. Not every play can offer both a scene in the London Planetarium and the spectacle of cartons of crisps hurled on to the stage to vomit forth their contents.

* * *

A redoubtable company of underactors, guided by a television director, fall predictably foul of Webster's The White Devil (Old Vic). The setting would be nondescript were it not for a brace of swing-doors downstage which inescapably suggest the lobby of an hotel with delusions of grandeur; the costumes have the same seediness, the same unspecified modernity. Everything and everybody is undercut, which is part of the effect Webster presumably aimed for, but by no means all of it. He was obviously fascinated by his luxurious assembly of homicides, and the moral judgments he delivered were considerably more complex and evasive than the puritanical disapproval, compromised by prurience, conveyed by this production almost before it has started. The amount of mumbling and gabbling indulged in is phenomenal.

Michael Lindsay-Hogg's production does actually manage some things unexpectedly well; one is a contest in melancholia between a couple of the play's numerous villains which dissolves when one of them receives good news and explodes with laughter; another is the portrayal of the boy-prince Giovanni. Glenda Jackson gives a small but accurate account of the title role, but she cannot hold the play together; neither can Jack Shepherd, who as Flamineo has the most chance. He establishes a properly confidential relationship with the audience but none with anybody else; he seems to comment on his own villainy without ever executing it. So the most varied and rewarding role in English drama fades into nothing. James Villiers does better as Bracciano, whom he just manages to keep believable as a sulky, possessive head prefect. Frances de la Tour is properly ambiguous as the seeming-virtuous Isabella, and Jonathan Pryce hovers to some effect as the cut-throat Lodovico. The end of the play is left confused; one character even escapes, most apocryphally, with her life.

* * *

The following night another TV director, Jack Gold, succeeded triumphantly in making another diabolical (if considerably more tractable) play command the stage. Indeed Tom Conti, leaning handsomely and languorously against the door on first entrance, looks more like The Devil's Disciple (Aldwych) than anybody reading the lines Shaw provided for his conventional romantic ne'er-do-well might imagine possible. Mr Conti and Mr Gold have been as assiduous with the play's wit as with its avowed melodrama; and the RSC have cast the play up to, indeed some way above, the hilt. The deployment of John Wood as General Burgoyne, whose every line is an infallible wisecrack, is definitely luxury casting and is of course pure delight; Mr Wood, like Mr Conti, announces his intention with his first movement, which is to lie full-length upon a table and gaze upward with lazy, insolent defiance. Even the emotions of the besotted Mrs Anderson get scrupulous attention from Estelle Kohler. T. P. McKenna has a roaring time as her transfigured mate, and I must mention Patrick Godfrey's perfect butt of a major, if only because I always mean to mention Mr Godfrey when dealing with this company and nearly always have to squeeze him out. I should remind you that the play is set in the American War of Independence and that this seems to be topical: military bands play patriotic airs on fifes.

* * *

At the Mermaid Hugh Leonard has adapted a Labiche farce under the title Some of My Best Friends Are Husbands, and may well have interfered with it. I suspect, though, that he is less the culprit than the director, James Grout, who permits his stars, Patrick Cargill and Moira Redmond, to give performances that have everything to do with actors' panic and nothing with that of the characters. Hugh Paddick nearly redeems the occasion as a masochistic, hypochondriac and possibly fetishistic cuckold; he fills the character to the brim but never over. The same tact and good sense is evidenced in smaller roles by Granville Saxton and Peter Bourke. It is also exercised by absolutely everybody in Donkey's Years (Globe), an unmitigatedly glorious farce by Michael Frayn to which I must return next week.
