Inside Pinter's Hothouse

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Manchester.

THE boss is having a drink in his office while talking to a subordinate. He loses his temper and empties the contents of his glass in the henchman's face. The response is unblinking: 'Shall I fill you up, sir?'

Harold Pinter's The Hothouse (Hampstead) was apparently his second full-length play, written between The Birthday Party and The Caretaker, but has only now been exposed to view. One sees why he has been so bashful about it: not that either its craft or its content are anything to be ashamed of, even now, but because it gives the Pinter game away. The combination of sudden impotent violence and unnervingly smooth obsequiousness has been repeated in most of his subsequent plays, but it has always been dressed up. The most effective disguise, paradoxically, has proved to be nakedness. Working on small canvasses, Mr Pinter, even when he has apparently filled in every detail, has still left us asking questions, even when (as in Betrayal) the question has been 'is that all there is?'

In The Hothouse there is, in terms of action and explanation, a great deal; there are seven characters -- Marlovian richness, by Mr Pinter's standards -- so the power games can be more extensively orchestrated than usual, the pecking yet more orderly.

At the end of The Birthday Party Goldberg and McCann dragged the catatonic Stanley away somewhere; the setting of The Hothouse could be where they dragged him. It is a mental institution, with a triple hierarchy: staff, understaff, and patients. The patients are referred to by numbers; the staff are called Roote, Gibbs, Lamb, Cutts, Lush and Tubb. Moral: never get yourself committed to the care of monosyllables.

Not one of them is what you would call compassionate, except perhaps for one underling who ends up with electrodes strapped to his head after undergoing what we used to think of as a typical Pinter interrogation. The play begins with one patient dying and another giving birth; by the end there has been generalised mayhem, which is ruefully surveyed by the man from the Ministry. The characters keep saying there's a Ministry and it turns out there is. So the play ends up making a statement: there is State-subsidised terror.

It isn't a particularly interesting statement, but the author is clearly more hooked on mechanics anyway. In The Birthday Party he left gaps in his story; in The Hothouse he takes us with him, but the results are less effective because more ponderous. This doesn't mean that mystification is more truthful, merely that you gain speed by cutting corners. The smooth brutality leads straight on to The Collection, The Homecoming and beyond, where the honing gets finer, and the people thinner.

What The Hothouse most clearly demonstrates is that The Caretaker, which followed it, was a glorious sidetrack. Mr Pinter has never matched its marvellous equipoise of character, language and feeling. And it's worth remembering that no subsequent playwright has come up with anything to match its critical and popular impact. The author has remained with us as a glory and an embarrassment: a major talent producing minor plays.

He has directed the current exhumation with great good humour, alternating between the avoidance and the exploitation of cliche. (It wasn't cliche then, but . . .) He benefits from the presence in the dominant role of Derek Newark, as forthright and unPinteresque an actor as you could find, who transmits a glorious bovine bewilderment. Others are more conventionally icy, the honours going to James Grant, suaviter in modo, and Angela Pleasence, a stunningly cool devil-woman.

* * *

Mr Pinter was once a small-part actor in Donald Wolfit's company; so, as the world is beginning to learn, was Ronald Harwood. The Dresser (Haymarket) is based on the strenuous provincial nights when Mr Harwood performed its titular function for Wolfit himself. There is little to say about it, except that it's very enjoyable. It sends a current of pleasure through the audience that is rare today, and was probably rare yesterday as well.

The theatre seldom attains or communicates this degree of affection when it is not dealing with itself, but there is little that is sentimental about Mr Harwood's play. His hero, known as Sir because he hasn't been knighted, is on his last legs, performing Lear during an air-raid, with his company depleted by the wartime call-up and himself uncertain whether he can bear the weight either of the role or of Cordelia.

Act One shows him in his dressing-room, being bullied and cajoled into going on (and discouraged from applying the Othello make-up by mistake); Act Two, set in the rest period between the hovel and Dover Cliff, shows him, bullying and flattering his troupe. The actor-managers used to dominate their plays to the exclusion of all others; Sir runs true to form. Every encounter tells us more about him -- his childlike courage and childish selfishness -- but his 'opponents' are so many ninepins.

This even applies to Norman the dresser -- queer (wrong era for gay); Northern, loyal and desperate -- who is excellent value, moment to moment, especially in Tom Courtenay's immensely skilful performance, but who counts at the end for less than his author imagines. It is the only real miscalculation in the play.

Freddie Jones as Sir, magically gaining in authority the second he has donned Lear's beard, is massively vulnerable, and we never tire of learning about him. The script and Michael Elliott's production are generous with professional jokes and practical detail. It is fitting that the best supporting performance should come from Lockwood West as an elderly Oswald suddenly promoted to Fool; his concerns are all professional and he is much the realest of the bunch.

* * *

This production comes from the Royal Exchange, Manchester, which is thriving. In repertoire with Love on the Dole is Gerard McLarnon's Blood Black and Gold, which uses religious imagery with the same abandon as Howard Barker, say, uses political, and seems to me worthy of at least as much respect. It has the same faults of over-ripeness (a trio of young crones, speaking antiphonally, is hard to stomach), but it makes scary capital out of the postulate of a personal God: if God became man, then man has a choice of becoming God or beast.

At the end a girl forced into the first choice lies dead; those who took the second are swathed together under the folds of a fairground monster. Mr McLarnon sends most of his characters around in groups, and individual psychology is hardly his concern, though Nick Stringer makes a striking figure, if not a person, of the master showman, and Dilys Hamlett (such a nice actress, I always thought) is in fine unrecognisable form as a banshee. Braham Murray's production copes imposingly with some terrifying scenic demands.

* * *

Constance Cummings is another nice actress, and an accomplished one, but she is hardly right for the inbred rudeness of Coward's Judith Bliss (who should have been a Dame as Sir should have been a Sir). The result is that Michael Blakemore's typically scrupulous production of Hay Fever (Lyric, Hammersmith) comes off a little flat, despite the deep, grizzled torpor of John Le Mesurier as father Bliss, the petrified politeness of Moray Watson, the pretty poison of Polly Adams, and Mary Griffiths as a dresser with little resemblance to Mr Harwood and less to Mr Courtenay.
