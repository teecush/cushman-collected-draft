Women Beware Women

In The Irresistible Theatre, his inspired and inspiring history of English drama, W Bridges-Adams remarked of Middleton's Women Beware Women that 'it is a play that makes one sigh for the hardihood with which our forebears improved on Romeo and Juliet, for its principal theme fulfills its promise almost to the last'. Some 30 years after that sentence was published comes Howard Barker to grant the half-strangled wish and re-order Middleton's conclusion. Bridges-Adams was a gentleman of the old school, and it is unlikely that the ruderies of Mr Barker would have much appealed to him. What he objected to in the original play was its final scene: a colourful revel in which six characters meet violent and generally spectacular deaths.

(One is pierced by a flight of poisoned arrows; another falls through a trap-door onto a very nasty spike). This finale has also displeased Royal Court impresario Max Stafford-Clark who says in the current programme that he finds it 'unperformable and rather silly. The first charge is demonstrably untrue (the scene worked in Anthony Page's fine production at the Arts years ago) but the reaction is an understandable one. Mr Stafford-Clark talked to Mr Barker and then to William Gaskill. The present production is the upshot. One thing it does is to reprieve every character on Middleton's death-list. This is unlooked-for mercy in Mr Barker, and in another programme-note (no production in recent memory has arrived so bristling with defences) he admits as much.

I almost said he admits it wryly, but that would have been lazy of me. Wryness and Mr Barker are not frequent companions. He admits it aggressively. Middleton's play deals with Bianca, a 16-year-old, who elopes from Venice to marry Leantio, a Florentine clerk. She is raped and/or seduced by the Duke of Florence, and shortly moves to his palace. Her husband, whose poverty now disgusts her, is bought off with the captaincy of a fort. Meanwhile, this being of course a play with a sub-plot, a yo ing gentlewoman LONDON NOTICES Ine Mara called Isabella, who fancies and is fancied by her uncle Hippolito, is falsely persuaded that they are not related at all.

To disguise what would still appear an incestuous affair she marries a rich half-wit known as the Ward. The two plots are thus linked by sex and money; and — this being an unusually tidy tragedy they are further unified by the character of Livia, a court procuress who decoys Bianca to the Duke and invents the story that takes in Isabella. To round everything off she falls for, and wins, Leantio: going into business for herself, so to speak. It is at this point the beginning of Middleton's fourth act that Mr Barker takes over. He begins by letting Livia and Leantio celebrate their loves. Five days and nights they have been at it, and their dreamy rejoicings are like post-coital Donne with the four-letter words left in.

('We fuck the day to death' says Leantio by way of getting things rolling). This scene is brilliant and confirms my impression, first bred by Victory, that nobody in the British theatre writes better about sex - the thing itself, as distinct from what may be made of or deduced from it - than Mr Barker. But of course he does try to make something of it: a revolutionary force, in fact. Awakened by her now love - the love itself rather than the lover - Livia determines to topple the Florentine state. The quaint means she employs is to have Bianca, the duchess elect, raped a second time: this time by a virginal malcontent called Sordido.

What? Let Livia explain (and note how Mr Barker's prose, purposely or otherwise, apes the rhythms of Middleton's verse): 'By this catastrophe she'll grope for knowledge her ambition hides from her. And simultaneously, Sordido's crime will rock the state off its foundations, which is erected on such lies as ducal marriages. 'Where', asks Leantio, 'is the realism in all this?' 'Realism', Livia screams back at him, 'I hate the word'; and so of course does her author. But an attack on a society is pretty hollow if the author is allowed to invent that society as he goes along. The royal-marriage satire has its point, but philosophical rape is hard to credit after the real thing.

In Middleton men ravish and murder for good solid reasons like lust and ambition, and that is far more satisfying. His avowed motive is to reclaim sex from seventeenth-century cynicism; even his lecherous Duke ends up a slightly better if broken man and he has so little quarrel with incest that the theme just disappears. His Sordido is a low-comedy servant transformed; and his Ward turns out to be no fool at all. (These two between them do a lot of talking). In the most hectoring of all the programme-notes the Marxist academic Jonathan Dollimore writes scornfully how else?) of 'those who want to fetishise the play as fixed and unalterable in its greatness. But revision-worship is no less of a fetish, and comparisons are unavoidable. In the original, the Duke is upbraided for adultery by his brother the Cardinal, and decides to reform.

His method is to have Leantio killed so that he may respectably marry Bianca; playing on the snobbery of Livia's brother he persuades him to murder the upstart. There you have social comment, religious satire, structural irony, personal pain, in a couple of acrid, economic strokes. Mr Barker — though he offers some off-thewall wit and a general rallying of forces at the end - cannot compete. The actors though are far happier with the modern half of the play than with the ancient. (I agree with Irving Wardle that the modern half seems more ancient, but never mind). At first I put this down to their inexperience with Jacobean verse, and that probably has something to do with it, but it became clear that William Gaskill's production was reining them in for the sake of consistency later.

Middleton's Livia is generally played (and was surely written) as a merry widow; Maggie Steed can give her only the graveyard shadow of wit, so that the well-fucked lady may have something to awaken from. She then takes control and quivers with life. Gary Oldman's Sordido is fairly arresting, but the character - or rather the style for there is nothing else — is a cliche of modern baroque. Simon Russell Beale who sang and clowned beautifully in Look to the Rainbow last year, is effectively giggly as Middleton's Ward and has immense innocent authority as Barker's; only Nigel Davenport as the Duke commands the stage as well. Joanne Whalley was a promising Bianca on the first night, and probably ripened later; her character, though, is squeezed for development: a metaphorical pawn in the first half, a dramatic pawn in the second.

Leantio goes for practically nothing; while Mr Barker's blows for revolution, Middleton's class consciousness breezes out the window.
