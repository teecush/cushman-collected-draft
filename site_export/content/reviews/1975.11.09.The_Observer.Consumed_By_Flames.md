Consumed by flames

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

J. A. WHITEHEAD'S Old Flames (New Vic Studio, Bristol) might be subtitled "Memories of a Catholic Boyhood."

At least, that at any rate is its main character - a youngish man called Edward who recalls that his first ever crisis was over the Pope. Later it transferred its affections to women but ultimately, to its author's discontent, on to other, consequent objects.

Most of its story has to do with sex. It is concerned, if I identify him correctly, with Edward himself who says that he uses it as cover for seduction, but he means it. And he thinks that by declaring it he in some way absolves himself from guilt and can continue as before. The same firmness underlies his refusal to his current girl to stop him when he goes too far. He half means it, but when she takes him literally and slashes his face he more than half resents it. This repression of an uneasy conscience upon a character too light to carry it is a subtle achievement, both in the writing and in the shifting heartlessness of Gary Bond's performance: a deep voyage into shallow waters.

The play actually takes place on a boat. Sally, Edward's new love, lives on one and she has invited him home. But she has also invited a selection of his current entanglements: two wives and, as aforesaid, a mother. The Pope, alas, fails to appear, but his influence is felt enough to deflect the new metaphorical overtones of the watery setting, but the dialogue and action reek of stillness.

At the simplest, there is Edward's mother, who has herself suffered a long and unsuitable marriage, pouring tight-lipped disapproval of her son for welshing on his first wretched alliance; his second of course turned out equally badly. Edward himself, giving thanks (typically half-baked and, as it turns out, rather premature) for the sexual revolution, toasts the assembled ladies with a magnificent unconscious pun: "We've been through the flames and we've survived." He has been through several flames and here they are, still burning, ready to lick him to death. He does not survive.

At the end of the first act, with everyone present, Edward lightly asks what's for supper. He is told, "you are," and he is. He is absent from the second act, which is largely devoted to a lively, not to say scabrous, discussion of his shortcomings and those of men in general. At length you fall to wondering what has become of him. He is not asleep, or in the lavatory, or even overboard. He has been killed, and the ladies, who appear to be consuming tomato juice and chunks of rhubarb (I was sitting quite far back), are in fact consuming his body and blood; the play concludes appropriately with a solemn parody of Communion.

Even the mother, who has sat in shocked silence through the younger women's more uninhibited exchanges, acknowledges firmly that Edward "had to be put down." This may strain credulity, but the play has been so formally constructed that the question hardly arises. But other questions do: is Edward, so finely exposed to us at the outset, anything worse than an ordinary man with common weaknesses?

But no sooner do you rebel against these women's unfairness to men than you remember that the author is a man; such rebellion is in fact a considerable compliment to him. Is this how women behave when alone together? I wouldn't know, would I. But the actresses seem convinced: in the case of Judy Cornwell and Susan Tracy, superb as the two wives, not just convinced, but possessed. Jane Asher is cool and bleached as the mistress of the revels who turns her houseboat into a Whitehead sepulchre; and Anne Dyson supplies one of her embittered elderly Northern women.

* * *

Miss Dyson is the only player in Jonathan Hale's production who strikes recognisably regional notes, and the disparity points to an ambiguity in the play. The characters at some points appear to be products of a specific background and culture (I assume Liverpudlian, though this is never made explicit); at others to stand for all contemporary men and women. Sexual exploitation may be general, but it does not always take the forms Mr Whitehead depicts. But in other respects his play is rewardingly impersonal; you might take it for the most fervent of propaganda for or against the women's movement. This means, not that it does not know its own mind, but that it has access into everybody's.

If Mr Whitehead writes well enough of women to convince one that he might be a woman, what of Charles Pierce, the American female impersonator at the Fortune? It would be interesting to hear his material - whose treatment of female sexuality is something more than innuendo and less than acknowledgment - actually delivered by a woman. Not, I think, since Mae West has there been a comedienne whose humour was primarily sexual; and she, of course, had censorship problems.

Mr Pierce does Miss West of course; he also offers a delectable Carol Channing addressing her choicest remarks to the Dress Circle with a smile and a curtsey; indeed with a smile that is almost a curtsey. He is most celebrated for his Bette Davis, most striking from behind, raunchily swinging her shoulders. His facial transformations are more remarkable than his vocal; even his account of Miss Davis is a bit approximate. He can do Tallulah Bankhead, but then (to use his own terms) couldn't everybody? There is something ventriloquial about his technique; he sets his creations bitching against one another, but you have to be a rabid film-nostalgia freak (simultaneously loving and resenting the idols) to get an enormous charge out of that. Now, if it were the real thing... But they are creations all the same, quite a few of the jokes are good, and Mr Pierce is quite free of the single-track hypocrisy of Danny La Rue whose humour consists in nudging you about how butch he really is.

* * *

Aneurin Bevan: Struggles Against The Iron Heel, seen last week at the Sherman, Cardiff, is now touring Wales; this week Aberystwyth. The first half is compilation-theatre at its worst; minced slogans which the actors must try hopelessly to convert into conversation. The author, David Illingworth, appears to be another of the breed, currently represented in London by Ian Taylor of Taramtara Taramtara, claiming to be dramatists at minimal expense. But in the second act he spreads himself a bit. Confining himself to two subjects - the birth of the NHS and the nuclear disarmament traumas of the fifties - he begins to build a play, and lets Colin McCormack as Bevan build a character. But the attitude to other figures is skittish, refusing to name them but banking on our knowing who they are.

* * *

The Haymarket, Leicester, has attempted Ibsen's last play When We Dead Awaken, an almost immovable monument which needs casting similar to or identical with the National's John Gabriel Borkman. Michael Meacham's production got the outlines of the play boldly right, but Andrew Cruickshank and Renee Asherson could not bring to life the hollow artist-Ibsen, his very self - nor the model he once betrayed (and condemned to a lifetime of posing on the halls). While they agonised, Heather Sears, in the more approachable role of the young wife intent on freedom, strode off with the play.

* * *

At Hampstead a group called Foco Novo has a free adaptation of Brecht's A Man's A Man, supplying it, among other things, with a happy ending, but failing to camouflage its elephantine (there's an in-joke for Brechtians) development.
