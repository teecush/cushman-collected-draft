The problem of *The Two Gentlemen of Verona* is not one that exercises my mind very often but when it does it takes worrying possession. This is the one play on which criticism can legitimately exercise its ultimate get-out clause and decide that Shakespeare himself didn’t really know what he was up to. (Admittedly I also thought this about *All’s Well That Ends Well* until confounded by John Barton’s rich and humane production.) This is the most complicated but the least sturdy of the early comedies; *The Comedy of Errors* and even *The Taming of the Shrew* make straightforward farcical sense, and once we reach *Love’s Labour’s Lost* we are firmly in the grounds of sophisticated comedy and can enjoy ourselves looking for fruitful ambiguities.

There is plenty of ambiguity in *The Two Gentlemen* but no solid basis throws it into relief. No other Shakespearean protagonists are as cardboard as Valentine and Proteus. Shakespeare may have been intending ironic twists on the love-and-friendship ethic, or he may just be telling an improbable story with a straight face, and no director or critic has yet persuaded me either way. I can go along quite happily with Valentine as the bluff anti-romantic toppled by love, a pale forerunner of Berowne and Benedick, though the idea is sketchily worked out, and a mere reversal of attitudes is not funny unless attached to flesh and blood. But ‘All that is mine in Silvia I give thee’ defeats me. If it’s just Valentine attitudinising again, it is too quickly over to register effectively; a few lines later he is winding up the play unopposed. On the other hand (the mind boggles but I’ve a feeling it’s true) it could be a straightforwardly acceptable noble sentiment. If so, one can only plead in Shakespeare’s defence that it’s a phase he quickly outgrew: no such situation recurs in the canon, though it returns like a spectre to haunt the deathbed of Elizabethan drama in numerous plays by Fletcher, Shirley *et al* which only in my wildest academic dreams do I want to see revived.

Richard Digby Day, treading a little heavily in the footsteps of the gentle and hilarious ragging to which Michael Langham once subjected the play at the Old Vic (memories, memories) has put most of his cast into Regency costume. (The exceptions are Sir Eglamour, very properly got up as a parfit, gentle armour-clinking knight, and the Outlaws who resemble left-overs from a *Robin Hood* panto, singing as they go so as to give approaching travellers fair warning.) This being the Open Air Theatre, however, he has sufficient scenic resources to reinforce the costumes, and those of us to whom fancy dress is just fancy dress soon cease to notice. A lightly mocking fantasia is the goal, but the indeterminate play and the implacably neutral setting are enough to defeat any director’s good intentions.

The surviving virtues are the fundamental ones of liveliness, intelligence and clarity, together with the magical bonus darkness bestows on the just and unjust open-air production alike. John Quentin, drawing a sharp line down the middle of his performance so that Valentine is immature before banishment and adult after it, nearly makes sense of part and play; more heartiness, less preciousness, might take him even further. Proteus and Silvia are pleasantly mellifluous blanks, which may not be what Shakespeare intended but is probably what he deserves. Julia is the all-conquering Gemma Jones, and a sufficient reason for seeing the production. Her warmth and poise, a rare combination, are two-thirds of what the comedy heroine needs; all that is missing is a sense of fun, especially in disguise. Wit already illuminates her scenes with Lucetta (Doreen Andrew, excellent). Launce usually steals this show, and then has it stolen from him by his dog, but this time Bernard Bresslaw and his four-footed friend are amusing but not larcenous.
