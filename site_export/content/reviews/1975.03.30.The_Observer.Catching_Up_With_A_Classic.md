Catching up with a classic

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Schiller's Don Carlos.

As far as our theatres are concerned Friedrich von Schiller is missing, presumed dead, and hardly mourned. The German classics stand high among the plays which our major companies are always promising themselves and us to get around to, but the promises have yet to be redeemed.

It could be that the plays would not suit them. Schiller's historical plays are not amenable to presentation in serge or leather; they demand brocade. So his spiritual home among us might well be Chichester. He would make a palatable alternative to Robert Bolt.

The Cheltenham Everyman, which attracts an audience perhaps more modest than Chichester's but definitely akin to it, is currently staging, with casual but commendable enterprise, Don Carlos; which may well be Schiller's best play and is certainly as neglected as any classic need be. (This seems to be its English premiere.) Its titular hero is the son of Philip II of Spain, but Philip himself is the real centre of interest, as he seems to have been for every writer who has ever touched him. He is walled up in kingship, pursuing a repressive policy in Spain and in the Netherlands, but tempted away from it by a liberal idealism which, though it actually belongs some time in the future (to be exact to the 1780s, when Schiller wrote the play), here finds premature expression in Carlos and his friend, the Marquis of Posa. But Philip's is not the power that rules Spain--that belongs to the Inquisition which brings the king to his knees and seems prepared to bring Carlos to the stake. And for Philip the issue is complicated by jealousy; for Carlos is in love with his stepmother, Philip's young wife. It is rather like seeing Hamlet through the eyes of Claudius.

So at least it seems in Cheltenham, where Norman Wooland's throaty Philip, though somewhat monotonous (this is really a part for a Scofield) has a lonely authority which inevitably breeds sympathy. Carlos, on the other hand, alienates it. This happens to be historical truth (the real Carlos was murderously mad), but it has nothing to do with Schiller's text; whatever he wanted of his Carlos (and he is not very specific) it is hardly likely to have been the petulant inadequacy proffered in this production. Losing interest in Carlos, we inevitably stop following the struggles (half sexual, half political) which occupy the middle of the play; this despite an intelligent Queen (Valerie Holliman) and a sharp collection of courtiers.

The production sails out into the open only with the final confrontation between the King and the Inquisitor (Dennis Edwards), old, blind and implacable; this is recognisably the work of a great dramatist. At the very least the play--in a workable translation by James Kirkup--must rank among the most intelligent historical romances ever written. I was grateful to the Everyman for letting me see it; and my gratitude seemed shared by the audience.

A rep that can offer such a rarity between productions of 'Move Over Mrs Markham' and 'Equus' seems to me worth preserving. And as a colleague pointed out last week, disturbingly and authoritatively, it is the survival of the theatre that now concerns us. (Of course, there are even worthier, more desperate, claimants of public money, but nobody supposes that the cash saved by killing off the arts would ever reach them.) While we hope for the best, we might stop cutting one another's throats; the life of one kind of theatre ought not to necessitate the death of another. I doubt if there is much overlap between the Everyman audience and that for the touring company Belt and Braces (it might be healthier if there were), but both organisations give identifiable pleasure. B and B were at the Jeannette Cochrane last week with 'Weight', a collaboratively written show, which, like 'Don Carlos', builds to a grand confrontation at its close; this time between a one-time miner's strike leader, turned negotiator for the Coal Board, and his wife, left behind in the life he has largely deserted.

Both the writing and the acting here are tough and passionate; a question which should be central to socialist drama--how do you actually live while awaiting the revolution--is posed and, yet more wonderfully, explored. Like John McGrath's 'Fish in the Sea', 'Weight' is a long chronicle, less rich in human detail, but considerably more searching (and more humorous) in its treatment of politics; it also offers some excellent music (though less good lyrics), pleasingly sung by the girls of the company. The men, meanwhile, get to do most of the acting. Is this a new gloss on the old complaint that "men must work, while women must weep"? And does it count as sexism?
