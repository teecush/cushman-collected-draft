Impaled on a rapier

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

EDWARD BOND'S Restoration (Royal Court) is one of those triple-pun titles. Dug to its roots, it refers to a massive act of social justice, not seen to happen within the play, but implied and prophesied. It's also a varnishing job on a period of English history and one of its literary forms, the one generally known as Restoration comedy.

Late Restoration, I think. The play's most prominent character, who has the peculiar name of Lord Are, might have been written by Vanbrugh, with the difference that Mr Bond is concerned to show us the more distressing effects of a nobleman's glittering selfishness. Here is Lord Foppington matched not against a spirited younger brother well able to take care of himself, but against helpless servants. Here also Are, I mean is, Sir John Brute not just ill-treating his wife, but actually killing her.

Why does he so? She is a coal-merchant's daughter, wed for her money, and desirous of being taken to London (mainly, I think, so that she can go to first nights and be glamorously seduced; you know the kind of thing that goes on). To attain this end, she dresses up as a ghost and threatens her husband at the breakfast table. Not in the least disturbed, he carelessly impales her on his rapier. Discovering her identity, he is mildly surprised: 'I do not recall she mentioned a fancy-dress breakfast. . . Who can fathom the mind of one suddenly raised to the peerage?'

Mr Bond may have fasted in sackcloth and ashes after writing lines like those, but he obviously enjoyed them at the time. Simon Callow, who delivers them and who thrives on insouciant outrageousness, certainly does. ('Fore god I am taken with my style. Who'd have thought I'd unloose such a show of bravado?' Everyone, surely.) He proceeds to frame his footman with the murder, and gets away with it. I found myself wondering about the politics of a play which presents the aristocracy as wittily resourceful, and the workers as gullible bores.

I'm kidding of course. Lord Are actually condemns himself, very fully out of his own mouth, and it is Mr Bond's great gift as a comic moralist to make him do this without sacrificing a scintilla of wit. He takes the Restoration style (with particular emphasis on its contempt for all things rural or unmodish), enters it, and turns it against itself. But that still leaves the opposition, it being an unfortunate dramatic fact that the oppressed are not in themselves interesting: usually the reverse. Denied effective action within the play, they have regularly to leave it and deliver lyrics, which, though resourcefully set by Nick Bicat as rock Eisler, all say the same thing: the present is wretched, the future is ours. Worse, the murder scene is fun to watch, a nice conceit, but it is not realistically believable: certainly not as staged by Mr Bond, whose direction, especially after his conquest of the Olivier with The Woman, is remarkably clumsy. But in the second act one character -- the fall-guy's black wife -- takes it as her cue for an earnest protest-drama.

Mr Bond makes some painful points about optimism and gullibility, and even builds up some tension, but his structure gradually crumbles. Philip Davis does something to reconcile the play's two faces, singing powerfully and acting with rat-faced bewilderment, and there is an uproarious intrusion from Irene Handl, though she proves, in more senses than one, a hard act to follow. But mostly in the second half we just wait for Mr Callow to come on again and even his performance, as the material gives out on him, begins to look somewhat askew.

* * *

Dario Fo's Can't Pay? Won't Pay! (Criterion) is, I suppose, a farce. Anyway its characters spend most of the action rushing around trying to conceal things from their marriage-partners and the authorities. The things are foodstuffs looted by housewives from a Milan supermarket as a spontaneous protest after a 50 per cent price rise. But the ladies are afraid their stolid party-line husbands will disapprove: hence the secrecy, thence the chases.

So the play begins with a political statement and it ends with one, or at least with merrily sung slogans; the mayhem in between is motivated less by politics or even by domestic threat -- since the police are a laughing-stock and there is nothing at stake between spouses that couldn't be sorted out in a moment -- than by the author's talent. This seems -- at least in Robert Walker's version, which anglicises everything but the names -- to be less for the strict disciplines of farce than for random zaniness, including much involvement of the audience. (This contradicts one basic farcical rule: there is No Way Out.)

The result is sometimes uproarious, as in a spiralling sequence at the end of the first act involving a truly hysterical pregnancy. Sometimes it's just wearing, partly because though most of the cast have their moments only Alfred Molina has found himself an overriding style, fast but not fuzzy, furious but not strident. He gets the most acute laugh when he introduces his father: 'Dad, meet a West End audience,' a nervous tribute to the oddity (not wrongness) of the occasion: the Half Moon not in exile, but under glass.

* * *

The 'Goose Pimples' syndrome -- placing people through brand-names -- has reached the stage of acute dermatitis in Carol Bunyan's To Come Home To This (Theatre Upstairs). 'She told him she was having an affair with her yoga instructor, right in the middle of the Morecambe and Wise show.' 'I blame those new plastic dustbins.' 'No, I won't let them play with your model railway,' this last with special reference to the InterCity engine. Someone's children are called Branwell and Benedict and Nyree Dawn. The author tells us so much about the habitat, she hardly has time for the people.

This is a shame, since her principal ploy has some subtlety: depicting the breakdown of a marriage by examining its surrogate, the wife's ballroom-dancing partnership. But even a good scene in which this relationship is brutally broken has to be capped by having the scheming dance-instructress remark how handy it is to live near Tesco's. The heroine's drinking problem, supposed to be central, never comes into focus, and anyway sorts oddly with her other symptom, compulsive domestic orderliness.

* * *

At Stratford last year I thought Terry Hands's production of As You Like It wild and woolly: the white and furry first-act set looking to cost the hides of more sheep than you could find in Arden. If it seems less obtrusively frantic now, that is not I think because the staging has been toned down. What has happened is that the actors have ripened, giving Mr Hands his full measure of frenzy (some of it playfully melodramatic, the rest a full-throttle presentation of love's madness), while finding their own space within it. Susan Fleetwood, who is given two enchanting entrances, spills herself vocally and physically full length across Rosalind, and I could happily enthuse for a column over the rest of the cast. Everything comes together beautifully in the end; I have never enjoyed one of Mr Hands's productions more.
