Quixote in his windmill

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

KERRY Lee Crabbe's new play The Last Romantic (Actors' Company, Wimbledon) celebrates the union of this year's most compatible couple, Doom and the English. Having ogled each other for most of the evening, they get together at the end in a small flash of apocalyptic symbolism. Suddenly last summer, in a Suffolk cottage made over from a windmill, the lights go out in the routine warning that heralds a power cut. To be honest I don't remember there being any power cuts last summer but maybe East Anglia had problems the rest of us never knew about.

The cottage is inhabited by an old soldier, who now aspires to save the country with a private army, and his son, a hard-drinking homosexual ex-BBC drama producer (that's not a character, it's a case-history), who aspires to hardly anything. The son's inertia is in itself an affront to his father's briskness and he can arouse himself from it to issue a lazy, memorable political challenge to the effect that the Left must be right because they're so bloody dull. Faced with this kind of insubordination, or with a daughter who decamps to marry the local doctor, dad is liable to throw a heart attack. Which is how it comes to pass that in the last scene, with the lights going out all over Suffolk, the old man is discovered, at death's door and his own, by a lady who, conveniently for everybody including the author, is passing by, selling candles.

Opinions obviously differ as to the utility of candles in a national crisis. Sylvia Plath, at any rate, would not have approved. I mention her because the programme does. For her 'they should have gone out with balloon flights and the stereopticon, these candles.' What is more, 'they are the last romantics.' In fact, in their unassuming inanimate way, they join the small queue of characters trying out for the play's title role. For that title could as well apply to the uncaring son (it is very lonely to be uncommitted) as to the quixotic father. (I got that from the programme, too; I like the idea of a Quixote who lives in a windmill.) Ambiguity can be a rich and stylish thing, but this is confusion.

Mr Crabbe has worked hard at both characters; he has given them excellent things to say, but little to do and less to be; the old man, spouting science and philosophy, seems to have arrived by a circuitous route at a very commonplace reactionary stance.

So attention shifts to the lesser characters, the daughter and the doctor, and they provide solace. Their courtship is shy and brusque (just a quick embrace and the odd bird-impersonation), their disillusion not violent but speedy and merciless. Beyond doubt this is what would happen to the girl in Alan Ayckbourn's Norman Conquests should she ever marry her dull and faithful vet. Mr Crabbe's physician is a similar type less farcically conceived; Tenniel Evans masterfully conveys the agonies inflicted and suffered by the apologetic bore. He goes through the play in stoically ridiculous discomfort, as if condemned to stand for ever on one leg.

With Mr Evans in this form, and with Ralph Michael and Charles Kay rallying round in the heavier roles, it is possible to accept much of the play as domestic comedy, oddly shaped but sharply written. Larger resonances are muted; by the end they are ringing out all right, but hollow and none too easy to distinguish. Mr Crabbe's Plath quotation ends, 'This is no time for the private point of view.' That is a nonsensical position for a dramatist to adopt (even a play written in committee represents a private point of view from where its audience is sitting) and it does not square with Mr Crabbe's own talent. Conceivably he disapproves of his characters, but he never insults them.

* * *

Heading the week's batch of oldies is Pinero's The Gay Lord Quex (Albery), wrongly described in the text-books as a society drama. John Gielgud's immaculate production reveals it to be a social comedy with farcical interludes. The production sometimes changes tone because the play does, but since author and director both know what they are about the changes are not jarring but exhilarating. We continually underestimate the sophistication and complexity of Victorian drama. Tribute, sometimes grudging, is paid to Pinero's engineering skill; nobody acknowledges that he wrote very funny dialogue.

He offers us here a female Figaro; Sophy Fullgarney, who keeps a chocolate-coloured manicure parlour charmingly realised by Alan Tagg, has an understanding with the male palmist across the way. Both of them adopt alien manners and accents (his French, hers London-genteel) to deal with their clientele; they deceive everybody but themselves. The play's aristocrats, by contrast, are almost totally self-deluded. Sophy enters their world to break off her foster-sister's engagement to the eponymous Quex, believing her to be really in love with an Army captain. She succeeds, but there is one element in the situation unforeseen even by her. Everybody in this play is fooled, more or less; hence its comic eminence.

Most telling is the realisation of Quex, the ageing lecher, that it is his age for more than his lechery which counts against him. Daniel Massey takes this moment with a fine laughing melancholy; in the rest of his performance he hammers the point a little hard. Mr Massey has been a juvenile lead for most of his career, but he does not have to atone for it by ageing so remorselessly. He realises that Quex's vaunted gaiety is as much a fraud as any other comic mask, but his performance could be speedier. He rises admirably though to his long cat-and-mouse confrontation with Sophy; there is a satisfying whiff of sawmill melodrama about this scene and you may feel like cheering Judi Dench's final defiance. Miss Dench's capacity to delight is greater than that of any other player I know. Two witty exercises in style by Rosalind Shanks (a good girl) and Sian Phillips (a bad) dispose of the trickiest parts in the piece. This is the West End resurgent.

* * *

The chief ornament of Jean Anouilh's Ardele (Queen's) is Charles Gray, a walrus in knickerbockers. He plays the General who was later to flower in The Waltz of the Toreadors (which might itself have flowered last year had Mr Gray been around). He is related to a Countess, Coral Browne, and a Count, Vincent Price. We are back in high society; and history, if it still takes an interest, may well find M. Anouilh's view of it harsher and truer than Pinero's. Right now, however, the play's trappings have dated without acquiring period charm. Its centre is difficult to judge, consisting as it does of several speeches on the bitter-sweetness of love delivered by the Count. Mr Price has a witty, deprecating presence and a good pettish way with a one-liner (both reminiscent of Clifton Webb), but handed anything more complex he mumbles. Odd suggestions by the others that cynicism is afoot are hushed by a soft, pretty production. This is the West End recumbent.

* * *

In The Knight of the Burning Pestle, Beaumont (though not by scholarly consensus and pace the Greenwich Theatre programme, Fletcher) took the Elizabethan theatre gleefully apart with a contempt for illusion that anticipates Sheridan, Tom Stoppard and Monty Python. The play can be hilarious, as in a school production I remember; or regrettable, as here.
