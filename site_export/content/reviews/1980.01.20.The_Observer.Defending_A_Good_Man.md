Defending a Good Man

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on plays by Howard Barker and Eugene O'Neill.

HOWARD BARKER'S The Love of a Good Man nipped into the Royal Court for a week, to be greeted with sniffy inquiries from the London reviewers as to just what their colleagues had found to admire in the play on its previous appearances in Sheffield and Oxford.

I happen to have been one of those erring provincials (I saw and praised the Sheffield production), so in defending the play now I am probably trying to justify myself. Bear that in mind. As it happens I share the growing dislike for the kind of play that The Love of a Good Man has generally been taken to be, the kind in which a playwright presents a received view of a Britain which he bothers less and less to check against the facts and which is dressed up in some suspect metaphor, bound to be described by someone as 'a powerful image.'

The Love of a Good Man does end (or at least should end - it goes on too long) with a powerful image: the selection, by a shivery and incontinent Prince of Wales, of an unknown soldier to lie in Westminster Abbey. The ceremony is performed on the field of Passchendaele, two years after the end of the First World War, and it has been rigged. The chosen corpse is German, as likely as not, though a visiting lady has been persuaded that it is not merely English but a particular Englishman: her son.

The undertaker employed to clear the field has fallen heavily for her patrician charms and has promised her to get the body back home. By hard graft and sharp practice he does so - or at least persuades her that he has. Meanwhile the Graves Commissioner has turned his own head into a cemetery; he knows the name of every fallen man, and can recite them alphabetically.

So we have a cult of death-worship, debased almost before it has begun: a campaign, not to make amends to the dead for the futility of their sacrifice, but to glorify it, and to get all the details wrong at that. (The one man who knows them is treated as a madman.) Brutality, nostalgia, snobbery and incompetence: a comprehensive indictment of the native myth, and it sticks. The distanced setting mercifully restrains Mr Barker from any pretence of ruthless realism.

His intrigue has a Jonsonian tang and is carried through with appropriate briskness. The dialogue is often pointedly funny: 'I think,' says Hacker, the sentimental mortician regarding the grief of his beloved, 'if someone made a picture of this, it would sell.' There is, on the other hand, a Lawrentian soldier who goes on about finding women with desire in their hips. But the girl he is leaving to pursue this quest knows he is talking embarrassing rubbish and says so. Mr Barker has the grace to loathe all his characters equally.

The only sympathy extended to them comes in the form of self-disgust, a state attained only by Hacker and the Prince. Ian McDiarmid plays Hacker full out, a jumping scream of anguished propriety; you expect to get tired of him after 10 minutes, but his energy and inventiveness never flag. There may be more to his inamorata than the brittleness discovered and exhibited by Diane Fletcher, but I have my doubts.

Nicolas Kent's production improves on its predecessor in most respects, but fails with royalty. The Prince is played for easy laughs (which are not always forthcoming); at Sheffield he was a pathetic, even poetic, oaf making sad but perfectly earnest attempts to reach his people. Here he patronises and is patronised. For all I know Mr Barker may prefer him played like this, but it robs his play of depth and dimension, and may partly explain why the critics have taken it for another easy attack on undefended targets.

This generation of playwrights has never been good on characterisation, which may partly explain why actors have felt compelled to take the matter into their own hands in improvised shows like Hull Truck's marvellous Ooh La La. That satisfies you as a schematic piece like this can never do. But in Mr Barker's own words, 'there are degrees of badness'; this is quite good as badness goes.

* * *

O'Neill, in his one-act plays of the sea, now revived as The Long Voyage Home (Cottesloe), worked in the opposite way; he was fiercely particular. He didn't exactly leave us to draw our own conclusions, but he didn't force a philosophy on us either. He showed us, and told us, that life for a merchant seaman at the beginning of the century was appalling. If we wanted to apply the point to life or society in general, that was up to us; though after watching a fresh-faced Swedish sailor shanghaied from a London waterfront pub to an even worst berth than the one he has just left, we may well consider that we have no option.

O'Neill himself was probably still on the way to engulfing pessimism, but this is recognisably the same world he was to show us in The Iceman Cometh (due later this season). Before that abduction - brilliantly handled as melodrama, so that we hope for a last-minute rescue even if we already know the outcome - we have seen meaningless celebration, lonely death and the brutal invasion of desperate secrecy. The episodes don't all add up to good plays, but the cumulative effect is very powerful.

The plays are, for the most part, excellently presented in a style that stops craftily short of naturalism. John Salthouse as the final victim makes touching use of those uniquely vulnerable Scandinavian vowels: a beamish boy snatched from the audience's arms.
