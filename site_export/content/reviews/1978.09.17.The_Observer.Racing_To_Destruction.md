Racing to destruction

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the revival of Inadmissible Evidence.

JOHN OSBORNE must have had to dredge a bit to find the unfavourable reviews of Inadmissible Evidence quoted in the programme for the present Royal Court revival. It is, after all, his junction play, offering something for everybody; for some, it is the first work of his maturity, for others the last flaring of his youthful promise. (This was 1964, seven years after Look Back in Anger.)

I would identify myself with the first faction. Mr Osborne's subsequent road has been a rocky one, but he has at least shown a lively awareness of who his enemies are (I speak as one of them); before that, he was merely snorting with the crowd.

Inadmissible Evidence is, of all its author's plays, the one in which the chief character shuts up for the shortest length of time. This is despite the fact that he is very rarely alone on the stage, and despite the further fact that his companions are very far from being stooges. The play covers two days inside the life, the office and the head (these are frequently overlapping zones) of Bill Maitland, solicitor.

Nicol Williamson was marvellous in the role 14 years ago, and he is so again now: his is one of the key performances of our time. But a remarkable aspect of this production (directed by the author) is its supporting playing, the best I have ever seen in an Osborne play. Clive Swift as Maitland's managing clerk, offering him sympathy discreetly measured out so as to escape involvement, Deborah Norton a waxen fury as his departing secretary, Rowena Roberts provocatively normal as his telephonist, Paul Greenwood and Marjorie Yates figures of spectral pain as the clients whom he hears with an embarrassing degree of concern since he is projecting his own agonies on to them: all are alive, and their humanity - in the face of all that Maitland can say to or about them - is the best rebuke to his self-absorption. What is more, he knows it. Maitland starts out with the feeling that everybody is against him (this morning he couldn't even get a taxi) and by the end of the play he has had the fact abundantly proved. Everybody leaves or rejects him: his staff, his mistress (more overlapping), his daughter, his wife.

Through it all he talks, with increasing desperation and increasing speed. The most impressive feature of Mr Williamson's performance is its constantly accelerating rhythm; you actually feel a mind racing towards breaking-point. And when his voice is not working, his hands are, convulsively.

The weakest passages in the play are the most self-consciously public: the opening dream sequence, which is paradoxically full of clumsy assaults on bureaucratised Progress, and Maitland's celebrated diatribe (to his daughter) on the subject of Youth, Modern. Otherwise Maitland, given more rope than any other Osborne protagonist, obligingly strangles himself.

He is, however, worth mourning for. Though dauntingly selfish, he is not unloving; that in fact is his downfall. It would be a short-sighted woman who railed at the play merely because it has a sexually exploitative hero. A thoroughly sensitive, responsible, caring man would however be entitled to reject the character and the play. I don't know anybody like that myself.

* * *

In a book called The Season, published a few years back, William Goldman analysed the Broadway phenomenon of the Snob Hit, an annual event - imported from Britain - producing rave reviews, bored audiences and large dividends - plus, every year, a competing set of unsuccessful transplants ending up as Snob Flops. The Americans now have their revenge in the shape of the Camp Hit (or, as it might be, Flop), exemplified by Dracula (Shaftesbury).

This is a 1920s dramatisation (credited, in almost invisible print, to Hamilton Deane and John L. Balderston) revived in New York last year with enormous success due to (a) Edward Gorey's sets - blown-up Gothic illustrations, magnificently stylish and guaranteed to obliterate almost anything that might pass as acting; (b) an uncanny display of wit and beauty by Frank Langella in the title role; (c) the perennial and, when done by Americans, perennially effective stock-Englishman joke, applied to everyone else in the cast.

The London replica has lost (b) and (c). Terence Stamp's Count flashes his cloak with elan, but has no presence beyond that, while Dennis Rosa's production, being conceived in opposition to the text, positively demands that the other roles be played badly. Miscasting would help, but these actors have not been miscast. They have to work at it.

All of them are, in some sense, Bats - a motif that can be found all over (a), whose splendours become in this context, embarrassments. Actually Mr Rosa's style might do better for the rival import, The Passion of Dracula, which was written as a joke. In the current state of the theatre, two Draculae are, at the most liberal estimate, one too many.

* * *

For genuine camp go to the New End, where in Arthur Whitney's A Tribute to Lilli Lamont the phenomenon is both anatomised and exemplified. The anatomising is done by introducing us to a New York fan-club (membership five), whose chairman has turned his obsession with movie memorabilia into a small business. The exemplar of camp is Gloria Grahame, who plays the object of their affection and who acts, as befits a cinematic lady, in short bursts: most of them craftily to synchronise with the dramatic points.

Miss Grahame seems a little surprised to find herself on a stage, but game to play along and to exploit the fact; the same is true, in her rather similar circumstances, of Miss Lamont. Her admirers, cleverly delineated as types (especially the autograph-hunter ruthless in his pursuit of famous persons), get out of hand when the author attempts to exploit them; it is not, after all, an inexhaustible subject and the second half goes downhill. Good performances by Don Fellows and Gordon Sterne.

I have a feeling that Tom McGrath's The Android Circuit (ICA) also belongs in the camp category. It is a harmless piece of SF whimsy, about the last man and his encounter with an exhaustively programmed female robot. Attractively acted, sometimes funny and - at just over an hour - over-extended.

At the Old Vic there is a likeable and finally exhilarating production of The Rivals - not a play I ever expected to be exhilarated by. All the characters are walking jokes, the one difference of each one is zestfully presented; they add up to a satisfying pattern and also tell a good story. The novelettish imaginings of Lydia Languish are beautifully done by Isla Blair; so are the humorous stratagems of her lover by Christopher Neame. Anthony Quayle as his father mingles apoplectic fits with reminiscent lusts; Margaret Courtenay, who took over Mrs Malaprop at short notice (an unenviable task in this of all roles - suppose she got the words right?) turns her mannerisms into obsessions and those obsessions into a personality.
