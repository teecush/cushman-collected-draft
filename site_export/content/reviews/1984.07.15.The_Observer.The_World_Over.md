The world over

ROBERT CUSHMAN

SALESMAN IN BEIJING
by Arthur Miller
Methuen £9.95

IT should by rights be eerie to carry a 35-year-old success around with you as if it were a case of samples. Arthur Miller hit his stride early with "Death of a Salesman." It remains his masterpiece, and he shows no signs of bitterness or embarrassment towards it. He not only loves it, he admires it: passionately but, it seems, objectively. He knows what it means, why it moves people, and every detail of how it works. He seems to be its ideal director.

Last year he directed it. In China. This book is the diary of his rehearsals at the People's Theatre of Beijing. Michael Rudman, who directed the most recent revivals in London and New York (the latter with Dustin Hoffman as a scrawnily brilliant Willy Loman), says that the main problem with the play is to keep the actors from playing their own families, since we've all been through similar rows and regrets, rather than the people Miller has drawn. The fact that we can all identify with it has always guaranteed the play a kind of success. Taking it to China -- where salesmen and Western drama are equally unknown -- was testing that guarantee to the limit.

It survived. "The audience," Miller notes at the end, "laugh in just about the same places as we do in the West, and I have seen many of them weeping for Willy." He found unexpected areas of recognition among the actors. Trying to explain Willy Loman's trick of ignoring obvious disasters, he is told "we're always doing that -- finding some hope where there really isn't any. It's our whole history." Apparently it's "as Chinese as hell." "I had always thought it Jewish. Obviously it's merely human." With Willy's overriding passion -- his love and ambition for his sons -- the Chinese had no trouble; they call it wanting their sons to be dragons.

The book is not especially surprising about China outside the theatre; rueful complaints about service and plumbing alternate with humane anger over the Cultural Revolution. What counts is Miller's remarks on the play. Like Willy, he lives partly in flashback, recalling Elia Kazan, Lee J. Cobb and the original production: the solving of problems that in China have to be faced all over again. Pondering the play for himself so that he can explain it to the cast he comes up with a definitive diagnosis:

"Willy is a lot of things but he is not particularly Good; it may even be that his faults and failings dominate him altogether. But at the same time the play obviously presents him sympathetically . . .

"Willy is indeed a social product while his autonomy as a person remains intact, and this can drive the dialecticians crazy."

It drives them crazy here too; academic critics, incensed that a play should succeed on Broadway and be taken seriously, have never ceased pointing out the protagonist's faults as if they somehow disposed of the play. (Try that line of reasoning on "Macbeth.") Actually it is purely a critic's problem; it has never bothered actors or audiences. A play that can make people laugh and cry for 35 years -- or one year, come to that -- is obviously a good one, though it may have to hit a century before it can be judged great.

It stands a chance; Willy's fate is shaped by the society he grew up in, but is in no way limited to it. In another culture he would have found another way of screwing up his life, and he would still have told himself that he was doing it for his boys. Miller always knew that; the Chinese confirmed it for him. "Death of a Salesman" may have kept him young.
