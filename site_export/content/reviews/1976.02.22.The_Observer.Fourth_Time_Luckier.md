Fourth time luckier

THE RSC production of Man is Man, currently on view at the Roundhouse Downstairs, is quite a good show. I do not say this lightly; I have assisted at three previous renditions of this early Brecht, and I did not anticipate any great pleasure from a fourth.

The play is the account of a transformation; in Brecht's mythical British India an inoffensive packer named Galy Gay is made over into "a human war-machine." Responsible for the metamorphosis are three soldiers who have mislaid a comrade and dread what will happen should they turn up on parade without him. For the sake of their safety Galy Gay must become Private Jeremiah Jip. The real Jip has spent the intervening time locked up in a pagoda, the prisoner of a huckstering priest who passes him off to his congregation as a god, to the great benefit of the collection-box.

Galy Gay's story proves that a man can be made to believe anything of himself; Jip's that other people can be made to believe anything of him. A third variation is offered by the character of Sergeant Fairchild, he of whose inspection the soldiers are so mightily afeared. Finding that sexual desire (which overtakes him whenever it rains) turns him from the "tiger of Kilkoa" to a mere man, he castrates himself to preserve his identity. The most resonant moment in the play (its resemblance to an episode in Peer Gynt is doubtless coincidental) occurs when Galy Gay witnesses this mutilation and decides that if that is what truth to oneself entails, he will stay malleable.

Brecht keeps the three stories going relentlessly; just as you think you are free of one of them, it recurs. For all that, there are loose ends; the Sergeant becomes steadily less formidable as the play proceeds, the soldiers' plight accordingly less desperate. But they go on working over Galy Gay just the same; they may not themselves be compulsive manipulators but their author is. The process involves much debate over the sale of an elephant; actually, two men under a grey blanket. The feebleness of the deception (which is enacted at great length) is held to be its point; the merest whiff of commercial advantage is enough to blind our hero to any trickery.

I would not dwell on the heavy handedness of this complacently cynical piece (it is, after all, far from Brecht's best) had I not been so frequently exposed to it. It appeals apparently to many people; there is a strain of audience that likes blatancy, enjoys repetition, suspects subtlety, and gets a kick out of denying its own intelligence. Howard Davies's production, though, panders to this response less than most. It is very light and quick on its feet; the opening episode--the soldiers' attack on the priest's pagoda--is particularly deft.

It is really the soldiers who drive the play, and two of them here are outstanding: Bob Peck, large, pungent and authoritative, like a well-cooked dish of tripe and onions, and Sid Livingstone, a small sly worrying terrier with something of the amazed serenity of Eric Morecambe. Geoffrey Hutchings is the blank-sheet protagonist, sent permanently packing, and Ben Kingsley gives himself and the audience a good time as the priest.

There is also much musical pleasure; Guy Woolfenden has composed a new setting for the "Cannon Song" (familiar from The Threepenny Opera), which ingeniously circles Weill's version without ever quite stepping on it; and his other contributions are so good that I often wished the actors would junk the play and just go on singing. I blenched though at the final chorus which would make of a violent unloving play a simple anti-war tract. I might take it from the 7:84 company, but not from the RSC, all lined up, with their committed faces on.

* * *

Another morality play, of earlier date, has been revived in Leatherhead, to celebrate the place's twenty-fifth anniversary as a theatre town. (The present Thorndike Theatre, comfortable and impressive, is still fairly new.) The play is Tom Taylor's The Ticket-of-Leave Man, a bridge between Victorian melodrama and problem play, the problem being that of a convict, released for what must have been extraordinarily good conduct and looking in this production remarkably well on it, whose efforts to go straight are continually thwarted: not, unfortunately for the credit of the piece, by society but by his old colleagues.

The ambience is still nearer to Oliver Twist than to Galsworthy's Justice, and Geoffrey Edwards's production looks appropriately Dickensian; the final stage picture, with the play's kind old gentleman seen in profile, benevolently inclined, might have been drawn by Cruikshank. Stuart M. Stanley's sets are excellent throughout; I only regretted the decision to place them within a stylised Victorian proscenium: This signalled that nobody was taking the play seriously, though two performances--Christopher Strauli's as the hero and, better still, Rosemary Williams's as his girl--seemed not to have been let in on the joke and were all the richer for it.

Some of the characters are actually meant to be funny, but if there is a way of making them so, it has not been discovered here. The play suffers, like Brecht's, from the author's determination to keep all his actors constantly employed; their antics protract the evening unduly. But--again like Brecht's--it perks up when there is a story to be told or a song to be sung.

* * *

E. A. Whitehead's Old Flames remains a theatrical black mass more potent than Jean Genet ever celebrated, though it is less compelling in London than it was in the studio space of the New Vic in Bristol. The small proscenium stage of the Arts surprisingly forces it into a naturalistic mould that gets us asking awkward questions: a man literally devoured by his mother and his two wives is a matter for faith rather than sober proof. As a character, though, he is thoroughly plausible and Gary Bond slithers through the role as masterfully as before. He dominates the first half of the play, damning himself (neither I nor Mr Whitehead choose our metaphors lightly) out of his own mouth; in the second, the women damn him out of theirs, and this section belongs to Judy Cornwell, at first a compact comedienne kicking up her heels in erotic reminiscence, then blazing in anger at the threatened waste of her life, and finally freezing her mother-in-law in hate and futility further gone in than her own.

The London first-night audience by the way was considerably more inhibited than the Bristol matrons who surrounded me when I first saw it and who laughed hot and cold throughout. If they were shocked, it was not in their sensibilities but in their souls.
