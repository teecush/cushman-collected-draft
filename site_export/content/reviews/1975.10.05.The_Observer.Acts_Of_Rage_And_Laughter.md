Acts of rage and laughter

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

TO RECAP on Trevor Griffiths's Comedians, which the National Theatre has brought to the Old Vic from the Nottingham Playhouse: it deals with six young men, learning stand-up comedy at evening classes under the tuition of an old pro called Eddie Waters, played with increased authority and undiminished concern by Jimmy Jewel.

At the end of the course they perform, one after the other, in a Manchester club, between bingo sessions. The adjudicator is Bert Challenor, a big-time London agent and, by temperament and experience, an enemy of Eddie's. He believes in escapism, where Eddie takes it to be the comic's duty to reveal the truth. His pupils face a last-minute choice: to stand by what he has taught and they have prepared, or to jack it in and improvise in the hope of a metropolitan contract. In the event they divide equally; in one case the division cuts across a double-act, reducing it to chaos. Otherwise, those who go for the money achieve it. One of them is an Irishman and another a Jew.

`Comedians' is not a play about race, but one of the most fascinating things about it is the way in which ethnic themes, recurring in the dialogue and action, bind it together. It starts from the observation that racial jokes and racial stereotypes -- malicious or good-humoured and very often self-propagated -- are the staples of pop comedy. To Eddie they are anathema, and those of his pupils who adopt them prove him right. Sammie Samuels, one of the sellers-out, is very much a case in point; assailed by a staccato catalogue of stock anti-semitic jibes, he replies, lamely but by the book, `you must have met the wife's family.'

The most memorable lines in Sammie's act are spoken after a brace of Jewish jokes have died on him: `All right, forget the Jews, everyone else has.' He switches into characterless aimless gag-reeling, a transition much approved by Mr Challenor who believes in having a go at everything without actually hitting anything. This is the technique adopted in excelsis by the Irish winner; Bert really goes for him and such is the lightness and panache of Stephen Rea's performance that Bert's judgment in this instance is hard to dispute.

There is also an Irishman among those who remain loyal to Eddie; his humour is quiet and realistic, rooted in his origin -- and more particularly in his position as an Irishman in England -- but not exploiting it; Bert finds him monotonous and parochial, though Jim Norton endows the act with such an easy Dave Allen authority that it is possible to think that, even in his own terms, Bert may for once be making a mistake.

A progression begins to emerge: Jewishness is now too familiar to be anything more than a tired comic reflex, Irishness is going the same way, but its state is still critical enough to permit of thoughtful laughter. Later immigrants are still the butt of jokes, but have not yet learned to seize the jokes for themselves (which could be aggression or capitulation, depending). But Mr Griffiths slyly suggests that they are already on the move: a Pakistani who wanders into the class, looking for some other form of adult education, returns at the end to tell a joke of his own which Eddie recognises as old Jewish. Which wraps it all up.

There are darker echoes here. It was hearing a mild anti-Jewish joke shortly after visiting the site of a concentration camp which sickened Eddie of professional comedy. He has come to distrust the easy attractions of aggression and he detects them in the work of his star pupil, Gethin Price.

Gethin's act might have been tailored to upset the man from London (though it is technically too elaborate to have been whipped up on the instant for that purpose), but even Bert grants him `some talent as a mime.' That is poor praise for Jonathan Pryce's miraculously supple Gethin, whose body is as taut and plangent as a violin-string. He actually plays the violin -- a mocking tuneless version of `The Red Flag' -- and there is mockery too in his stance; at one point he executes a devastating ballet of sham abasement. Outside his act he can be funny as well as electrically angry. Within the act -- which is largely a diatribe against two cardboard (literally) middle-class figures -- a cold coherent rage takes over. Eddie's doubts about whether it can truly be called comic are justified; it is not funny, but more to the point it is not -- the defining mark of comedy -- oblique.

I am not sure that the play fully recognises this; and the uncertainty -- along with some cloudiness, and even a touch of sensationalism, in the argument about the camps -- must be held against it. Otherwise the evening (scheduled for only a few performances, but we can get it extended if we shout) is exceptionally rich.

Characterising his six aspirants, their teacher and their judge, Mr Griffiths sets up complex cross-currents which Richard Eyre's production masterfully navigates. He is witty, too, and it is piquant and instructive that while the vocational comics hammer away, the best lines go to outsiders -- a caretaker, a bingo caller -- who are unconscious of possessing them. Behind it all Mr Griffiths is giving us a re-handling, in immeasurably strengthened form, of his previous play, `The Party.' For comedy serves him as a metaphor of dissent, even of revolution; and the characters divide, without forcing, into those who accept or profit from it, those like Gethin who want to dynamite it, and those like Eddie who hate one side for its mendacity and fear the other for its violence. Mr Griffiths, I would guess, has split himself between Gethin and Eddie; but this time the ambiguity comes over not as an evasion of responsibility, but as a recognition of human complexity.

* * *

The National's season at the ICA has ended at its best with Richard Crane's Bloody Neighbours. The programme credits Mr Crane, the Ernie Wise of fringe playwrights, with 10 plays this year. Like Ern, he often draws on schoolroom classics; `Bloody Neighbours' is one of his rare modern subjects. It still has a touch of precocity about it; its first half featuring a suburban couple is beguiling at the time, but thin in retrospect. The second act, set among supposedly freer spirits, moves heavily, but lingers longer. Brenda Blethyn makes a nagging wife more than tolerable; when she describes a fantasised affair she is positively touching. Touching, too, is the homosexual next door, played by an actor improbably billed as C. J. Allen.

* * *

Mr Griffiths's scheme is big, but smaller than his play; Mr Crane's scheme defines his play, but confines it, too. Andrew Cruickshank's Games (Haymarket Studio, Leicester) is all scheme, and requires characters who have hardly been introduced (in either sense, theirs or ours) to strip one another loquaciously bare. Mr Cruickshank's own presence, a massively quizzical teddy-bear, is the chief compensation.
