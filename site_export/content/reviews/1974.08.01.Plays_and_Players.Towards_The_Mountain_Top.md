Towards the mountain top

PETER HALL talks to ROBERT CUSHMAN about his plans for the new National

ROBERT CUSHMAN: Why did you take on the job of Director of the National Theatre?

PETER HALL: When I left Stratford in 1968 I had a very firm conviction that I would never, under any circumstances, run a theatre again. I'd had it. I felt it was a dreadful strain, which is obvious, but more than anything, it was a liability on someone who's trying to be a director of plays. The unfortunate fact is that when you're directing a play is always the moment when news comes that the Arts Council grant cannot be increased or some member of the press accuses you of doing dirty plays or some actor finds he cannot honour his contract. One is very vulnerable. So I thought from now on I'm just going to be a director--swan around the world doing this and that. But it was a reaction against a central action. Whether I'm any good at running a theatre or not is for other people to say. I certainly don't know. But I do know that for 21 years my main motivation has been to try and create working conditions for other people which I myself can test and enjoy. It's selfish, but also altruistic. I think the two things go together. And the fact that the National has this extraordinary new building proved absolutely irresistible. I don't mean that buildings make theatres--I don't think they do--but good facilities are a considerable help in making good theatre. They're no guarantee of it. They can be a seduction. They can be a difficulty indeed. But I think the South Bank has three beautiful spaces. And it was a bit like being asked to go back to Go, and start again, with better resources and better opportunities. And it was therefore impossible to say no.

RC: If the building had been less attractive, would you still have been interested in the idea of running the company?

PH: No. If the building had been like most modern theatres that I've seen round the world, or indeed if the building was not there and if one was going to stay at the Old Vic, I would not have done it. No, I want to run a theatre again, but I want it to be one with good working and rehearsal conditions.

RC: Are you aware of a certain feeling around that people are going to miss the Old Vic a lot when the National Theatre leaves it?

PH: Oh yes of course. I mean the Old Vic is a very beautiful theatre to sit in, but hard to work in. It's nice to act in, but not to rehearse in. The actors never create their work on the stage where they're going to perform it. They are in dreadful rehearsal rooms which any factory worker would walk out of. I've spent many fine evenings there, as I'm sure you have, and it will be sad, it will be a pang, to leave it; but the National Theatre has reached its end point in Old Vic terms. It can't have enough activity, I think, to be a National Theatre while it remains there.

RC: Now, why is the National Theatre different from other new theatre buildings?

PH: Well, first of all, it was designed by an architect who listened to the brief of a group of theatre people. Denys Lasdun used the knowledge of the theatre that was available. The building committee of the National Theatre--I don't think there has been anything like enough comment or interest taken in this--under the chairmanship of Sir Laurence Olivier, was a very extraordinary cross section of people, including Peter Brook, Michel St Denis, George Devine, Bill Gaskill, Michael Elliott, Jocelyn Herbert, Tyrone Guthrie for a short period. It was a very wide section of theatre opinion, and things were really hammered out. It wasn't a question of A's prejudices against B's prejudices. We really set ourselves the task of defining what is the best possible theatre at this moment. That was the brief, and that's what was given to Denys Lasdun. So a number of very crucial decisions were taken. We concluded that although fashions in staging may come and go, the one thing that does not alter is the scale of the human figure. We decided that if any member of the audience is more than 65 feet away you have a dilution of experience. So we said 65 feet is the maximum, and that is a very very high standard of communication indeed. The back of the stalls at Stratford is I think 83 feet--a lot further. And we thought of servicing the actor, so that he could work in one place, not in a draughty rehearsal hall on the other side of London, but actually work on the stage with his props and actually create his work for the place where he was going to present it. These may seem luxuries but they make for better work.

RC: They don't seem like luxuries, but I imagine they're common to most of the new theatre buildings?

PH: No, they're common to some of the European theatres, they're not very common to most of the new buildings in America.

RC: Just mentioning America, are you frightened at all by what's happened at Lincoln Center? It seems to me a perfect example of an imposing building which has never managed to fill itself with anything very interesting.

PH: I think the problem with Lincoln Center has been that the building was designed without reference to a company, and there was no company either then or now. They keep struggling to make one. I think the virtue of Sir Laurence's philosophy was that he tried to form the National Theatre Company and the tradition of the Company before the building was designed or built.

RC: How much do you consider yourself as being Olivier's direct heir as far as that's concerned?

PH: Well, I was on the building committee, not as heir apparent, but as a member of the theatrical profession. We worked for many years, long hours. I think when it's all totted up in 30 or 40 years time there will be seen to be great similarities between the Olivier theatre at the National, and the Barbican Theatre of the RSC. They came out of the same decade, the same impulse of theatre. I certainly see myself as Sir Laurence's heir because I endorse these two buildings.

RC: I didn't so much mean that, you said yourself he committed himself to forming a company first. Listening to you talk for example, I get the impression you are now more excited by the idea of the building than by the idea of running a company as much.

PH: No. Why on earth should I want to run a building? I think the only way to work in the theatre is with a company. And decent facilities--such as this building provides makes a company more possible. In fact to answer your first question again, the reason why I accepted the job in the National Theatre is because being a freelance is so very wasteful as far as failure is concerned. If you fail as a freelance, you fail, that's it. Within a Company, failure can be as productive as success. There is a continuum. On the other hand I think that within our society the only kind of company you can make is one which is reasonably small in its numbers. I don't believe you can have a company if it's got a hundred actors in it. A company is about 30 or 40 people. I also believe that you have to recognise that actors in order to endorse membership of a group have to have their freedom as well. They do not want to be part of a corporation for ever and ever amen. The only way you can get them to stay is by positively encouraging them to go away. The new company system at the National is based on the fact that we will have three or four groups which will be encouraged to develop their own personalities--their own identities--and that they will be there for eight months and then away for eight months and then back for eight months so that they can do outside work if they so wish. I think that without a continuity of actor/designer/director you can't do anything at all in the theatre. But equally, you shouldn't kid yourself that you've got a permanent company when you haven't and I think we've all tended to do that.

RC: Well, obviously this is a difference from your philosophy when you started with the RSC?

PH: Yes. Nobody had tried when I started at the RSC to run a permanent company. I think that if I did nothing else, I proved that in fact you can't, in the sense of saying to an actor: "Here's a five-year contract. You're not to go away. If you want to do a television serial in order to educate your children privately, it is a betrayal of the company and the company's aesthetics and how dare you--you're a traitor". And all that. I've been through all that.

RC: Under your new scheme, how many plays in a year will you reckon a company actor to be doing?

PH: Well, a company actor will come for eight months and he will be in two main house productions in the Olivier as a minimum. Now, that's not very much for eight months. But he will also have the opportunity of being cast in the new writing seasons or the thematic seasons in the Lyttelton (which are short runs), or the short runs of new plays or of other work in the smallest house, the Cottesloe. So he will have a really wide variety of work which he must and will be drawn into. He has to evaluate his casting on his Olivier theatre offer, and then see how the rest develops. We're doing long term planning for the Olivier and deliberate short term planning in the others so that we can get things up quickly and respond to new plays and new events.

RC: So your first year and a half in the new theatre anyway presumably is mapped out by now?

PH: We're mapped out at the Olivier as we speak until November of next year but we're not mapped out at all in the two others.

RC: My own feeling about the work of the Old Vic so far is that though I've enjoyed most of the productions individually, I haven't had any sense of a corporate achievement at all.

PH: I think it's much too early to say that, if I may say so. Many of last year's company went off to Australia in The Front Page and will be returning to the National in the autumn. Others are going on the West End transfer of Saturday Sunday Monday. I think that the work that came out of The Tempest into Spring Awakening and is now going into Romeo and Juliet--I'm talking about the younger members of that company--is developing a corporate identity. I've no doubt about that at all. And I know that the Next of Kin/Marriage of Figaro group have in themselves, it may not be evident to you, a strong feeling of corporate identity. There are two groups who are in growth. You can't talk about company feeling until it happens. It happens by doing it. And after all we are under six months old. It took the RSC three years before I suddenly woke up and noticed that everybody was telling me we had a company in that sense.

RC: I think that may have been wrong. I think as far back as your first RSC season in 1960 one felt the quickening of something which isn't evident at the National as yet.

PH: I don't know how to answer that. I think you are forgetting the hostility to the idea of the RSC in 1960 and remembering only the praise. I can see it particularly in the Spring Awakening group where I think there's an enormous number of very talented young actors raring to work and to do things.

RC: On the other hand a lot of those talented young actors seem to be fairly uneasy about the prospect of committing themselves to more than one play, never mind about committing themselves for years.

PH: Yes, but they were griping and were encouraged to gripe by Plays and Players. At this moment they're working for a theatre with one auditorium, although they're programmed into a new theatre with 3 auditoriums. I don't blame some of them for being restless. They haven't got enough work to do. Although at the moment Plays and Players talked to them they were just doing The Tempest. Now that they're on tour with Spring Awakening and rehearsing Romeo, I'm getting cries from them that they're being worked too hard. The point I'm making is that the spectrum of work which we can offer an actor will not be there until next April. It can't be, we're not in the theatre.

RC: I'm thinking more of an actor like Michael Kitchen who said in P&P (May) he didn't feel like committing himself to more than one play at a time. I think this must be a fairly widespread feeling among young actors. Is it going to present difficulties for you?

PH: Yes, and I've got to convince them that they're wrong. But they're not going to come for one play at a time, that's all. If we are sitting here saying that the company is the most important thing, it behoves me to stick to the rules of making a company. And to convince the profession that this is a good way of working. And I'm not going to have, and I say this absolutely categorically, I'm not going to have guest visits, in the Olivier. I'm not going to work that way.

RC: Are any of the actors in your current season guests? Or put another way, how many of the actors you have at the moment do you see yourself taking with you as far as the end of this year and then into the summer?

PH: The majority. All the leading performers of The Tempest are returning. All the younger people, when they've done Romeo will be able to evaluate whether they want to go on or not. We couldn't run our eight months on, eight months off scheme when we are only at the Vic. We are in a transitional phase. But Gielgud, Arthur Lowe, Julian Orchard, Denis Quilley, they are all coming back when we do The Tempest again. That was part of the arrangement with them. Now this doesn't mean that someone will be contracted to play the doctor in Macbeth for 2 years and 4 months. He may change. But Macbeth and Lady Macbeth and Macduff and Banquo will not change. Otherwise, they won't play it in the first place. And an author knows his play will be current for a total of 16 months over 2 years, which isn't bad. It's better than anything that's happened so far because any play at the National or at the RSC up to date that's chalked up more than 100 performances has been doing jolly well for itself.

RC: But do you think the National Theatre by definition has to be so large?

PH: But the whole virtue of what I'm doing is that it isn't large. If we were going to take on that space, run 3 theatres 7 nights a week and tour we would need about 150/160 actors, and a repertoire of 40 plays. That is impossible.

RC: That's not what I'm saying. I'm saying really does the National Theatre need to have that number of spaces, that many touring commitments.

PH: No, I don't think it does, but I am glad in the event that it has. Let me explain. When I was on the building committee of the National, there was a minority group of us who believed very passionately that the National Theatre should not have two main auditoria--it should have one. And if we weren't prepared to commit ourselves to what a theatre was in 1970 we shouldn't be doing it at all. A minority of us thought that the proscenium theatre was an insurance which was not necessary. Sir Laurence felt very strongly that if the National Theatre was to be a continuing facility for future generations it had to have a proscenium Theatre as well. And I think on reflection that he was right. If I was thinking of running a theatre in the way that I ran the RSC which, although I don't mean in any way to diminish what my colleagues did to me and for me, was very much the expression of what I wanted, then I think one auditorium is enough. But I don't think that way now. I think the National Theatre in this day and age means a company of actors who are very prejudiced and develop a particular aesthetic. But I also think a National theatre must be an expression of the whole nation's theatre. Everybody who wants to should play there--other companies, other groups. The company itself should do a fairly small amount of work--there won't be more than 6 productions at the Olivier, plus the possibility of touring and seasonal work in the other houses. And most important there will be a great deal coming in from the rest of the nation's theatre or from overseas. That seems to me at this moment to represent National Theatre. I can of course be absolutely wrong about this. The company itself must be as small and lean as it possibly can be. And believe you me if I can do this with two groups instead of 4, I'll do it. But all the rest of it, the ferment of theatrical activity, the cross-fertilisation by going out to the regions and the regions coming in to us, that represents National Theatre to me. It's not an expression of my taste--it's a facility. And I think that the building is primarily a facility in which a small National theatre company can function and the nation's theatre can talk to the whole community. So that's why I now think Olivier's decision was right.

RC: With the great increase in the National's own activity is the Aldwych going to seem a little redundant?

PH: I wouldn't have thought so. It seems to me that there are whole areas of drama which are the concern and interest of the RSC at this moment and which will remain so. I thought when I went to the National that, possibly, there were only about 500 plays in the Western repertoire which merited revival. I think I was wrong. I've had the benefit of 2 years of study and thought. There's an enormous amount that needs to be done. German theatre's virtually unexplored in this country. French theatre's also unexplored in this country. As far as new writing is concerned I basically believe that the more opportunity there is to put on new writing, the more new writing will exist. Therefore if the Court, the Aldwych and the National are all presenting new plays there will be that much more new writing and the more new writing there is the more new writing there'll be. And there's one thing I would like to stress very strongly. There seems to be current, because the National Theatre is a modern theatre and it's therefore, and why shouldn't it be, full of modern equipment, that we are going to be engaged in large spectacular productions. That we will constantly be staging The Battle of Trafalgar with full scenic effects. The machinery that the National has been designed for one thing only: to make it possible for a play to be changed from the matinee to the evening performance and to make it possible for the actor to create his work on the stage in the set. There will therefore be more variety in the repertoire day by day. With the cost of raw materials and roaring inflation we are going to be driven and should be driven in my view into discovering ways of doing theatre with people in space and with light. No doubt about that whatsoever. And as two of our theatres out of the three are spaces where scenery is not necessary we are in a good position. I think there is hostility to the new National because it is the biggest and the glossiest and the most heavily endowed. I think also we're going through a national nervous breakdown with a consequent lack of confidence. We like to think that everything we're doing is disaster. I don't just speak of the theatre.

RC: Isn't there a more general disillusion with institutional theatre?

PH: Yes, and that is a swing of the pendulum. I think it's grossly exaggerated and wrong. I'd like to know what kind of life you'd lead as a critic if the institutional theatre did not exist.

RC: I'd lead a dreary life. You said yourself, about a year ago, that you thought all three of the institutional theatres had got to a stage where they were very predictable.

PH: Yes, I agree. I still do. I think everybody is looking for new paths. I don't expect that I'll find it and I don't expect anyone'll recognise I've found it until we're in that new building. I believe that the National must have a repertoire of plays which mean something to the moment--in other words I don't think there's much virtue in having a repertoire of classic revivals for scholastic or representational reasons or to show that we've all got a catholic taste. I think that you've got to believe that the repertoire should represent the collective obsessions of the actors and directors who are working in the National Theatre because they believe that the play will speak to their audiences. Our theme seasons in the Lyttelton will investigate areas of the repertoire which are entirely neglected.

RC: Which areas?

PH: Well, German expressionist drama is totally unknown in this country. It's just something we pay lip service to. I would think twice about doing a Toller play or a Kaiser play in the Olivier repertoire, but if I was going to do three of them together for a short season in the Lyttelton called German Expressionist Drama, with a few films at the NFT of German expressionism, some pictures in the foyers and a few talks about German expressionism, one could get something going. A further responsibility is obviously new writing and that will come from the writers who want to write for us. Pinter is there, he's an Associate Director. I hope he will write for us. We've commissioned plays from Howard Brenton and from David Hare of the younger dramatists, and all the heavyweight dramatists have been asked if they would like to write a play for the new National Theatre. They may or they may not. That's up to them.

RC: Let's keep for a moment to the idea of what the plays you yourself personally want to do as a Director. Do you feel very differently about that from what you did, say, when you were directing the RSC?

PH: Oh yes, because when I was in the RSC I was more than anything passionately concerned with Shakespeare and there are a number of plays I very much want to do, but they are not in the centre of my preoccupations at this moment. I would very much like to do Ibsen, and I'm going to try and do John Gabriel Borkman at the end of this year. Obviously if Pinter or Beckett write anymore I would hope that I would be involved because they speak to me and I feel very deeply about them.

RC: I made a joke about terminal theatre in The Observer, because I felt that it's a long way from The Wars of the Roses to All Over. I mean that's the direction in which you've been travelling. Are you conscious of this?

PH: No, I'm not actually. Do you think it's getting more depressive?

RC: Well I do, I think what I was actually on to was that your work is getting smaller.

PH: I can offset that by saying that I think the most important piece of work for me that I've done in the last few years was the Monteverdi Ulysses at Glyndebourne, which was a piece of epic theatre. That's why I want to do Tamburlaine in the Olivier. I find Borkman an epic play which starts small and opens up. It starts on a sofa and ends with the world.

RC: You've never done Ibsen before.

PH: No, never. Why I'm attracted to Borkman is precisely that sense of the iris opening out--you start in a claustrophobic tiny room and end on the mountain top looking at the world. In my mind it connects with Tamburlaine actually. On the other hand, I'm doing Beckett's Happy Days which is extremely terminal, I must agree. As a director, I would like to do farce, I've never done farce. I think I need it. I think I need the anarchy of farce, and the vigour of farce. But I think once I went into Opera, my theatre work tended to become more and more concerned with the head of a pin in a way, Pinter and Albee--very, very detailed. I left the larger gestures to the opera world. Well, I won't say I've finished with opera, but obviously opera is not going to play much part in my life in the next few years, but large emblematic statements of theatre like Tamburlaine I'm very keen to do. I'm very keen also to try and bring back some rhetoric, not in a pejorative sense, to classical acting. When I have to stand up and be counted I do think that the contribution of the RSC, which I was partly responsible for, was the reconsideration of what Shakespeare's verse meant intellectually and what its ambiguities were; how you spoke it wittily and lightly and quickly. I'm proud of all that. I think that was something that's made a mark. But the converse of it produced by its strength, is perhaps 300 actors who are extremely intellectual in their approach to Shakespeare in danger of being insufficiently physical and emotional.

RC: Isn't a National Theatre in a tricky position when another company has the first right to the national dramatist?

PH: I don't think so, there are 37 plays. The Stratford tradition is something which I honour and obviously have a terrific love for, and it will go on because the Stratford festival will go on. I believe that the National Shakespeare is going to take a little time to emerge. I know what it is and what we intend to do, but I'm not going to tell you because it has got to happen, not be talked about. We're not in competition with the RSC in any kind of "Hands off Hamlet" spirit.

RC: The National's Shakespeare has after all had 10 years to emerge already.

PH: Yes, but you see one of the interesting things about that is that they haven't done enough Shakespeare.

RC: That's one thing I'm getting at, they haven't done enough.

PH: Well I think we will do quite a lot but in different terms to the RSC situation. You're pushing me, and I don't want to be pushed. But I mean I take your point that the National can't ignore Shakespeare or it can't say "Thank God we don't have to do Shakespeare, there's the RSC", because actually inescapably the centre of our theatre tradition, whether you like it or not (and I do), is Shakespeare.

RC: Well, I wouldn't be interested in the theatre if it wasn't for Shakespeare.

PH: Nor would I, nor would I, you know, but on the other hand I would hate to run a theatre again whose main external purpose was Shakespeare. If you've got to do six plays a year they come round with great regularity.

RC: I'm beginning to find as a critic that's not true of just Shakespeare.

PH: You're not seeing enough Shakespeare?

RC: No, I'm seeing quite enough Shakespeare. I'm also seeing enough of a certain number of other classics. I don't know how big the classic repertoire is outside it. I find it quite significant that the Actors Company this year are doing two plays that the National have already done, one of them pretty recently. I do sometimes worry about there being too many companies, too many institutional or semi-institutional theatres chasing too few plays and too few actors.

PH: Yes, I worry about that too and it seems to me that one of the things that the National has got to do is broaden the repertoire. By looking, looking out, out, out. We've got to German expressionism, but we've got to do Marivaux.

RC: I'm glad you said that.

PH: Right, we just must. I mean we will of course have little help from the critics who are very stick in the mud as far as looking at the past repertoire is concerned.

RC: I would take that personally very badly indeed.

PH: There's too much received opinion trotted out. Too much of a feeling that if it's old, it must be boring. The reception of Spring Awakening was generally very good and the audience response has been splendid, it's packed and all that and there's a lot of young people there, because I think with reason young people find it a play which is about authority over the young, you know; but so many critics simply said "In this permissive age, who needs a play about sexual liberation", which is exactly like saying "In an age where countries are not ruled by hereditary monarchs, who needs Hamlet". I mean that's really not the issue.

RC: I quite agree, but I think one reason why they've been driven towards it is a feeling shared within the profession itself. Are we all a bit too hung up on relevance.

PH: I think we are. That's the cant of the last 10 years which has hardened into a kind of dogma.

RC: Do you find anything odd in the idea of writers like Trevor Griffiths or David Hare writing for an institutional theatre representing a society to which they are fundamentally opposed?

PH: Well, who says that these institutions represent their society in that sense. I feel certainly that I'm running a theatre which is supported by the society that I live in at this moment. But the society is not telling me what I shall do and shall not do. I had a great argument with Trevor, who I'm very fond of and think very highly of, not very long ago when he said to me "Yes, I'll maybe give you the play after next, but I'm not going to give you the next play because it wouldn't be good for you, you wouldn't do it." And I said that is the biggest bloody impertinence. If you say I won't do it, you are turning me into the sort of theatre that you want to turn me into and you're not giving me the chance to be anything else. Now why will I not do it? And he said, well you wouldn't it would be too controversial. I said well you're frightened I'll say yes, and it would then upset your dogma. We had a terrific carry on. It was good. If a dramatist is absolutely seeking to change his society, it seems to me he can decide either to use the institutional theatre in order to get the message across, or not. It's up to him isn't it, not up to me. But certainly this particular runner of an institutional theatre is not going to silence argument or controversy or unpopular opinion. I won't. Obviously there's going to be a good deal of this sort of discussion and argument. The last thing I want the National to do is to trendily run after the most insulting and revolutionary dramatists and do them in a desperate attempt to show that we are broadminded. I really think that would be odious in the extreme.

RC: How broad would you say your tastes were?

PH: I think they are getting a little broader. I managed to get through the period at the RSC without doing a single restoration play. As I was leaving we did The Relapse. Restoration drama has been for me for years something insignificant. Now I'm beginning to understand it. I must say for many years I have thought of Shaw as a sexless pamphleteer with very little passion. Over the last years I've been re-reading and re-thinking Shaw very much and I want to do a Shaw play very much now. Where the boundaries of my own taste lie, I don't know, they change a great deal. I suppose that I could be seen as a working class scholarship Cambridge-warped, media-altered, lefty trendy child of the 'sixties and that's my period, I suppose. But I refute that, I'm beginning again. The excitement of the 'seventies for me, is going to the National. I feel all sorts of new currents in myself. I'm excited at the prospect of working again in a theatre which must be part of the community. So I won't be labelled: it makes me indignant.
