Playing it for laughs

THEATRE

Robert Cushman on the frontiers of comedy and farce.

The term "sex-farce" is beginning to be employed, shall we say, promiscuously in the London theatre. A title (and there are many) of the order of *Bed and Bored* will, of course, make any critic cower; we are none of us in favour of British rubbish. We cling, however, to the belief that they order these things better in France.

Or anywhere else on the Continent. One side-effect of the Feydeau fashion which spread so enjoyably a few years ago has been the labelling as farce of any reasonably jocular theatrical treatment of infidelity among the European middle-classes, at the end of the last century or the beginning of the present one. If the play includes a scene in an eating-house, the case is generally considered clinched.

I am referring as much to audience expectation as to managerial promises. As far as I know, Molnar's *The Wolf* has never been advertised as a farce, but when I revisited it this week (on the first flight of its abrupt transfer to the Queen's) the leisureliness of the comedy clearly took the house by surprise. After all, the setting is Hungarian, and the programme clearly locates the first act in "the dining-room of a fashionable hotel." In such a soil preconceptions prosper.

The production now goes some way towards meeting them. The fantasy sequence is more sharply stylised than before, so the audience twigs that it is a dream far earlier, and is deprived of the realisation which, at the Oxford Playhouse in September, only dawned when the curtain fell. This seems to me an improvement, but I have to admit that it detracts from the play far less than I would have expected. The weight now rests firmly on the evolving relationship of the married couple played by Judi Dench and Leo McKern.

Their performances have gained not so much in depth (which was there to begin with) as in ease and solidity. Both from time to time employ familiar comic effects, Mr McKern tactlessly raising his voice to his wife with waiters present, Miss Dench lovingly lengthening the vowels of her final "I adore you"; but the decoration always sits logically on the structure. A good test for comic acting is the ease with which it can support an occasional extravagance. In the last act Edward Woodward, released from a series of brash disguises, shyly emerges on the level of the other two. They make a very likeable trio; the evening is perhaps the most distinguished to be had in the West End.

Molnar shows us a husband who is unsure of his wife's love and so attempts to buy it. A faintly similar situation is presented in Georges Courteline's *Home Life*, at the Howff, in what is frankly billed as an evening of French farce. Here the husband attempts to blackmail his wife into submission by fining her every time she displeases him. The mechanics of this system are sufficiently emphasised for the play to qualify as farce; and there is one definitely zany moment when the lady threatens to commit suicide by jumping from a ground-floor window. But nothing so grand as adultery, real or suspected, is at issue; the relationship is compact of pettier aggravations, the kind that actually do make marriages miserable. The play in fact puts farcical techniques to seriously comic ends: a respectable bastardisation. Courteline is a bridge between Feydeau and Ionesco; one of his wilder pieces is composed mainly of a loving couple throwing the furniture at each other. But his success can hardly be judged from this production, in which the two performers seem hardly to be in the same play, let alone the same marriage.

Coupled with it, and far better done, is a genuine Feydeau, *Respectable Women*. The master's one-act plays are held by connoisseurs to be his masterpieces. The complications are distilled into a perfect symmetry, thoroughly predictable and mathematically pleasing. Given a little breathing-space, Feydeau develops the bilious view of character implicit in his full-length works; he virtually invents a miniature genre, the farce of bad manners. Philandering males and outrageously prim cocottes feed off each other (almost literally, thanks to the traditional restaurant setting); Christopher Benjamin and Christina Greatrex are a fine pair of mutual predators. Realism, though not of the Stanislavskyan kind, is the key to Feydeau; this truism is still neglected, not least in Paris where I recently saw a prettified version of *L'Hotel du Libre Echange* (*Hotel Paradiso*), which turned the play from a satire on bourgeois morality almost into a celebration of it. (The audience glowed.)

Type-casting has an ugly reputation, but it is a necessity in farce, and in much comedy. Intelligent approximations are not enough. The wittiest, cleverest performance in *The Apple Cart* (Bristol Old Vic) is given by Judy Parfitt as Orinthia, but it misfires since Orinthia exists in the play as the King's mistress and Miss Parfitt is one of the theatre's natural wives.

Ultimately, of course, Shaw's play depends on the presence of one of the theatre's natural monarchs and this it is granted. On the first night Peter O'Toole, creased and handsome in green corduroy, staked a good deal on charm (as, I suppose, royalty must) and won. Clearly under-rehearsed, he fluffed engagingly through his first scene, so that one's heart lurched at the thought of the marathon speech ahead. In the event (and really we might have known), he brought it off flawlessly; "You did speak that piece beautifully, sir," says one of the ladies in the Cabinet, and I expected to hear the auditorium echo in agreement.

There should have been applause, too, for Thelma Whiteley, whose delivery of the Powermistress's attack on the big business interests bit harder than anything else in the play, a circumstance for which Miss Whiteley and the state of the nation should perhaps share the credit. Nobody will begrudge Shaw his easy laughs at politicians, and Mr O'Toole makes the fleeting fantasy of kingly rule undeniably seductive. Perhaps that is because it remains a fantasy throughout; it might have been harder to swallow the reality.

The Bristol complex is currently booming. Mr O'Toole is packing them in at the Royal, the Little is celebrating its golden jubilee with *Blithe Spirit* (Peggy Ann Wood, who, with her husband, Ronald Russell, built this theatre up, plays Madame Arcati, the happy medium), and the New Vic Studio, more harshly occult, offers John Downie's collage version of *Dracula*. This begins in stock menace and ends in total obscurity: the middle, though, is scary. Howard Davies's rigorous and inventive production demands a high level of intrepidity from its actors and, especially from Roger Gartland and Gay Rose, receives it. The action is filtered through the mind of a madman, a popular device which gives the audience no means of checking the relevance of any section or the worth of the whole experience.
