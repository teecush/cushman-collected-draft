Voices off

Edward Albee, writing his first radio play (broadcast last Sunday on Radio 3), took to the restrictions of the medium with wary eagerness. Listening was the title, putting the audience on the spot from the moment they read the billing. On another page of the Radio Times could be found the baleful news, imparted by the author himself, that the play was "just another of those things about people's failure to communicate". His characters were less casual about it; there were three of them, and the least comprehensible of the three repeatedly complained that the others did not listen. Then, having made the charge several times, she refined it: "pay attention is what you don't do". Mr Albee's regard for language is as scrupulous as ever.

It is the only thing for which he does show any solicitude. His stage plays since Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? have been increasingly hard on their spectators; All Over, his latest play to be seen in London, was as difficult to sit through as any piece of the last decade. Listening to Listening was easier, by the difference between an Aldwych stall and one's own armchair. One could fidget in comfort. And the banality behind the lines was less oppressive. One resents a play like All Over that fools around with death; fooling around with non-communication is permissible--everybody does it.

The protesting character was called The Girl; she seemed disturbed, and possibly in care. She and The Woman bitched at each other with varying degrees of elegance. The Man stood by, defensive and appalled. An American actor, James Ray (the play was recorded in New York), played him in a style of fruity bewilderment dispiritingly observable among radio performers in this country. Irene Worth, having proclaimed herself a more disciplined actress on the air than on the boards, justified the assertion with a reading of precise and silvery inhumanity. The author, though billed as The Voice, was not heard from.

There were two moments of solidity. In the first, Miss Worth told the story of her grandfather who had vanished; to judge from the conflicting reports of those who had seen him depart "he'd gone off in several directions at the same time ... he had, in effect, dispersed". Since present relationships were so tenuous, it was nice to have this bit of past to cling to. The second came when The Girl jumped into a fountain: no audible splash (this is modern radio, very pure) but at least we got our bearings for a moment.
