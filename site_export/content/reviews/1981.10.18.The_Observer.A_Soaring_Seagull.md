A soaring Seagull

by Robert Cushman

I did not expect to see a better production of anything this year than Max Stafford-Clark's Irish version of The Seagull. I certainly did not expect to see a better production of the same play. But here (at the Almeida, Islington) it is, directed by Mike Alfreds for Shared Experience and winning by a beak.

Both the grouping and the individual performances are three-dimensional. When the spectators at Konstantin's play begin chattering and interrupting we almost become accomplices in their rudeness; the break-up of the performance carries enormous force, and is a major foretaste of Konstantin's self-destructiveness.

The opening duologue sets an unsparing tone for the production. For a few seconds it seems over-emphatic; then it delivers the truth. We are beginning to learn that Chekhov can take tough handling; the marginal character of Medvyedenko (here played by Peter Wight) gets funnier and more wretched with each production. Masha's famous reply to him, 'I'm in mourning for my life,' is followed here by the unspoken words, 'you fool.' It doesn't stop the schoolmaster from whining on about Konstantin and Nina being in love, and uniting in the creation of 'one artistic image.'

Maggie Wells makes a meal of Masha's snuff-taking, and of her melodramatic confessions of love (delivered always to a third person, sympathetic but unsympathising). At every point the production homes in on the characters' small sillinesses and larger evasions. I think of Shamrayev (Matthew Scurfield) sashaying on with a riding-crop to play the part of brutal overseer; Sorin (David Blake Kelly) reflecting that to be 'a small literary figure would be nice' and wincing in memory of his own futile career when it is suggested that Konstantin should follow him into the Civil Service; the doctor (Philip Voss is the best and pearliest Dorn since George Devine) elegantly sidestepping emotional commitment; and his whimperingly adoring Polina (a funny and revelatory performance by Sandra Voe) snatching from his hand the roses presented to him by her daughter.

Philip Osment's Konstantin is a bearded, unruly mother's boy, who in the third act returns to the womb almost before your eyes. But there is enough fire and anger in him to make Nina's love seem at least a possibility, and a wonderful moment towards the end when she falls crying into his arms. As Nina, Lorna Heilbron passes from enchanted (and enchanting) impressionable girlhood to bitter maturity; she slides into 'I am a seagull' with dauntless conviction. Jonathan Hackett's Trigorin is properly deglamourised; his trousers are held up with string and he gravitates naturally to the outskirts of any group; but he lacks authority and his long speeches sag.

Gillian Barge wittily sizes up Arkadina's indulgences, but she overdoes them. She has great moments though: ploughing through a salad while supposedly worrying about her son, and at the end when she is plainly aware of the meaning of that pistol-shot. She eagerly accepts Dorn's reassurances, and the action is then prolonged a little; we see the doctor striding off to attend to the corpse, while the others settle back into their game of bingo.

If the rest of the production had not done so, this sequence -- extending the play without falsifying it -- would establish Mr Alfreds as one of our three or four best directors; add to it the huge audacious pause that follows the end of Konstantin's play when everyone waits for somebody else to say something.

* * *

Chekhov is briefly invoked in Michael Abbensetts's In The Mood (Hampstead), when a black actor tells about playing Uncle Van in a musical about three sisters who want to leave Atlanta to live in New York and in which one character wears black because... (For some reason nobody is threatening to chop down their cotton plantation.) Mr Abbensetts may also have been mindful of Chekhov's admonition that if you show a revolver in Act One, you must fire it before the end; he threatens, but there are no gunshots. In fact, little momentous happens.

In a cast of five, three men and a woman are West Indian; the fifth (played with appropriate washed-out attractiveness by Marty Cruickshank) is an actual or potential girl-friend for all the male trio, who represent different experiences and viewpoints among British blacks. One is a well-to-do plumber, another his WWII buddy hooked on reminiscent patriotism. The third is younger and angrier, but a chronic wastrel.

The play is decently entertaining, but everything about it seems obvious. The title refers to Glenn Miller; the fact that nostalgia is under attack doesn't make the shorthand any easier to accept. Stefan Kalipha is funny if disorganised, and Norman Beaton's contained irony makes him the favourite black actor of white spectators, this one included.

* * *

A few rungs down the same naturalist ladder is Ellen Dryden's Harvest (Ambassadors), in which a London refugee miserably returns to her provincial family. Just down the streets from the theatre a plaque commemorates the Wesley brothers; they are less vividly present inside. The play begins with a Methodist funeral service; for the ending see the title, probably also referring to what the characters have got out of their religion, which dramatically seems to be not much, despite a hovering minister.

Again the expected conflicts are gone through; the most potentially interesting -- involving the breakdown of a mature student on a degree course -- is frittered away by being filtered through to us only through the nominal protagonist, the victim's sister. Lynn Farleigh plays her with the tart, bloodless cadences of Glenda Jackson. (The play comes from the same stable as Miss Jackson's hit 'Rose.') Supporting acting is sharp; especially Constance Chapman as the eldest relative and Sylvestra Le Touzel as the youngest. Donald Gee is touching as the mazed brother.

* * *

Finally a late and early Shaw. The early is Arms and the Man (Lyric), given an attractive, good-natured production by Jonathan Lynn. Bulgaria here becomes Ruritania, and some of the cast too obviously relish the joke. Anna Nygh as the supposedly sensible maid looks constantly about to burst, red lips and hand on hips, into Ivor Novello; and though Peter Egan looks the ideal hussar as Sergius, his striving voice betrays his disbelief; his descents into bathos mean little because he's there already.

The evening's main success is the developing romance of Raina and Bluntschli; there is chemistry from the start between Alice Krige, who commands both the heroine's faces, and Richard Briers, who may not be the image of a professional soldier, but whose timing and delivery are impeccable. There are well-grounded elders from Richard Pearson, Pat Heywood and Anthony Pedley; pretty sets from Alan Tagg; and infallible patterning of theme, plot and dialogue from the author.
