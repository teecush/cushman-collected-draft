Case for subsidies

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a worrying report.

NOTHING that happened on stage last week had quite the dramatic impact of the report in The Guardian that a dozen theatres, and four fringe companies, might have their Arts Council grants axed in the coming year.

It may be a false alarm. Here's hoping. But it's worth pointing out that the whole question of theatrical subsidy is at stake. It would be helpful if the Government could state clearly whether or not it regards the theatre as worth supporting. If it does, then it has to accept that the theatre is an entity, and indivisible. If sections of it can survive without public money, then hooray. But most cannot; and the theatres in question -- they include 10 reps and, in London, the Royal Court and Open Space -- would die without it.

If the theatre is to be subsidised, then the money cannot just go to a few centres of supposed excellence. In the first place, it isn't fair; in the second, excellence has a disconcerting habit of changing its abode every couple of seasons; and in the third, the best depends on the good--even sometimes on the bad. We need standards of comparison.

To make the argument less rarefied: a successful National Theatre or whatever needs to be able to draw on actors who are experienced on other stages and have not had their morale shattered and their skills blunted by almost total unemployment. None of the theatres reportedly threatened has an unblemished artistic record, but then who does? If the Government does not believe in supporting the theatre, then perhaps it should say so now, and put us all out of our misery.

* * *

Meanwhile (and I trust that nobody will take this as an argument for decreasing their subvention) I have to report two rather fidgety evenings with the Royal Shakespeare Company. Howard Brenton once illustrated his scorn of apolitical drama by pointing to the forgotten Russian playwrights of the years preceding the revolution. 'Fancy writing about drawing-room love in that situation,' he said. I doubt if Maxim Gorky was among the dramatists he had in mind. All the same, watching Gorky's 1905 play, Children of the Sun (Aldwych), I found myself recalling Mr Brenton's strictures, and unexpectedly endorsing them.

The play is not wholly about drawing-room love. There is a world elsewhere, a world full of cynically discontented peasants and servants. At their head is a drunken, wife-beating blacksmith. The gentry hate and fear him, but they need his services. Eventually he is quietly felled by the cool and competent yardman, the figure who seems most clearly designed to show the central characters up.

These characters are: an ivory-towered scientist, his wife who loves an artist, his sister who loves a vet, and the vet's sister who loves him. There is no way these relationships can balance out, especially as most of the characters, though terribly gentle and terribly polite, are blind egoists. So they thwart and stifle each other across four acts, at the end of which the most sensitive among them are either mad or dead.

In the meantime they have done much talking, for each is obsessed by an idea. The scientist, a benevolent Frankenstein, looks forward to the time when men will come into their inheritance as 'children of the sun.' His sister feverishly laments the cruelty and brutality of the world. The artist is understandably put out by all this vociferous idealism.

Chekhov's people talk and dream in much the same way, but with a firm structure to support them. Gorky's plays are peculiarly frustrating for an audience. Here is an obviously major writer, and he cannot hold your attention.

Terry Hands's production does not make concentration easy. The play at its best is a comedy of manners, so both text and actors could do with some realistic support from their surroundings. Here we get three hanging screens, with a fence (of both practical and symbolic utility) beyond; also a few very spartan chairs.

Carmen du Sautoy, Sinead Cusack and Natasha Parry all extend their range remarkably. Norman Rodway has a nice barking geniality, vocally in the Donald Sinden line, but he lacks the shameless Sinden bravura that might propel the play along. Alan Howard as the vet oozes distinction, but his performance is hard to get at, walled round as it is by an impenetrable Caledonian accent. John Shrapnel makes a cheerful vigorous job of the artist.

* * *

Anna Christie (Other Place, Stratford-upon-Avon) is well carpentered enough; the trouble is content. This is the most popular of O'Neill's early plays, largely because it provides a plum role for an actress: a prostitute who returns to her long-lost sea-faring father hating all men, but is eventually reconciled both to him and to her Irish stoker of a lover.

The final curtain finds them prepared to settle into a spectacularly ill-omened menage-a-trois, but for once in his career O'Neill seems to intend no irony. Anna's dad, old Chris, who has blamed their troubles on the malign influence of the ocean, now blesses it. The student may find some interest in the appearance of the symbols of fog and sea O'Neill put to richer use in his last work, and in the occasional unmistakable note of bedrock hopelessness, but for the most part there is only sentimentality and verbal and visual drabness.

Jonathan Lynn's production squeezes as much poetry from the text as may be, and Susan Tracy is generally excellent, foursquare and volatile, as Anna. When she faltered I was inclined to blame the text: O'Neill's notorious tin ear makes things difficult for a British player. You cannot just fall into the rhythms.

Fulton Mackay chuckles endearingly as old Chris (his constant invocations of 'dat ol' davil sea' are less trying to hear than to read, though no more relevant) and Lila Kaye livens up the opening wharfside scene as a precursor of Tugboat Annie.

* * *

Two new plays about police violence. The less successful, though the more ambitious, is Michael Picardie's Shades of Brown (King's Head), in which a South African cop comes to a coloured folk-psychiatrist and, after some preliminary intimidation, asks to be cured from the trauma that has haunted himself and his wife ever since she gave birth to a brown child.

This fanciful confrontation, and the lightness of much of the dialogue, do not really match the author's obvious underlying fury. Mr Picardie's witch-doctor is a charming performance; his patient is the brilliant Antony Sher, whose uncommonly keen nose for absurdity in dialogue or behaviour may have pitched the play further towards comedy than was intended. 'In my job,' he says, 'you need all the laughs you can get.' I know how he feels.

Barrie Keeffe's Sus (Theatre Upstairs) is a dour, economical and compelling piece about a black Briton pulled in, and similarly brutalised, on suspicion of having killed his pregnant wife. It is set on the night of the last election and his two interrogators look forward to the freedom from liberal restraints that they will enjoy under 'the Thatch.' Ann Mitchell's production is effectively low-keyed, though some of the acting may be more expressionless than was intended.

But there are even more frightening problems that Mr Keeffe, like most of his contemporaries, has yet to examine: the moods and attitudes in the British people, not just the police, that might explain why we now have a Conservative Government.
