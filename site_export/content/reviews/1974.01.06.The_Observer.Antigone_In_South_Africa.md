Antigone in South Africa

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Fugard and Sherlock Holmes.

THE liaison between Athol Fugard and the Serpent Players of South Africa's Port Elizabeth is in some respects a strange one.

Mr Fugard is a dramatist of literary stamp, spare in construction, but sometimes over-ripe in expression. He is himself an actor and director, but it is difficult to imagine him working up his plays in collaboration with his casts. Yet this has been precisely his method with the Serpent Players.

There is, in fact, no author named for The Island (Royal Court). We are told it was "devised" by Mr Fugard and his two actors, John Kani and Winston Ntshona. By all accounts the process worked magnificently in Sizwe Bansi is Dead. But in The Island the joins show; it is streakily superb.

Messrs Kani and Ntshona are naturals. This is not to deny their technical skill, but watching them one thinks as little of acting as when watching Laurel and Hardy or Tony Hancock and Sid James. Can you imagine such performers engaged in acting out an existence in South Africa's maximum security political prison? Think for a moment of Mr Fugard's avowed master, Samuel Beckett. The cosmic, comic agonies of Mr Beckett's no-man's-land are those of Mr Fugard's everyday world. And in his two actors he has found or created or sparked off an immaculate Vladimir and Estragon: the primal double-act.

The lineage is particularly apparent when they begin a rehearsal-within-the-play. John and Winston share a cell. They are preparing a performance of the Antigone story for the prison concert, and John, the sharper of the two, has some difficulty in persuading Winston to go through with it.

Winston vents his misgivings in the very accents of Estragon refusing to recognise the advisability of waiting for Godot. He has a legitimate grievance: not only has it been kept from him until the last unguarded moment that Antigone and Creon probably never existed, but his assumption of the heroine's wig and padding has John falling about. Crushed and resentful, Winston foresees an eternity of teasing from the other prisoners. John's attempt to assert that the show must go on is met by a beleaguered rhetorical question: "You call laughing at me theatre?" John now shows himself a masterly persuader. "Let them laugh," he says, "so long as they listen at the end."

The implicit claim made for theatre is a huge one, but this show validates it. At its close we watch the Antigone performance: we see Winston come on stage, self-conscious in drag, and we don't laugh. So all right, we've been conditioned by what we have already seen. But when Creon, the State, sentences Antigone, the conscience of humanity, to be walled up within a rock, upon an island, with just enough food to keep her alive, we assist at the re-activation of a myth. It is possible to sense what the Antigone story may have meant to Sophocles's audience, to Jean Anouilh's during the Occupation, and indeed to Athol Fugard's when The Island was performed in Cape Town.

But between the Antigone rehearsal and performance a longish stretch of play intervenes and it is here that doubts arise. John is told that his 10-year sentence has been commuted; he will be out in three months. Winston, the lifer, seems delighted for him; in the face of such unselfishness we wonder if the play could actually be depicting imprisonment as an ennobling experience. But as John's elation grows (to the point where he asks, self-consciously, for his impending release not to be mentioned in the cell again), jealousy and desperation overcome Winston. Frantically, graphically, he describes the good times in store for his friend; the tirade is all the more bitter because it is with the relation of just such dreams as this that the two have taken it in turns to make life bearable for each other. But there is a difference in texture here which is more than a matter of context.

When John beguiled Winston with a long imaginary phone-call home the words seemed to flow from him, but by this later stage the playwright appears to have taken over. We are into Fine Writing, even momentarily into Fine Acting: good things in their place, but this is not it. We have also been uncomfortably reminded of the inert passages in Mr Fugard's more orthodox plays, though their concomitant sin - a thinness in characterisation - has vanished in the presence of these two players. Mr Ntshona, with his strength and his appalling vulnerability, seems to me marginally the finer of the two, but it is their interaction which we shall remember. This production, in both its style and its substance, is an affirmation of human interdependence (which encompasses human conflict); it takes us down to the roots of drama.

Sherlock Holmes (Aldwych) offers another rich relationship though not, as you might expect, between Holmes and Watson. In this melodramatisation (vintage 1899), adapted from Conan Doyle by the American actor William Gillette (with, naturally, razor sharpness) the good doctor is a shadow of his legendary self, skimpily written and rather callowly played. Holmes's true partner is his greatest adversary, Professor Moriarty. They even, after much brilliant fencing, suffer a similar decline, Moriarty crumbling into custody as bathetically as Holmes dwindles into a husband.

Wreathed in a truly Holmesian fog (regular Aldwych customers might deduce from the smoke that a Shakespearean battle was in progress), the evening proceeds on a rapturous course, in Carl Toms's sets which, for Victorian gusto and mechanical delight, have not been matched since Sean Kenny's for Oliver! (The Stepney gas-chamber is a masterpiece.) Frank Dunlop, a director who usually arouses my admiration and despair in equal proportions, here puts not a toenail wrong. His is not a totally straight-faced production - it begins with a funereal fiddling of the James Bond theme - but it plays a neat game with our responses: the actors' voices may waver ("Not Sherlock Holmes!"), but their eyelids remain in place.

John Wood's vocal technique is so developed that he can switch from burlesque to stern reality within a sentence; he is Holmes to the afterlife. Philip Locke's silky lisp is perfect for Moriarty, and his associates - Nicholas Selby, Barbara Leigh-Hunt, Harry Towb, Trevor Peacock - might have stepped up from Tussaud's basement. Mr Peacock, who plays a safe-breaker (with stethoscope), has a particular aptitude for what I would call mutton-chop acting.

So, though the onset of 1974 leaves me as cold as it does most of you (overcoats are currently de rigueur in theatres) it has, thus far, been a good year in my patch. A piece called Judies (Comedy: place and genre) let down the tone a bit, but we can live with that.
