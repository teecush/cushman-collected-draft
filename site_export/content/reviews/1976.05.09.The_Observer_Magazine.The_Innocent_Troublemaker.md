Lee Remick, like Audrey Hepburn and Shirley MacLaine - is one of those top movie actresses who really wanted to be dancers. As a 12-year-old at school she hated acting - 'a perfectly natural hatred for an awkward growing kid. Those who enjoy it have probably got a lot wrong with them. At that stage dancing, a purely technical discipline, requiring me to use my body but not my personality, was far more attractive.'

She got her first job by lying about her age. 'I was 16, said I was 18, actually looked about 11.' At the end of the season the choreographer confessed that she hadn't been fooled for a moment, but had gone ahead and engaged her anyhow.

Miss Remick, now 40, cool, sensible and obviously able to take care of herself, is as unfairly attractive off the screen as on it. She is married to Kip Gowans, a British assistant film director (living with them are her two teenage children, a girl and a boy, by a previous marriage). She moved to London from the States seven years ago. In that time she has made two incursions into British television: one of Tennessee Williams's neurotic Southerners - the repressed one - in a BBC production of 'Summer and Smoke' four years ago, and the notably unrepressed Lady Churchill, née Jerome, in the ITV serial 'Jennie' which brought her probably her biggest audience. She has also shot the odd film here, including 'The Severed Head'.

In 'Bus Stop', the story of a group of people temporarily stranded in a small café, she plays another Southern heroine, though of a very different kind from Tennessee Williams's Alma, or from the Faulkner girl of 'The Long Hot Summer' whom she played memorably almost at the outset of her film career back in 1957. For 'Bus Stop', this will, surprisingly, be its London premiere, though it is 20 years since it was a hit on Broadway and almost as long since it became one of the most memorable of Marilyn Monroe's screen vehicles. Lee Remick is unlikely to offer anything resembling that performance, though she too has a fondness for 'naive innocent women who create havoc around themselves without realising it'.

She was lucky in being able to break into films without having to break into Hollywood. It happened 15 years ago when Elia Kazan saw her in a television play, and asked her to read the script of 'A Face in the Crowd'. He even asked her what she thought of it. She seems to have thought enough, and well enough, to get the eye-catching part of Andy Griffith's baton-twirling girl-friend. The film had, as she says, 'nothing to do with Hollywood; it was shot in New York and Arkansas' and it afforded her not only 'a splashy entrance - being a dishy kind of sex-pot part' but a prestigious one.

Kazan remained an enormous influence as he has been, she thinks, on almost every actor who has worked for him. 'Actors on other movies are always harking back, or imagining what he might say in that situation.' He directed her again in 'Wild River', her favourite film. But probably the movie in which she made her most decisive impact was directed by Otto Preminger: it was 'Anatomy of a Murder', in which she played the prototype of those wide-eyed trouble-makers. Preminger she found, considering his fearsome reputation, 'amazingly benign; I'd just had a baby so maybe the feminine mystique was in my favour'.

The film was considered immensely daring at the time, though there was nothing indecorous about what was seen in this courtroom picture. Such shock as there was lay in the evidence. (One ruffled British critic said the picture might have been better called 'Anatomy of a Rape'.) She stresses that the girl in 'Long Hot Summer' was sexy and funny and that though 'Wild River' had 'a strong undercurrent of sexual drive, there was no conventional sex-symbol aspect'.

The subsequent move towards sexual explicitness in films leaves her a little cynical: she regards it as largely responsible for what she calls 'a certain paucity of interesting parts for women'. 'It's ironic,' she says, 'that women were more alive in films in the 1940s, years before women's lib, than they are now. All those ridiculous restrictions on sex meant that women had to be shown doing other things. So the Rosalind Russell heroine got to run factories, or work on newspapers. That's why Jennie was so fascinating: a chance to do something more than lie down in bed'.

Robert Cushman
