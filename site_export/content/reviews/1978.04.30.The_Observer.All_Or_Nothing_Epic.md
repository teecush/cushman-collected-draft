All-or-nothing epic

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Ibsen's 'Brand' at the National.

THE fascination of Ibsen's Brand (Olivier) is the impossibility of determining where the ice breaks.

The implacable Norwegian priest in his glacial mountain parish works necessary wonders in stiffening the souls and spines of his flock. He does much the same to his own neck. By the end of the play his vocation has resulted in the deaths of his wife and child and his own exile; stoned by the formerly faithful. A mad girl, finding him broken and bleeding, takes him for Christ. A moment later, in highly symbolic circumstances, he is killed by an avalanche, while a disembodied voice proclaims the God of Love: thus presumably letting Brand know where he went wrong.

We may know where, but how about when? There is no identifiable moment at which beneficent rigour shades over into inhuman presumption. Even at their most repellent, Brand's actions are in some way defensible; the balance of forces is more perfect than in any play since 'The Bacchae.'

That much is theory, and necessarily vague. The practice, unfortunately, is hardly more specific. The programme (the most informative at the National in some time) quotes Ibsen's declaration "that Brand is a priest is really immaterial; the demand of All or Nothing is valid for all aspects of life." Brand, he said, might just as well be "a sculptor or a politician" (as in subsequent plays -- all variants of this one -- he actually became).

This is all very well, but it means that we see very little of Brand, so to speak, actually on the job; he vocalises about his duties and responsibilities, but he never defines them. He calls for sacrifices, but, except when dealing with his immediate family, he refuses to explain not only what they're for, but what they are.

He is in fact the idea of intransigence, and as such quite at home in an epic poem -- which is actually what Ibsen wrote. But since it is in dialogue it is bound to be staged, and, since it is by Ibsen, it can, with the right actor, work. It did so at Hammersmith 20 years ago when Brand was gauntly and explosively played by -- I almost wrote "the late" -- Patrick McGoohan, since his subsequent career has been another tragedy of waste.

But the incumbent pastor is Michael Bryant, whose strengths lie in humanity and understatement. Probably no actor can play Brand without visibly and audibly goading himself, but with Mr Bryant the ends -- which is italic quoting -- fails to justify the means.

His adversaries, the crowd, are largely at the mercy of Ralph Koltai's ingeniously sliding set. When it is, so to speak, at full tilt, the groupings look interesting; on the flat they are painfully limp. The director, Christopher Morahan, staged some good massed scenes in 'State of Revolution,' but that was in the Lyttelton; if the future of our classic theatre lies on vast open stages like the Olivier, our directors will have to acquire new skills. (Or perhaps re-acquire old ones: I write this fresh from reading 'Astonish Us in the Morning,' an inspiring collection of tributes to Tyrone Guthrie, who was both the prophet and the great showman of the open stage, and who left a gap as yet unfilled.)

I have to add to all this the information that the performance lasts close on four hours. However, persevere. There is a salty new translation by Geoffrey Hill, unexpectedly funny (I look forward, after a suitable break for refreshment, to Mr Hill's 'Peer Gynt') and varied in its rhythms, thereby offering fidelity to its source and opportunity -- too seldom seized -- to its interpreters.

There are also good middle-reach performances: Patience Collier makes an ashen irruption as the hero's avaricious mother, and, as the Mayor, Robert Stephens is quite stunning in his cunning. As written, this local politician is vigorous but a type; Mr Stephens, turning all his idiosyncrasies to account, does him full emblematic justice but humanises him as well, giving his author more than he asks but nothing he cannot accommodate. Previous acting versions have dispensed with Ibsen's topical satire; here it seems the most pertinent part of the evening.

* * *

In Prospect's Twelfth Night (Old Vic) Sir Andrew refers to a "dun-coloured stock" instead of a flame-coloured one. He has to, clad as he is in a pair of 1920s ducks from the Ben Travers collection. This woke me up to the fact that this is a modern-dress production; earlier I'd taken it for granted. (It would take something really revolutionary -- a doublet-and-hose, say -- to really jerk me out of my seat.)

You will gather that I know this text too well; were I less jaded I might have got more out of this show, in which the funny bits are done funny and the melancholy bits melancholy, the latter winning out, since the comics are unintuitive while the tragics have on their side Eileen Atkins's wise Viola, Louise Purnell's frightened Olivia and -- as her lugubrious soulmate and our master of ceremonies -- Robert Eddison. He is actually playing the fool, but it is difficult to wish him other than he is. His is the final and the finest moment of the play, when the wind and the rain song ends in "we'll strive to please you every day" and "we" turn out to be the Prospect company promising not only scattered showers but the rest of their season.

* * *

The sisters of Tom McGrath's play at Stratford East are three in number, and they aren't ever going to get to Moscow. Or anywhere else they might want to go. Their hopes are blighted almost before they are expressed. They are East End schoolgirls, only friends really: One Pakistani, one West Indian, one working-class. (Well, of course, they're all working-class but the script's descriptive powers don't run to a third adjective.)

It is the kind of play that seems to have been researched rather than written: lively and apparently accurate; but aimless. When creativity does take over, the results are jarring. Most of the scenes alternate schoolroom and playground; as blackboard jungles go, this is a mild one. The teacher even manages to make himself heard, though the script may have intended more riot than the production achieves. A show whose most convincing moment is a fit of vomiting has surely got its priorities wrong somewhere. Among the cast Tim Stern, in fierce black specs, fetchingly suggests the teenage Harold Pinter (or a teenage Harold Pinter), pauses and all.

* * *

A Cool Million (Open Space, transferred from the Half Moon) is the same kind of theatre in fantasy-time. Based (freely, I imagine) on a Nathanael West novel, it charts the downfall of innocence in a strange region of depression America, somewhere between Mid-West and Deep South. There are bright, sharp moments, mostly occurring in Andy Smith's pastiche songs, wittily and meticulously written and engagingly performed.

In the same category is a monologue, of doubtful provenance, from Michael Irving as an Indian chief who has a gesture for every word, and delivers both simultaneously. Benny Young makes an appealing male ingenue of the hero Lemuel Pitkin, who loses a couple of organs, a few limbs and finally his life in pursuit of the American dream, duped by a Fascist party which finally claims to have saved America from sophistication. On the evidence of this show they have done a thorough job.

Last week, in reviewing 'Henry VI,' I read Bury for Berwick. Obsequious apologies to the burgesses of both towns, to all my readers, and to the entire Royal Shakespeare Company. In self-defence, it sounded like Bury. But I should have looked it up.
