Play of the Month--Look Back in Anger, Sunday 8.45 BBC1

Twenty years ago, Look Back in Anger was first performed in London. It was only the third play ever produced by the English Stage Company at the Royal Court Theatre, and its author was an unknown playwright called John Osborne. But it became a pivotal point in British theatre. This week's special television production stars James Hazeldine as Jimmy Porter, Ciaran Madden as Alison and Neil Daglish as Cliff. Here Robert Cushman discusses the play's impact on contemporary British theatre and pictures take us from the first to the latest production.

Looking back at Look Back in Anger

IT IS a commonplace that the first night of John Osborne's Look Back in Anger 20 years ago at the Royal Court Theatre in London was a landmark. The stretch of theatrical history it opened up is the one in which we are still living.

It was the third production of the English Stage Company, a group set up at the Court with the intention of 'doing new plays': filling a hole that, nearly everyone was agreed, gaped wide. The question of what kind of new plays they should be was not much canvassed. The initial approaches made by the director, George Devine, were to novelists such as Angus Wilson and Nigel Dennis, who might be expected to come up with plays which--though certainly out of line with West End fashion--would be delicate, oblique, satiric in their approach; they would get reviews in which the adjective 'civilised' would feature rather strongly. This at least was true of Wilson's play The Mulberry Bush with which the new company went into business. It had already been done at the Bristol Old Vic, a 'civilised' theatre of the first order. So had the second Court offering, Arthur Miller's The Crucible. Miller, like Wilson, was a name, if not a commercial name.

But nobody had ever heard of John Osborne before 8 May 1956; the day on which the English Stage Company (not a name which ever caught on--from here on I'll call it the Court) staged Look Back in Anger as its third production. From that date, the Court was the theatre for New Dramatists, and the British theatre took its tone from the Court.

New dramatists were, and have remained, what it is all about; and critics gnash their teeth and point their fingers whenever some extravagantly long period of time--a fortnight, say--passes without one appearing. This has, one must admit, been hard on the old dramatists; I mean those still alive and producing. (Death pays all debts.) And of course even a new dramatist grows old, and may find himself unwelcome in his own parish. The Court has been faced with a tricky problem as the years have passed. Should it remain faithful to the authors it has nurtured, or should it cast them out?

For close on 15 years it took the first course; now it seems to have decided on the second. Of Osborne's last three London premières, two have been at Greenwich and one at the National Theatre. The dramatists who, within a couple of years of his debut, had most auspiciously followed him into Sloane Square--Arnold Wesker, John Arden--have likewise been absent. I have even heard it maintained that Edward Bond, whose debut came a full decade after Osborne's, has no right to a place at the Court. That opinion was advanced by one of Bond's greatest admirers, who still felt that the Court should be ashamed of itself harbouring someone so ancient. I honestly don't know why; the place has no particular obligation to youth. There's nothing about youth in its charter. I'm not even sure it has a charter.

Even so, ever since those brave days of '56, it has behaved as though it had: for better and for worse. Critics who objected when the worse predominated were apt to be met with aggrieved incredulity: 'Don't they know that we started a revolution? We've always been terribly brave, it says so in the books.' And, as far as those early days are concerned, the books are right. I doubt if the Court knew when they began rehearsing Look Back the extent to which, in our minds if not always on our stages, things were not to be the same again. I doubt if Osborne himself knew when he started writing.

He has since referred to the play as 'formal and old-fashioned'. But this was its greatest strength; there is nothing about its form which could puzzle its audiences or distract them from its content. It is a three-act naturalistic play which--unlike most examples then successful in London--took the form literally; it was written out of its author's experience, which had not been glamorous. In the West End, a shabby-genteel hotel (as in N. C. Hunter's Waters of the Moon or Terence Rattigan's Separate Tables) was about the limit of imaginable squalor. A serious work usually had a comparably serious setting; Wilson's The Mulberry Bush was set in a university, as was the novel most immediately associated with John Osborne's play, Kingsley Amis's Lucky Jim. Osborne's hero Jimmy Porter has a university background, but we owe this information to his wife; he never mentions it. He never harks back to it, and he never complains about his present poverty--though he complains about a lot of other things.

Kenneth Tynan, the play's most vociferous champion among the critics, says now that for the audiences this was the most disquieting thing about it. An aspiring proletarian hero they could have coped with and recognised. But Jimmy refuses to be patronised. He has no apparent desire to rise to the social level represented by his wife Alison, her Indian Army father and dragon mother; the action of the play consists of his effort to make Alison live and love on his terms. On the first night Tynan spoke to the American dramatist and novelist Irwin Shaw, who said that Jimmy's lack of any wider ambition would doom the play in America: 'Nobody in the States will ever stomach a play about a hero who doesn't want to be a success.'

Shaw was not the only luminary present; unheralded the play may have been, but plainly something was in the air and could be sniffed for miles around. Tynan remembers encountering Amis (who ordinarily never went to the theatre and whose main comment on Jimmy was that since the chap played jazz trumpet he couldn't be all bad). His comment on the play was 'bloody good on language, bit thin on plot'. 'Binkie' Beaumont, the most powerful of West End managers (and one who, like all his colleagues, had turned the play down before it came to rest chez Devine) was present, and had premonitions: 'We may as well,' he was heard to say in the bar, 'roll up the map of the West End.' It seems to have been a night for quotations; Tynan remembers a gross figure moodily and repeatedly proclaiming that 'the rough beast has finally slouched home'. He will now swear it was Evelyn Waugh.

Tynan himself meanwhile was basking in 'the self-righteous glow that comes when you encounter a play that speaks directly to your own temperament'. The following Sunday, in the Observer, he was able, with lasting effect, to communicate that glow to his readership.

He admitted that the play had faults (he mentioned its length and the 'painful whimsy' of its end), but he disposed of them in a sentence. The rest was rhapsody, but trenchant rhapsody; Tynan's contribution to the play's success was less that he liked it better than other critics (he did but not that much) but simply that he wrote about it better. If there was a key-line in his review, it was that the play 'presents post-war youth as it really is'. It is difficult to judge the precise validity of a claim like that, but I am sure that it became truer once he had written it. Post-war youth, having read the review and/or seen the play, set about living up to its image.

Tynan made no detailed political claims for the play; he merely referred to Jimmy's 'instinctive Leftishness' as one of the traits to which he responded. John Glenister, who is directing the play for the BBC, reminded me of one fact--often forgotten: it came just before Suez and Hungary. It reflected a vague discontent that crystallised almost immediately, and of which the Court became one of the intellectual centres, so that the 'instinctive Leftishness' became more programmatic. In successive years a Royal Court banner flourished on the road from Aldermaston--and Osborne marched beneath it.

In the late 50s and early 60s he did his share of snarling at governments, and indeed at oppositions. But then he seemed to snarl at nearly everything. Once he had achieved success he fell with ironically obliging ease into the 'angry young man' stereotype invented for him by the gossip columnists (and incidentally by the Royal Court publicity department). But his personal brand of rhetoric, which has grown increasingly clotted with the years--ever since he wrote Luther, whose constipated hero moved his lips as violently and painfully as his bowels--has never been suited to the articulation of a political viewpoint. It was, and is, impossible to tell just what his famous 1961 'Letter to my fellow countrymen' (with its 'damn you, England' refrain) is meant to be about.

Jimmy Porter's rhetoric, by contrast, is free-wheelingly muscular; it is difficult to see how anyone could have failed to surrender to it from the outset. It is, apart from anything else, so funny. (The most recent London revival, at the Young Vic, had its audience rolling.) The most generalised and, I suppose, most famous declaration in the play--about there being no good causes left--is actually noticeably tacked on to the rest; what seem integral are Jimmy's onslaughts on specific targets. You do not have to be an ideologue to lampoon Tory ladies and the English Sunday, but they are subjects which would raise at least a wry smile, if not a whoop of assent, from any lively and reasonably cynical mind in 1956 (or indeed 1976); Jimmy's style, and his creator's, are very telling that way. I certainly, at the time, recognised and sympathised with the spirit evoked in Tynan's review (at 12 I was into reading reviews rather than seeing plays) and apparently though more hesitantly confirmed in Harold Hobson's piece in the Sunday Times. (Amusing that a play which begins with a riotous put-down of the posh Sundays should so quickly and decisively be enshrined in them.)

Obviously what was going on was wittily iconoclastic, and even I knew that that was a good thing to be. Others felt the same way, including at least one future playwright; Trevor Griffiths, just gone into the army, was also a review-reader rather than a playgoer and he too was convinced by Tynan that something good was beginning to happen, something to which he would later contribute. Some who saw the play itself took quicker action. Arnold Wesker who encountered it in Wimbledon later in the year, went straight home to write Chicken Soup with Barley, itself to become a Court play in 1958. What inspired him was 'something to do with the energy of the characters, a kind of impatience which came through the way they spoke'. He recognised it as an impatience he had expressed in short stories (he too had written about those 'bloody Sunday bells'), and wondered if Osborne 'felt the same way I did'.

I doubt if he did, quite, but perhaps the great attraction of the play at its premiere was its ability to act as a mirror of disaffection for a generation in need of it: you could read your own discontents into it. Jimmy's rages--which are not all the play but which drive it along--certainly give the scope; Osborne himself says of him in a stage-direction that 'to be as vehement as he is is to be almost non-committal'. But events rapidly conspired to give the vehemence a new edge; and before you knew it we had a committed theatre. Twenty years, two subsidised companies, and innumerable fringe outlets later, we still have. Tynan, now ex-Observer and ex-National Theatre, remains the dominant critical influence, and most reviewers still expect a writer to show his political colours. Or else.

Else can be quite stimulating; she even seems to function as Osborne's muse. Reviewers now accuse him of a swing to the Right; they recall the suspicious sympathy accorded the Colonel in Look Back and murmur that they should have known all along. But there has been no betrayal, since there was no real compact in the first place.

One phrase in Tynan's article which most pleased Osborne at the time was 'the determination that no one who dies shall go unmourned'. It is Jimmy's grief for his father and for the old woman who befriended him that gives him distinction in his author's eyes; it is Alison's loss of her baby that, according to the play's logic, finally makes her worthy of him. Death, as in a Jacobean tragedy, is the fount of honour--but this time for the living as well. The idea of an aristocracy of feeling is constant throughout Osborne's plays right down to his latest, Watch It Come Down. If he shows his chosen characters disdaining the outside world for no well-stated reason, he is honest enough to show them savaging each other as well. If we look back to Look Back we can find this double level already there; Jimmy's tirades may be detached from their context to be relished or detested according to taste (though the detestation, I think, has largely died out), but they are also part of his pursuit of Alison. The wit refuses to date (actually it's pleasant to be reminded of jazz and milk-bars) and the personal relationships seem more convincing in each new production; even the ending, with Jimmy and Alison playing at bears and squirrels, seems truthful enough to me. John Glenister, who directs the television production, describes the play as a love story, and he is surely right.
