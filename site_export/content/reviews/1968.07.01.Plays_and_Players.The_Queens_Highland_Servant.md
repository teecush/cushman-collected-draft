The Queen's Highland Servant

I would hazard, gentle reader, that you get most of your kicks on the subsidised circuit and that theatreland for you is arc-shaped, extending down to Waterloo Road, over the bridge and round to the Aldwych. Do you, I wonder, ever cast fleeting glances of guilty longing up the Strand, Trafalgarwards? Well cheer up, you're not missing much. For before you have a chance to succumb to the Siren Song of *Charlie Girl* you must negotiate Scylla and Charybdis. On second thoughts, let's jack the classical bit in and come home to Bunyan for metaphors; I hereby rechristen the Adelphi the Slough of Despond, and name the Savoy and the Vaudeville twin projections of Doubting Castle.

Of late, Giant Despair has been having a ball—indeed, two balls—one being *The Imaginary Invalid* imported from Billingham (Where? Oh yes, of course, Billingham) and the other a morel of Victoriana from William Douglas-Home. When a crime has been committed you look for a motive, so here goes: Molière obviously counts as a revival and we all know about revivals, don't we? And the importation from Windsor (where presumably it counted as théâtre vérité) of *The Queen's Highland Servant* only makes sense as an attempt to cash in on the loyalist reaction to Edward Bond's *Early Morning*. Victoria has now been led out of the house of Bondage back into her ancestral stately Home; where were you all on that cold, empty second night?

John Brown, the faithful ghillie, is here seen pouring copious draughts of whisky down his sovereign's throat, and telling braw home truths about her and her ministers. She liked it (“You're such a comfort, Brown”)—they didn't. This point is reiterated at some length; of twelve scenes, about eight are built on a recurring pattern. The politicos chatter among themselves about the distressing effect of Brown on Queen and country; to them enter Brown who puts them to shame by talking to them as man to dog. First time round this is quite funny. Thereafter Brown is a bore, unsalvageable by any, except possibly Marianne Faithfull.
Due to his excessive zeal in chasing the Queen's enemies and detractors round the castle (what wouldn't he have done to Edward Bond?), Brown eventually catches his death. This is in Act Three Scene Two but both Queen and play proceed for two more scenes and become much sprightlier, and not above tossing the odd epigram. But up until the mouldering of John Brown's body, Victoria is not really much fun. She must, she tells us, always have a man to look up to, which is all very credible but not on her own lips. And however smoothly he may rationalise the relationship, Mr Home seems unable to dramatise it. For twenty whole years it stands still and inert. And platonic. Mr Home has, in fact, created a new genre—the royal non-scandal—but one, I fear, of limited fascination to any who do not bring an obsession with Victoria into the theatre with them. Others may find themselves mentally constructing a spicier play, tentatively entitled *The Balmoralists*.

For acting and production there can be nothing but praise, so let there be nothing.
