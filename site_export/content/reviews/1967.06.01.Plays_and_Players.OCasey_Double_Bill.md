O'Casey Double Bill

Most reviews of this double-bill have ignored the curtain-raiser and it is not difficult to see why. *A Pound on Demand* is a blown-up revue sketch about the antics of two drunks in a post office which only the exotic tag of Irishness could possibly sell to an English audience. The writing presents the depressing spectacle of a first-rate comic talent at third-rate pressure; a piece of genuine mediocrity would be easier to accept, but the continual unavailing strain towards a higher level of whose existence author and audience are thoroughly aware is really punishing. If only the name attached were not O'Casey's there wouldn't have been so earnest an effort to laugh; as it is, all one can salvage is the memory of some virtuoso alcoholic sliding from Jack MacGowran.

He also reigns supreme in *The Shadow of a Gunman*, which nobody needs telling is a much better play. All the same, it adds to the creeping suspicion of the last few years that during his long period in the wilderness O'Casey was, paradoxically, oversold. I can only go on the plays I've actually seen, since phrasing that on the page appears overripe has always to be read with the mental proviso that it probably sounds marvellous in a Dublin brogue, and the sudden changes of gear on which his structures are based demand to be fleshed. But neither the National Theatre *Juno* nor the present production raise him above the “rather good” level; not to be sneered at, but not to be lauded either. The myth of his verbal genius needs careful examination. It holds good for some magnificent passages of sustained comedy (in this vein the jeep sequence in *The Bishop's Bonfire* seems to me to surpass anything in the acknowledged major parts) but the corresponding tragic effects are willed rather than created; instance the twice-repeated maternal lament in *Juno*: high-sounding verbiage to pressure-cook a situation. In *The Shadow of a Gunman* the shock effect of Minnie Powell's death is blanketed, not intensified, by the vaporous rhetoric with which the poseur Davoren greets it; and I don't think there's a dramatic point here—at this point, as far as one can tell, he has stopped posing.

Still, it's a sturdy play in which most of the elements add up; the Civil War appears by refraction in Davoren and the superstitious ex-patriot Seamus Shields, and then in grim earnest in the catastrophe to which their antics and their neighbours' war-obsession lead. But at the Mermaid the whole falters for lack of life in the parts. The episode of Mr Gallagher's petition drags laboriously, so that its eventual effect on the denouement is obscured; the loquacious drunk who should heighten the tension of the second half fatally dissipates it.

Through it all, Jack MacGowran perseveres with his usual ferrety expertise, though without ever reaching the heights of his recent work in Beckett. Seamus is no immortal but he's a sharp and funny creation and an obvious star part. Unfortunately, he's not the protagonist; and Davoren, who is, is in fact only the shadow of a character—his not being a gunman is the most positive thing about him. Brian Phelan lacks the exceptional magnetism that might hold the stage in such a wholly passive rôle. But Shivaun O'Casey, though rather too predestined a martyr, gives a haunting account of Minnie, and Terry Downes, no less, makes a most convincingly brutal Auxiliary, though the relationship to what is usually known as acting is remote.

O'Casey is an easy dramatist to love. I wish I were convinced.
