THEATRE

Acting your age

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Turgenev at the National

ACCORDING to their creators Iago was 28 and Hedda Gabler 29. To these little-known dramatic facts -- unscrupulously concealed by generations of middle-aged actors and their admirers -- may be added a third: Ivan Turgenev swears that Natalya Petrovna, protagonist of A Month in the Country (Olivier), was exactly the same age as Miss Gabler.

Thus she is played by Francesca Annis in Peter Gill's new production and the effect is most instructive. Natalya, the wife of a rich Russian landowner, lives in perfect comfort; not only is her husband devoted to her, though inclined to neglect her for the management of his estate, but she has a resident platonic lover to be alternately teased and cultivated. She then falls in love with her son's tutor, a youth of 21.

Or at least she thinks she does. Maybe she fools herself and this is how Miss Annis seems to be playing her. What happens is that Natalya sees the tutor playing at kites with her 17-year-old ward, and feels jealous. (Miss Annis tellingly signals the onset of emotion by gazing fixedly at the spot where the young couple have lately stood.) They make her feel her age, and she complains to her standby, Rakitin, about what fogies the two of them are becoming.

The joke of course is that she is still presentably young; from these fabricated fears springs a fabricated passion, one she believes she owes it to herself to feel. Jealous of her ward Vera, she plots to have her married off to a local buffoon; and, by one of the play's numberless ironies, though she gives up on the plan, it still goes through. Exit Vera; exit Rakitin whose own passion has been exposed in the light generated by the new one; exit young Belyaev, scared and flattered by the emotions he has stirred and which he does not reciprocate; exit the indispensable match-making doctor, in tandem with the household governess. Natalya is left alone with her husband, which may not be the worst thing that could happen to her, though at curtain-fall this is understandably not how she sees it.

So we have a labyrinthine comedy of self-deception, meticulously plotted by Mr Gill, whose gift for clarifying emotional intricacies is almost unmatched among British directors. Our rewards are a good deal of hard-headed laughter and a silvery high-comic display, almost in the French tradition, from Miss Annis. Turgenev ceases to look like a flawed test-run for Chekhov, and comes on instead like a late Marivaux.

That at least is one way of looking at it. If Natalya, in however compromised a fashion, really does love Belyaev then Miss Annis, except for a penultimate tearful sequence in which she abandons vocal arabesques, is giving an intolerably artificial performance, all trembles and vibrato. (And it must be said for that view that three and a half hours is a long time to watch an imaginary passion.)

Otherwise the virtues and vices of Mr Gill's staging are pretty clear-cut. He holds the attention firmly through a play in which six duologues can seem to be doing the work of three; plainly his actors have been encouraged to think (rehearsals must have been fascinating, they should sell tickets) and they excel at sustaining and varying tensions while addressing each other from across huge expanses of stage.

His greatest weakness is that he undercasts: not so much in terms of talent as of temperament. Michael Gough's doctor is a charming sketch, rather than a full-blown portrait of shady opportunism. Vera (Caroline Langrishe) and Belyaev (Ewan Stewart) begin pleasantly but become stolid under pressure. I liked Nigel Terry's unforced self-contempt as Rakitin, and thought Robert Swann superb as the patient puzzled spouse. The translation is by Isaiah Berlin, and I can't think of a word to say for or against it (in any language) -- which is probably to its credit.

None of these performances actually distort the pattern.

* * *

The effect of seeing John Kani and Winston Ntshona in Waiting for Godot (Old Vic) is considerably more complicated. The curtain goes up on two black actors in a conceivably South African wasteland, and one thinks, well why not? They are confronted by the bullying Pozzo, played white, and the expected resonances are heard, though their effect is soon dissipated.

When Pozzo returns blind, to be alternately tormented and patronised, the situation becomes altogether more intriguing; as it does when our heroes vent their spleen on the helpless poor white, Lucky. And no Estragon can ever have compared in poignancy or point with Mr Ntshona when it comes to the line about being beaten up by the same gang as usual. Colour helps at moments, can be ignored at others; and incidentally is most useful in lending new dimensions of pomposity and fear to Bill Flynn's Pozzo.

It is the actual acting of Messrs Kani and Ntshona that disappoints; they have immense presence but little range. Mr Kani can work wonders -- of ingratiation or scorn or both combined -- with his gaping smile but can do little without it; at least he seldom tries. He has a marvellous commonsensical unanswerable inflection for the reiterated title line, but more often the actors' speech rhythms deaden those of the text. Jokes get lost and so, as a logical result, does the full impact of the play's despair: a despair that I take to be unanswerable since it takes full and sympathetic note of the ways in which we spend our time, and pronounces them all irrelevant in the face of darkness. We do things, it says, to give ourselves the impression we exist.

The first British critic to pounce on that line, incidentally, was Kenneth Tynan, whose 1955 Observer notice said everything important about the play. I mention this because it is now generally assumed (for example by Penelope Gilliatt speaking at Tynan's memorial service) that Beckett was one of Tynan's blind-spots. On the contrary: Tynan (unlike his rival Harold Hobson who has never stopped patting himself on the back for it) may have little instinctive sympathy with Beckett, but that made his criticism all the more rigorous -- far more than it was with Brecht whom he thought he liked.

* * *

Bernard Krichefski's A Perfect Retreat (Bush, closing tonight) takes two common themes -- a right-wing takeover of the English Government and lack of communication within a middle-class family -- and combines them to effect. Father, mother, son and daughter each have a strategy for resisting the cataclysm: what is more they all love each other but there is no way that love will involve trust. By the end of the evening -- especially at the end of the evening, with the shadows lengthening over the alfresco dinner table -- Mr Krichefski, though in excessively clipped dialogue, has said something.
