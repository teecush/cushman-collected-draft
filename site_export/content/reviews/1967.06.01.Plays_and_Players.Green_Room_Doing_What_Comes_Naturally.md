Considering what a bleak year for new plays was 1966, 1967 has, to this still early date, been remarkably productive. We have, in quick succession, been offered *Happy Family*, *Benito Cereno* and *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead*, all way ahead of any of last year’s offerings. So, come to that, was *The Daughter-in-Law*, which might squeeze in on a legal quibble as the oldest new play in captivity, a quibble which anyway enables me to pay passing tribute to a fine and—in these pages—undervalued performance by Judy Parfitt as the daughter-in-law herself.

That the Lawrence and the Lowell have both come and gone so rapidly is, I suppose, in the expected nature of things; yet neither of them had the look of West End transfers, and one just hopes that their limited public got to the Royal Court and the Mermaid in time. They complement each other as examples of what the literary mind can do in the theatre in despite of the shrieks that go up there at the mere mention of the idea. Or any idea. And while *Benito Cereno* ties in very well with the current obsession with imagery, ritual, and anything involving Negroes, *The Daughter-in-Law* has all the fascination of a sport. We’ve been told non-stop for years—if not decades—that naturalism is dead—the cry goes back so far in fact that it’s a wonder naturalism ever got born. For a demonstration of the perniciousness of the doctrine take a look at Arnold Wesker, who was informed ad nauseam that the success of *Roots* lay in its climax and that the rest was just dreary documentary padding. His subsequent plays, earnestly attempting to implement all this good counsel, have got progressively thinner, and a revival of *Roots* itself has confirmed what should have been obvious all along: that the first two acts are fascinating, and that the close is—well, rootless; acceptable only if you add a layer of irony the author pretty obviously never intended. Nobody denies that naturalism can easily become pedestrian, but *The Daughter-in-Law* was a welcome reminder that it also has considerable reserves of motor-power, and the strength of Peter Gill’s production lay largely in its exploitation of the fact. I can see why Frank Marcus felt slightly guilty about his enthusiastic response to the detailed actuality of the presentation, but I am sure he had no need to be. The intimacy of the Royal Court gave the occasion the intensity of good television. I’m not being flippant; of all the media television has at the moment the highest *overall* acting standard. (Individual peaks are another matter.) The newest generation of actors and audiences are geared to intimacy; take that smirk off your face, you know perfectly well what I mean. They are likely to be happiest in theatres of Royal Court dimensions; for a superlative example go and see Glenda Jackson in *Three Sisters*. This is written before the official first night, and it can stand whatever the critics decide. If she pulls off more like this one when she returns to the arena (Aldwych-on-Avon) she is one of the half-dozen with whom the action will reside.

Footnote: Since the above the notices have appeared. Most of them have damned Glenda with faint praise, and themselves, in my eyes, with her. I still think she’s a better actress than Marianne Faithfull.

## The rules of the road

Though it is reviewed elsewhere in this issue I want, while in the giving vein, to salute *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead*. I wished, which is extraordinary, that it would go on for ever. I should think it will as a matter of fact; it was a virtual sell-out even before the reviews, and this, coupled with the relative ease with which you can get into *Much Ado About Nothing*, the National’s current pop classic, constitutes a surprising reversal of the established order. Which order has taken its toll at the St Martin’s where the fourth of my admired plays, Giles Cooper’s *Happy Family*, has been withdrawn to allow an untrammelled passage to Lonsdale’s *On Approval*. So ends Peter Bridge’s attempt to establish a repertory in the West End. One can’t blame an unsubsidised manager for abiding by the rules of the road/avenue but Mr Bridge’s statement—‘It has proved financially impossible to run two plays in repertory in a theatre of this size when one of them is not playing to capacity business’—leaves questions in the air. Would the Cooper play in fact have stood a better chance had it been presented on its own? Would the houses it attracted have carried it had the management not been involved in the overheads of a rep system? Although with only two plays—single set, small cast, and 75 per cent the *same* cast at that—those overheads are surely not enormous, in spite of high rates being paid to technical staff. As a critic, one pays lip-service to the priority of new plays over revivals; as a playgoer, with a limited budget for theatre tickets, one usually plumps for the revivals in the expectation of value for money. A thing must be worth doing, runs the logic, if it’s worth doing twice. But this particular case, of a new work of genuine originality leaving the field to an established rep standby, is bound, however blameless and inevitable, to induce murky reflection. In fact, the more blameless and inevitable, the murkier.

## I should care

One of the most heart-warming television interludes of recent months was the spectacle of William Gaskill and Milton Shulman being studiously sweet to each other on *Late Night Line-Up* while discussing the Evening Standard Drama Awards. I’d like you though to wrench your mind back beyond the reassuring bonhomie to their earlier horn-locking confrontation on *The Look of the Week*. The third participant on that occasion was Harold Hobson, who was asked by Robert Robinson whether he was ever tempted to disguise his exact feelings about a play for the sake of journalistic effectiveness. Not, notice, whether he ever succumbed to the temptation, but just whether he ever experienced it. The answer was a decisive no; Mr Hobson sounded indignant at the very idea. I was amazed, and have since become more so that nobody has taken him up on it. No wonder critics are so resented if the myth of their infallibility is so deeply ingrained.

For myself, I think it’s time to make confession. The temptation to sacrifice an honest opinion for the sake of a snappy line, or even (more insidiously) for an orderly arrangement of paragraphs is an obvious one, and, like most obvious temptations, easily resisted. Integrity feels so much nicer. Anyway, a strongly-felt position is not so easily come by as to be wantonly thrown away by anyone but a fool. But here lies the real problem. Most plays provoke a mixed reaction, and rendering a scrupulous account of the mixture is an extremely delicate operation. The reaction very often takes place on an instinctual level; if these instincts are to be transformed into criticism they must be intellectualised, and this process frequently involves semi-conscious distortion. Nobody bothers to turn white into black, but every critic must at times have juggled with the various shades of grey at his disposal. Lingering doubts get suppressed, rationalised out of existence, so that a coherent argument can be marshalled. Prose, especially journalistic prose, is rarely subtle enough to meet all the demands made upon it.

Not only do I not always know precisely what I think about a play. Sometimes I don’t even care. One of the major pleasures of doing only occasional drama criticism is the marvellous sense of freedom experienced in going to a play with no responsibilities attached. In such circumstances, I always find myself recoiling in horror from the possibility of having to review it. The plays I go to see off my own bat always turn out to be the unreviewable ones. (So do the others, but not quite as often or as irrevocably.) I’d hate to have to analyse the blank indifference—it was nothing as positive as boredom—that possessed me as I emerged from, say, *After the Rain*. And yet I know that, if pressed, I could have made out a good case for or against it without much straining my conscience either way. Many reviewers will tell you that they’re never really sure of their opinion till they get it down on paper. And then, they’re trapped by it.

‘Convictions,’ as Nietzsche said, ‘are prisons: they exclude from life the fun and doubt of flexibility.’ Early Tynan. I hope he still stands by it.

## Time after time

Kenneth Tynan provides a convenient cue for the next object which is a theatre programme. Yes, that old thing. At the National it comes under his jurisdiction and splendidly has he acquitted himself in this regard. You can still get a good read at the Mermaid, too, though the programmes there aren’t as bulky or as handsome as they used to be, and the noble free distribution idea took a fatal tumble a couple of years back.

In the West End things were presumed to be looking up with the arrival on the scene of *Playbill*, the monthly magazine that American playhouses have been serving up to their patrons for years. So now, if you go more than once a month and still retain enough of your childhood innocence to want to know the names of the cast, you have the dubious privilege of paying several times over for the same collection of star interviews and good eating hints. The mixture is admittedly much the same in the States but at least they don’t charge for it over there.

*Playbill* in fact, on the terms we have it in this country, is a badly misfired idea. The contents appear dedicated to the propagation of general amnesia; no suggestion is permitted that the theatre might have changed in any way since the mid-fifties. Well, all right, it probably hasn’t as much as the cheer-leaders would have us believe; we could do with any amount of scepticism about the great revolution. But that’s not to be found in the scented slush that has characterised the last few issues of *Playbill*. That the Royal Shakespeare, who dispense magnificent glossies at Stratford, has to allow their Aldwych offerings to be thus chattily bedecked is particularly disheartening. Maybe it’s just sour grapes at not being able to afford the restaurants so tantalisingly catalogued each month by Anthony Blond but I can’t believe that the theatre is doing itself any service by perpetuating within its own precincts its traditional reputation of a leisurely machine for bridging the gaps between meals. Take away the tinsel sophistication and behold the stage’s answer to the *ABC Film Review*—minus the cheesecake.

These thoughts were partially prompted by a rumour that the National and Sadler’s Wells were to combine on an equivalent publication, presumably geared to subsidised audiences. The National have denied any substance to the rumour, which is a relief. Their programmes for individual plays, coupled with free cast-sheets, are among their most valuable public-relations weapons, and it would be a severe blow to their image, and the goodwill of their public—certainly of this member of their public—if they were to alter the format to that of an all-purpose monthly. As one who likes to revisit productions I’ve enjoyed first time round I find it a great relief to be able to ascertain cast-changes without having to buy the full bit all over again.
