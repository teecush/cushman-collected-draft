Back to the Fifties

Theatre

by Robert Cushman

Plays of the 1950s seem further from us at the moment than plays of the 1930s. Depression chic did a great deal for last year's revival of Clifford Odets's Rocket to the Moon. Cold war chic, however, has yet to happen; and without it Odets's later work, The Country Girl (Apollo), looks a little forlorn.

The director, Robin Lefevre, punctuates it with the moody sounds of Judy Garland and Mel Torme; and that helps a bit. This is a play about actors, and it starts on a bare audition stage; the fire escape and the step-ladder of John Byrne's design sum up a whole phase of American drama, from the Group Theatre to West Side Story; and that helps a bit more. The actors help a lot; and Odets puts up a respectable fight on his own account. There is lots of good detail but not much centre.

The Country Girl was first seen here in 1952 under the imposingly meaningless title of Winter Journey; its original name is another kind of problem. The country girl is the protective wife of Frank Elgin, an ex-star lured back on to the stage and off the bottle by a determined young director. It is a three-cornered personal drama with no social resonance. You may see in Frank the plight of Odets himself, the former committed playwright now sweating it out in Hollywood, sob sob; but this thesis is more help when writing a revue than when watching a play. The play catches our author at an awkward moment, between the passion of Golden Boy and the enjoyable self-pity of The Big Knife: plays which, not having seen them, I imagine to be more revivable.

The people talk in well-rounded, well-aimed phrases from which the salt of slang has disappeared, leaving only swagger. Odets has a craftily delayed revelation about the Elgins' marriage that works very well the first time you see the play. This, though, was my second; and I got frustrated at being denied a good, hard look at the relationships. If the wife is the centre of the play she is a curious one; Hannah Gordon's performance is strong on long-suffering nobility but the director takes her for a destructive shrew and all one can respond is, funny, you don't look shrewish. The battle for Frank's soul never really happens; the play subsists on wilfully engineered misunderstandings.

Martin Shaw is the boy genius metteur-en-scene if not to the life than at least to the accepted convention; and John Stride does very well as Frank. The accent sits so lightly on him that I actually started hoping for one of those transatlantic rewrites explaining that Frank had come to the States after a successful career at the Old Vic; but his silver-maned golden-toned performance is otherwise a fine blend of disgust, egomania and burgeoning professional excitement. Supports are okay; Michael Perceval as a stage manager has an actor's dream line, telling off a producer for being rude to his performers.

* * *

Cut and Thrust (Drill Hall), devised and directed by Robyn Archer, is a hybrid of cabaret, anthology and revue. Songs, poems and monologues are tidily grouped according to target: the Tories, the bourgeoisie, capitalism, liberalism, unemployment, racism, war (with special reference to the Falklands), imperialism, the bomb, men, the Tories.

Do you detect a certain predictability? Listening for two and a half hours to the strident proclamation of opinions with which I mostly agree, I began to wish that I didn't. Shared anger can keep you going for five minutes in the theatre; after that you need something else. In a play you would ask for conflict; in a revue you would settle for irony or at least a few good jokes. Most of the audience were getting too high on hate to notice, but Cut and Thrust (a misnomer for a show that is mostly bludgeon) has a very low laugh-count.

The pandering is shameless: whether to shared masochism ("middle class" treated as an automatic term of abuse by people to whom it applies on both sides of the curtain) or shared prejudice. A Mrs Thatcher impersonation is assumed to be funny in itself. This is sloppy as comedy, implicitly misogynous, and horribly simplistic; since it treats the Prime Minister as a cause rather than a symptom. They even seem to be blaming her for the advent of AIDS and herpes.

Complexity comes as an occasional blessing, notably in a piece written by Miss Archer herself about the impotence of the floating voter, and in a rendition of the Coward masterpiece There are bad times just around the corner, an apolitical song, going on reactionary, here turned effortlessly against itself. It's unkillably hilarious whatever you do to it. I got specialised joy out of raids on two neglected American musicals: How Now Dow Jones deals satire on Wall Street, which is senselessly and half-heartedly anglicised presumably on the assumption that the audience are incapable of making the connections for themselves; and from Jamaica there is an anti-atomic calypso with coruscating E. Y. Harburg lyrics and an electric arrangement by which Harold Arlen, the composer, might be surprised but not I think displeased. It is wonderfully sung, in Carmen Miranda gear, by Noni Hazlehurst, a small and perfectly controlled dynamo equally magnetic when up in song or down in soliloquy. There are reasons for seeing the show, and she is most of them.

* * *

I write this from Dublin, now halfway through its festival. I have seen only one play but there was a lot of it; Thomas Murphy's The Gigli Concert, at the Abbey, goes on for three and a half hours with only three characters which beats even O'Neill for gall.

It belongs to the Physician, cure thyself genre pioneered by Equus: a dynamatologist (you heard) claims to turn people into what they want to be, but of course cannot work the trick for himself. To him comes a burly soak who wants to sing like Gigli; he is magnificently played by Godfrey Quigley, whose rapt fantasies are so spellbinding that one feels cheated when they turn out to have been just a passing phase. His therapist, meanwhile, is played by Tom Hickey with a hop, skip and a jump and many of them; this gets wearing after three hours, or indeed after three minutes, but it is not his fault that the writer has failed to line the characters up properly.

Last week, raving over Glengarry Glen Ross, I inadvertently and inexplicably failed to mention Karl Johnson. He plays the kingpin role of the office manager, and his quiet implacability, gradually revealed, is one of the major attractions of the evening.
