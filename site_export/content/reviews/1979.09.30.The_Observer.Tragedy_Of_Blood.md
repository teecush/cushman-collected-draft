Tragedy of blood

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Nottingham and London.

JOHN MARSTON wrote a series of comedies with -- even for the seventeenth century -- a notably high scurrility content.

That is the side of Marston that we have known, insofar as we have known him at all. But now Nottingham Playhouse has given us the chance to see what he made of the tragedy of blood. Peter Barnes has clapped together Antonio and Mellida and its sequel, "Antonio's Revenge," and called the result Antonio. There are two rival dukes, Andrugio and Piero. Andrugio is good and Piero bad; or at least Piero's vicious qualities have greater scope, since he has defeated his enemy in battle and is now intent on killing both him and his son Antonio, currently disguised -- with an eye to safety and theatrical effect -- as an Amazon.

In the end father and son save their skins by appearing at court and shaming Piero into repentance. Antonio is even allowed to marry Piero's daughter Mellida, whom, naturally, he has loved all along. "Here," proclaims Antonio, "ends the comic crosses of true love."

He's right in a way. In Part Two tragedy -- or at any rate mayhem -- takes over. Piero begins by murdering the resident honest man (you can tell he's honest because he dresses badly and insults everybody), stringing up the corpse in his daughter's chamber, and accusing her of inconstancy. He then bumps off Andrugio, seduces his widow and forces Antonio into a second disguise: this time as a fool.

From this privileged position Antonio organises a conspiracy of more or less virtuous malcontents, who set on Piero at a banquet, extract his tongue and kill him. Along the way there have been minor murders and much girding at the rottenness of court life, as exemplified by a large supporting cast of gulls, degenerates, lechers and whores.

Marston was much mocked by his contemporaries for his rumbustious, neologistic style, but to us, coming to him from Shakespeare, he gives an impression of weightlessness. There is plenty of dirt in his writing, but no solid earth. The people are largely indistinguishable, all actions are liable to be undercut by a burst of sententious satire. You might take the piece to be a parody of Jacobean tragedy; certainly Antonio's situation echoes Hamlet's, but the great sanguinary writers, Webster and Tourneur, came after Marston. He is presenting us with instability for its own sake. This is a good bad-dream play.

Mr Barnes, in his adaptation and his production (co-directed with Geoffrey Reeves), serves his author wonderfully well. He has excellent technical back-up: designer Trevor Pitt supplies a simple, menacing set (a tilted golden disc overhung by a huge crown) and ornately bizarre costumes; choreographer Stuart Hopps sets the whole play moving like a masque; and composer Carl Davis fills it with music that -- in and out of ghost scenes -- truly haunts.

Mr Barnes has gone along with some of his author's strangest fancies -- letting Antonio and Mellida, for example, conduct their reconciliation scene entirely in Italian -- and they all seem to work. Though the overall tone is light, he plays less for laughs than I had expected; he cuts for example the assassins' dangerous declaration that, with their work now finished, they will retire to a monastery. The play remains enigmatic, but it seems to know its own mind, even if we don't.

The actors too, considering that they cannot play characters (since Marston didn't give them any and admits as much in his prologue) but can only play a style, are remarkably sure of themselves. The dominant performance comes from Christopher Ettridge as Piero, with his sickly usurper's smile. Alan Rickman, carefully patting his single Amazonian breast or swinging in on a rope and in motley, is a first-rate sardonic Antonio, and Derek Godfrey is manfully the butt finally metamorphosed (to his great relief, I suspect) into an avenger.

Just to straighten various records: this is (a) the best excursion into the Jacobean funeral parlour since the RSC's fabled "Revenger's Tragedy," (b) Nottingham's finest hour since "Comedians," (c) by far Mr Barnes's outstanding theatrical achievement to date, and (d) the most notable rep production in ages.

Marston's master -- indeed the model for the entire Elizabethan school of death -- was the Roman Seneca, from whose Thyestes he literally took a cue. "You cannot say you have avenged a crime/Unless you better it," says Atreus, the kingly villain of this piece, and Piero quotes him approvingly. Atreus avenges himself on his brother Thyestes by serving him up a pie of which the vital ingredients are his own sons.

* * *

Seneca may have lacked Marston's theatrical flair (and certainly his humour) in dealing with such an episode, but his rhetoric, even in translation, is superior. The problem is what to do while speaking the stuff, and the Company of Three (operating now at the Fountains Abbey pub in Paddington) fall back on the outmoded idea of turning every possible or impossible scene into an orgy: a small one, since there are only five in the cast. But the play takes hold; it has more to say to us than Marston about the ethics of tyranny. And it is frankly a relief to see the homicidal maniacs who people Greek mythology stripped of Attic dignity.

* * *

Ecstasy (Hampstead), Mike Leigh's latest improvised work, is as expected highly polished and -- as acted, or rather inhabited by, its cast of six -- flawlessly believable. At its centre is a woman alone in a London bedsitter with no prospects. She doesn't like the men she picks up, she says, and they don't like her; they just hit her.

The play is at its best in strong statements like that or in moments of silence. But it goes awry in a long sequence of joyless conviviality when three old friends descend on her for a grisly approximation of a night out. While it goes on, she is going to pieces. That much is powerfully conveyed, but it could be done more economically in a written play.

* * *

All the same, it can make a play like Men's Beano (Warehouse) seem obsolete. I am probably being unfair to the author, Nigel Baldwin, whose script is well observed and reasonably entertaining. But the territory he shows us here -- the desperate companionship of males in a dead-end world -- has been thoroughly explored before.

This saga of a trip to the seaside by minibus (how did they get it up the Warehouse's stairs?) is "Factory Birds" on wheels. The men pick up a female hitch-hiker and spend most of their time trying to make her. At the end she rebukes them for their sexism -- which would be fine if the author hadn't got most of his mileage earlier on from showing it off. The mixture is not too far from the pious sensationalism usually attributed to the News of the World. The best acting, with the best material, is Cheryl Hall's.

* * *

The revived Hello Dolly! (Drury Lane) is the loudest non-rock musical I have ever heard. The amplification strips the last vestige of humanity from the gravelly squawk that is the hallmark of Carol Channing's Dolly. She sells herself to the audience relentlessly; her performance is one long curtain-call. She is naturally irresistible in the title-number, which, however, puts paid to the rest of the show: after that gorgeous walk, about everything else is an anti-climax.

But there are pleasant numbers early on: the jolly "Put on Your Sunday Clothes" and "Dancing," which suddenly exposes the lyrical side of Gower Champion's choreography. Otherwise, this chocolate-box show is surprisingly short on romantic feeling, though Maureen Scott and Tudor Davies work attractively with what there is. Eddie Bracken's merchant of Yonkers is nobody's curmudgeon, but it is nice to have him back, even if it isn't quite where he belongs.
