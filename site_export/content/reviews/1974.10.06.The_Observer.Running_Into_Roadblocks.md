Running into roadblocks

BEYOND the motorway The Freeway (Old Vic) lies in wait. By the uncertain future date at which Peter Nichols's new play is set, motorways (together with what are described with incredulous contempt as 'the old A roads') have been virtually abandoned by the progressive motorist; those who dare to use them are liable to become the prey of 'marauding lorry-drivers.'

The freeway, F1, which apparently runs the length of the country, north to south, is, nearly everyone agrees, the only way to travel. Such a consensus is, of course, liable to result in fearful traffic-jams, but probably, in the normal course of events, these would be philosophically accepted. Still, the particular jam which is the subject of this play would tax any philosopher; it stretches over 80 miles, and it lasts for three days.

It is also--and this is perhaps the most sinister thing about it--a symbol. In this it recalls Mr Nichols's earlier play, The National Health. Both titles set off reverberations which the author has been unable to resist exploiting, but which he has only sketchily followed through. Early on in the present piece someone speaks of 'the Free Way,' tentatively breaking up the syllables as if testing them for their weight-bearing capacity. By the end of the evening the metaphor is full-blown: a Minister of Movement has been brought on stage to inform the trapped and indignant travellers that the freeway is, in effect, their way of life. The dominion of the motor-car is what they voted for; if they wanted freedom, they now have (traffic conditions permitting) total liberty to drive to Scotland, the rest of Britain having been reduced to a gigantic by-pass.

The metaphor is neater than that of The National Health and I, a snug non-driver, found it reasonably convincing. It is not, though, dramatically interesting. There is no passion behind Mr Nichols's vision of the future, no real point of view, and not even very much indignation: just a generalised cynicism. It was possible during long passages of The National Health to forget the play's schematic pretensions and appreciate Mr Nichols's best features: his ear for dialogue (fully as acute as Harold Pinter's), his eye for realistic detail, his nose for character. An uncommitted satirist, he needs to fasten on a solidly contemporary situation. (When, as in Chez Nous earlier this year, he buttons down tight on an enclosed group, the result can be really memorable.) Futuristic fantasy loosens all the controls; he proves inventive in short bursts, never seeming to know where he is heading.

His main characters are the inhabitants of two adjacently stranded vehicles. From a huge and luxuriously appointed motor-caravan ('an Englishman's motor-home is his castle') there pour (oh, the irony) a Ford works supervisor and his family. From the small and battered car in front issue a peer, a close friend of the Prime Minister, and his fearsome mother. Forced into proximity the two groups are soon on first-name terms. For the time being they have much to offer each other. The workers have some food, some fuel, the expertise to repair his lordship's car, and--most important--a portable lavatory. The aristocrats have affability. The lady is admittedly reactionary (and makes several repetitive speeches to prove it), but she turns up trumps on the third day, when there is a baby to be delivered, rallying all the other girls to help. In the end, however, when the multitudes are ordered to walk home, she and her son are raised aloft to depart by helicopter. (How often have you seen gods getting into a machine?)

Mr Nichols pays acid tribute to the British class system (the play is not as futuristic as all that) but, though he may try hard to persuade himself, I doubt if he is really angered by it. This part of the play needs perhaps to be rewritten by Edward Bond; though he would probably have had his roadside castaways eating each other by the second act. Stretches of The Freeway are very funny, but it keeps running into road blocks. Some of these are the fault of the production; for its realisation the text demands clean technical virtuosity, the quality in which Jonathan Miller as a director is most notably deficient. A first act curtain, meant to be menacing, peters embarrassingly out; violence, though much talked of, conspicuously fails to impend. Some obstacles were hopefully peculiar to the first night, when the cast succumbed to hideous panic; nerves and memories collapsed. Subtracting the agony and adding a few days' worth of confidence, one can calculate several good performances.

Graham Crowden is all quizzical good-fellowship as the lord, bending over forwards (this being the angle at which Mr Crowden plays) to humour his inferiors; Rachel Kempson may be our stage's most terrifying gentlewoman, instinct with the insouciant vulgarity that only breeding can bestow. The caravan party is led by Paul Rogers, worryingly conscientious and so unobtrusively right that his full quality escaped me on the night, and only thudded home the following morning. Irene Handl is her good theatrical self as his wife; to her belongs the evening's most enchanting moment, as she recites a solid patch of 'Hiawatha', and its most hilarious when, tidying a monumentally messy grass verge, she tosses back on to it a piece of litter for which she takes no responsibility. Joan Hickson, as a predatory belle of the ballrooms, and Lionel Murton, as a visitor from Canada, warily play out the early stages of a courtship which the play proceeds to forget, though, as if in compensation, it bestows on Mr Murton the responsibility for a final curtain which is obviously significant and confusingly ill-prepared. Mark Dignam is unpersuasive as the Minister, but he is lumbered with the worst written scene.

* * *

Peter King's Away From It All (Terrace Theatre, ICA), is, I suppose, a serious comedy whose seriousness (amounting at times to tartness) worked better on a sparsely attended second night than its humour. The production seemed jerky, needing to be lubricated by laughter. This might make the play's technical contrivance (particularly as regards exits and entrances) a formal delight rather than an irritant. Zena Walker as an overworked wife is throatily enchanting (she always reminds me of Judi Dench, though historically she got there first) and her emotional generosity goes some way towards filling the house.
