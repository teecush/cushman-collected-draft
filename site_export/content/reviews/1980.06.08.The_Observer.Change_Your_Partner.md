Change your partner

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Ayckbourn at the National.

PAY attention, and I will try to make clear the four-scene structure of Alan Ayckbourn's Sisterly Feelings (Olivier).

It all takes place on a green but muddy hill. Here long ago Ralph, a doctor, proposed to Amy, now deceased. After her funeral Ralph brings his family and their connections on a sentimental visit. Ralph has two grown-up daughters, Dorcas and Abigail. Owing to a shortage of cars one of them will have to walk home in the coveted company of a fancible young man named Simon.

Simon is (here we go) the girls' brother's fiancee's brother. Which lady he shall escort is settled for him (here we go again and deeper) by the toss of a coin. Its fall determines the content of the next scene, since it is taken for granted that the walk home will launch either Dorcas or Abigail on an affair with Simon.

The second scene accordingly consists of a family picnic (father's annual treat) in which Simon sports with Dorcas (or Abigail), jealously observed by Abigail (or Dorcas), and also by Dorcas's former boy-friend (or Abigail's husband), who wants her back (or wants her back). The end of this scene brings us to another junction-point; the sister in possession may either stay with Simon or abandon him to her rival. The decision is left to the actress concerned, and it determines the content of the third scene which may be either Simon participating in a cross-country race with Dorcas as a steward, or Abigail defying her business-executive husband by opting for one night of the simple life, spent with Simon in a tent. Either way the scene ends with Simon forsaken, and old ways resumed. The last scene never varies, and is cunningly constructed to make sense whatever has gone before. The upshot is that no decisions we make, or let chance make for us, have any effect on our ultimate fate. To register this point properly you have to see the play twice, taking in different versions of the middle scenes on each occasion.

Mr Ayckbourn offers us both a technique and a philosophy. The first works superbly. There are few theatrical excitements to compare with the suspense on Tuesday night when Penelope Wilton as Abigail, knowing she had the audience in the palm of her hand, lingered out her moment of choice at the end of the second scene. Our suspense had everything to do with the actress, nothing with the character; it is, after all, built into the scheme of the play that the character has no effective choice.

And here Mr Ayckbourn's comic determinism betrays him. 'We're really not at all alike,' says Abigail to Dorcas, and we are presumably meant to think that she is fooling herself. But from what we see they really don't seem to have much in common; and though Mr Ayckbourn gets a lot of acute fun out of their joint presentation, competing for their mutual flame or counting the odds on their father's decline into senility like a suburban Goneril and Regan, there is a damaging imbalance between them.

Dorcas, a local radio presenter and obsessive lame-duck collector, is considerably the nicer of the two and therefore, in an Ayckbourn play, decisively handicapped; Anna Carteret needs all her charm to fill the role out. Her lover Stafford, left-wing poet and layabout, is way off Mr Ayckbourn's beat, and his farcical attempts at re-conquest seem to have been written in the author's sleep; though Simon Callow (who, when on the fringe, was invariably cast as a pillar of the Establishment and is now in the reverse situation) plays him with compensating energy.

He responds ferociously to advertisements for four-figure bathtubs (if you follow me) in the Sunday supplements. Patrick, Abigail's husband, reads the same ads with (in every sense) consuming devotion. So they are really brothers under the skin; but Patrick, politely ruthless, is a far more tractable dramatic subject. Michael Gambon who plays him is a fascinating actor operating, behind a bland mask, on a scale that moves from fatuous benevolence to stark brutality and with a precise knowledge of how and when to adjust it.

Miss Wilton, spoiled and nasal, makes him the perfect attitudinising spouse, and their reunion is fore-ordained. Stephen Moore's vacuously athletic Simon jumps off hillocks or flies kites with a delicious air of modest self-satisfaction.

The play's underpinning is generous and secure; in its sequence of family reunions nearly every line tells, and very few cheat. It is typical that the sisters' crucial uniting drama is ticking away behind the amorous facade; they are determined that their brother shall not marry the girl, of his choice and here too fate overtakes them. Selina Cadell as the patronised fiancee is well worth cheering on.

The casting throughout is abnormally strong, though sometimes excessive; Andrew Cruickshank's fey plangency is too other-worldly for the decomposing paterfamilias and Michael Bryant's distinction emphasises rather than disguises the stock qualities of the policeman he plays, though it's something to hear him intone 'it's a free country' in accents of funereal regret. The author and Christopher Morahan manoeuvre the play impeccably across the treacherous undulations of Alan Tagg's set.

The reason, said Tom Lehrer in one of his few relevant remarks not quoted in Tomfoolery (Criterion), most folk-songs are so atrocious is that they were written by the people. He also (and this is quoted) paid sardonic tribute to the courage of the young poet-singers who pelted their audiences with admonitions with which they were fully in agreement anyway.

I am not sure that Mr Lehrer's position, singing anti-bomb and anti-religious songs to a liberal audience, was so very different; but with him there were the crucial differences of insight, flair and wit. By the Sixties I had lost touch with him; so his later songs seem much fresher to me. But I think anyway that he was developing from a sick joker to a satirist; these numbers have a broader base, and offer the performers more scope.

In Gillian Lynne's production The Vatican Rag and The Folk Song Army take imposingly to the stage. The evening even has a shape, with miniature reprises of previous numbers built into the final jolly nuclear chorus, We Will All Go Together When We Go, a song which, after some years' disuse, re-emerges as certainly apt but not at this moment very funny. To say that I admired Robin Ray's prowess as presenter, singer, dancer and momentary violinist is insufficient; I was jealous. A carp: audiences at Death of a Salesman are assumed to know what Linda means, so why Anglicise an all-American song like My Home Town?

Buchner the German prodigy of the 1830s who died at 23, may have discovered modern boredom, as eventually popularised by Samuel Beckett. In Danton's Death and in the strange comedy Leonce and Lena (now on view at the Gate Theatre in Notting Hill) his characters groan under the sheer weight of observed phenomena. It clogs their lives, and their conversation. Some of the tedium is transmitted all too efficiently, but more is bracing and amusing.

The Gate production is imaginatively designed, with Stuart Fox quite stylish as the hero, heir to the tiniest of principalities, who eventually blunders into a secret marriage with his own fiancee. Buchner's world, in the end, is surprisingly similar to Mr Ayckbourn's.
