Body, mind and soul

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Terry Hands's new production of The Changeling.

RIVERSIDE Studios' production of The Changeling was good; the Aldwych production is better.

It is, for a start, much steamier. Perhaps this has been overdone, but the faults in Terry Hands's staging are on the right side and they are redeemed by two outstanding virtues. One is the close attention paid to the play's verbal wit; puns are not just decoration, they are the vital material of a play about changing states - spiritual, physical, psychological. The other, closely allied to the first, is the performance of Diana Quick as Beatrice, with whose body, mind and soul the play is principally concerned.

Usually the interest in this play passes to De Flores, the servant whom she has unconsciously seduced long before circumstances cause her to make explicit overtures to him, who all too knowingly seduces her. His is an uncommonly grateful role for an actor; a good De Flores can justify The Changeling as a good Iago can justify Othello. He will, all the same, leave the heart of the play untouched - as happened at Riverside, and years ago at the Royal Court.

Those earlier Beatrices were blanks; Miss Quick bangs over every phrase of the character's development (rather too hard at times - one thing the performance lacks is a flowing line). At first she is a spoiled child, impatient both of her official fiance and of De Flores's offensive ugliness, which she construes as a personal insult. "The next good mood I find my father in," she declares, sounding in the play's marvellously conversational verse the imperious note of debutantes down the ages, "I'll get him quite discarded."

Needing his services as a murderer (what else could he be fit for?) she becomes exaggeratedly affectionate, removing her shawl to reveal a good proportion of her bosom: no wonder he thinks he is dreaming. It is of course absurd that she could then profess dismay when he claims her as his reward, but it is quite believable. Beatrice is very weak on assessing the consequences of her actions; hence her revulsion when De Flores produces the dead man's finger as evidence of mission accomplished.

Miss Quick gives full value to Beatrice's evasions - her crushingly ironic appeal to her modesty, for example. Her degradation, half enjoyed, is done with similar conviction. She is married in effigy to her beloved Alsemero, while at the back of the stage her actual body is enthusiastically humped by De Flores. Her complaint, "this fellow has undone me endlessly," wearily delivered, becomes an hilarious double entendre that Middleton (of Middleton and Rowley, playwrights) would surely have approved.

De Flores, meanwhile, is reduced in scale; Emrys James has perhaps played too many villains, too many malcontents. Here he acts almost in the abstract, giving us all the man's logic, but nothing of his suffering. But the supporting cast are uniformly good, notably Julian Glover and Charles Dance as the Piracquo brothers - murdered Alonzo and avenging Tomazo - a pair of blond grandees in a predominantly brunette world.

In Mr Dance's rendition, Tomazo's passion, like those of Beatrice and De Flores, approaches madness - as it should, since these respectable blank-verse people are set against the disreputable prose population who only play at madness and who themselves are placed among certified, locked-up lunatics. Mr Hands keeps these gibbering on stage throughout the final scenes; and he runs out of things for them to do (one berserk caper is much like another). But, before this, he has animated the intrigues and brought out their pertinence with considerable flair.

Here too we have a besieged heroine (spirited Charlotte Cornwell), but one who can deal with her situation. Here we have another eavesdropping servant, a would-be villain whom Barrie Rutter turns to genial farce. And here we have virtuoso essays in feigned dementia by John McEnery and Stephen Jenn, both haunting and funny. Mr Hands takes on the entire Jacobean apparatus, dumb shows and ghosts and all (throwing in a particularly good banquet en route) and makes it live.

The past is recalled with comparable skill, though in vastly different style, in the current Riverside attraction, Joint Stock's The Ragged Trousered Philanthropists. Stephen Lowe's adaptation of Robert Tressell's novel concentrates on men at work, counterpointing the possible joys of house painting in 1906, and the actual humiliations.

Rankling with the men as much as their starvation wages is the fact of being forced to do a job in half the time necessary to do it well. The chief revolutionary among them is also the finest craftsman; so absolute are his demands that he almost holds aloof when his fellows finally take collective action with limited aims. Their rising, at once tentative and effective, ends William Gaskill's production on a magnificent note, tense, expectant and satisfying; a miraculous balance of forces.

In the main play the seven actors constituting the workforce double as bosses and onlookers - sometimes stepping before our eyes from one role to another. These moments are strangely exciting, as are the repeated assumptions by each of them of the character of foreman, achieved simply by donning a suit. This only misfires when the man commits suicide - the result of the multiple performance is to prohibit not only empathy at this point but simple interest.

In general the fluid direction sets off performances of exemplary solidity - the best case yet for Joint Stock's participatory methods. They have made the book their own (it takes on an independent life unexampled among stage versions of novels) without ever betraying it.

* * *

However, two playwrights with talented pasts (and, quite possibly, futures) are giving trouble at present. The title character of David Pownall's An Audience Called Edouard (Greenwich) is Manet, though he never appears. He is looking, with us, at an animated canvas of his Dejeuner sur l'Herbe, whose four figures have broken for an extended chat. They are joined by two other figures, one of them Karl Marx - an obvious threat to their Second Empire existence. So, in another way, is Manet himself (and they, the sitters, what with strikes on the Suez Canal and personal problems, aren't too happy either) but battle is never joined; the stasis inherent in the original idea is never transcended.

The main interest of Alan Strachan's production is its capacity to come up with tableaux that look every bit as provocative as Manet's own; indeed, consulting a picture book at the theatre bookstall, it is possible to indulge in the pleasingly heretical thought that Edouard got it all wrong and his naked figure looks nothing like Susan Hampshire.

In Ballroom (Young Vic Studio) Terence Greer shows us, at numbing length, a couple of Ionesco automata (the sum of their own prejudices and therefore dramatically dead) taking out their deficiencies on their children, who revolt. The play is set in the thirties; among the things for which the characters are apparently blamed is the rise of Nazi Germany. This, since it yields guaranteed shudders, is cheap as well as inflated. Mr Greer seems to be paying off old scores; it's rather late in the century to be satirising the appeal of Shirley Temple. There are some passages of bravura writing, and the acting in Mel Smith's production is well tuned and, when opportunity permits, lively.

* * *

At the Open Space, Beckett has directed an extraordinary Krapp in Rick Cluchey, an ex-con whose San Quentin Drama Workshop grew out of the affinity of Beckett's plays to prison life. Cluchey's is the bleakest, most soulless and terrifying Krapp I have seen: a nightmare vision who unveils his (half) banana with godless joy and is tetchy to the point of violence. He is a ghost, the un-dead, and as foreign to the younger, articulate self of the tapes as that self would be to the impoverished scarecrow who shuffles before us.

Endgame, the other half of the bill, was directed by Cluchey in consultation with Samuel Beckett, an association that apparently lasted 10 days but left the members of the troupe satisfied that this was Beckett's production. Nevertheless, the result is much less successful (as it is, for me, a less successful play), despite some beautifully musical exchanges.
