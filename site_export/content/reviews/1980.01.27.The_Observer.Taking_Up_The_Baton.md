Taking up the baton

ROBERT CUSHMAN talks to actor-manager Timothy West, who opens in London this week in Beecham.

TIMOTHY WEST believes that the cinema doesn't know what to do with him: 'My trouble is that I don't play the parts I look like.' What he looks like is teddy-bearish, most of the time anyway. At present he sports a goatee which makes him look like a minor Mephistopheles: a laughing devil.

It has been grown as a necessary adornment to his performance as Sir Thomas Beecham, opening officially at the Apollo Theatre on Tuesday. Written by Caryl Brahms and Ned Sherrin, it is not quite a play, not quite a one-man show; he shares the chat with a narrator. It is of course the latest in the West line of historical portraits, following his Churchill and Edward VII on television and his Oliver Cromwell on radio, not to mention a superb Oscar Wilde at the Open Space.

'People think you have a special talent for something because you've done it before.' He cites the case of Ian Richardson, approached to play Anthony Blunt after his success in Tinker, Tailor, Soldier, Spy: 'They've decided he plays moles.'

There have obviously been peculiar problems involved with Beecham and Churchill, both well-remembered public figures; the actor - especially since the scripts are generally as much documentary as drama - has to have a touch of Mike Yarwood about him. West says himself that 'it's much more a graph-paper, and geometrical instrument kind of thing than ordinary acting. Beecham is actually not a terribly different problem from Churchill - they're both easily imitable men. Both, one might add, self-created enfants terribles, though the actor warms to Beecham, sympathises with him, more readily than with Churchill.

There have been only two major technical difficulties with Beecham. One is the voice: 'Beecham's delivery was so slow that you couldn't reproduce it accurately' without, presumably, lulling the audience to sleep. (West, as it happens, is a noticeably crisp, fast actor). The other is the conducting itself; sometimes it looked as if Beecham wasn't doing anything at all up there. But West doesn't think the audience would take that from an actor: 'They need to be assured that you know the rudiments of what you're doing, so I do more than Beecham did.'

Though happy with Edward VII in particular, West would admit that bio-drama is, at best, a sub-division of acting. That he should recently have done so much of it is a pointer to the peculiar position in which he has recently found himself. At 45, he is a TV star and a theatrical leader.

There are few actors who command more respect within the profession, both for his work and for his grasp of the theatre as a whole; art and business. But his recent theatrical roles have been modest: Claudius in Hamlet, the Doctor in A Month in the Country, Enobarbus in Antony and Cleopatra, Judge Brack in Hedda Gabler - all pivotal and rewarding parts, but none of them major challenges to an actor who eight years ago was an admired Lear.

Reviewing the Claudius - the most exciting of those performances, with a genuine sense of danger about it - I called him 'a supercompetent actor,' a description which I gather both amused and worried him. He admits that 'I'm somebody who demands quite a lot of good acting around me, and quite a lot of thoughtful direction. Left to myself, I have a facility for producing something that is serviceable.'

After his Lear, an actor told me that 'Timmy wants to go to Hollywood and be Rod Steiger.' That didn't happen; what did was a season as artistic director at the Forum Theatre, Billingham, acting in one play and directing three others. He discovered that he liked directing and enjoyed having some control of his own destiny.

Since then he has had a spell on the Arts Council Drama Panel and has been a co-director of the Prospect, now the Old Vic, Theatre Company. At the end of last year, on the resignation of Toby Robertson, he took over the direction of the company. He had in fact been asked to step in for a year's emergency rescue, but declined to take the job for less than two. Otherwise he would have no chance of implementing his own policies.

So for the last couple of months Old Vic plans have chased Beecham's scores and witticisms round his head. For the time being the Vic will be filled by visiting companies. (One aim is 'to revive, in some way, the World Theatre Season.') In September his first homegrown season begins with Macbeth, with Peter O'Toole ('very keen to be included: an artistic associate'), The Jew of Malta, directed by Jack Gold, West's own productions of Trelawny of the Wells and The Relapse.

West is the first man I have heard enunciate a feeling which many share but which he himself would have argued against three years ago, which is that he refuses to make a fetish of new writing. 'Nobody can tell us anything new; there are no icons left to be attacked. The writing is on the wall, in words of one syllable 10 feet high. In times like this the classics become especially precious.'

But they have to be presented properly, and he admits that 'a lot of our recent work has emerged looking a bit careless.' He will not, I think, be satisfied if after two years Prospect's reputation remains as obstinately behind that of the large companies as it has, to date. Because he is now, unarguably, an actor-manager running a company with a major touring commitment, he has been repeatedly compared to Donald Wolfit. But the comparison hardly holds. Wolfit always played the lead and never cared much about production standards or supporting playing.

Tim - I am tired of calling him West, which sounds quite unsuitably brusque - is a shrewd, sly judge of acting, who is likely not to play enough leads. He has had recent tucked-away attempts at Macbeth and Falstaff and found them discouraging. On the other hand he recognises that the public expect a certain amount of activity from their favoured players, and that this must include a few attempts on the peaks. His idol, unastonishingly, is Ralph Richardson, who has expressed himself in similar fashion on the perils of mountaineering, and whom he admires for attaining truth through eccentricity.

Tim is not an eccentric actor, though he can certainly play eccentrics. What distinguishes him is an odd combination of weight and roguishness. Other actors alternate between feet-on-ground and head-in-clouds; he can do both at once.

He has, on and off the stage, impeccable comic timing, an attribute shared with his wife, Prunella Scales, and indeed with his father, Lockwood West, who, in the juicy role of a crafty old malcontent, is the best reason for listening to Wagner's Walls.

Despite his theatrical parentage, Timothy West hesitated a few years, pretending he didn't want to be an actor. He was a recording engineer before taking the plunge. The delay didn't seem to do him any harm. To whatever he has done, he has always come well prepared.
