Gosh, what improvising!

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on two plays from Stratford and Max Wall's one-man show.

THE 1974 season at Stratford was generally reckoned to be unfortunate, an estimate in which I concurred. I did not, however, see all the productions; and the two which arrived in London last week are gaps that I am glad to have filled. Last year on Avon was happier than I had realised.

Babies Grow Old brings the RSC to the ICA. It was first staged in Stratford's smallish Other Place and, like most of the work of its director, Mike Bogdanov, is composed through improvisation. Surprisingly, considering that this has been going on long enough perhaps for its actors to regard it as just another text, it retains its peculiar fascination, the overheard quality of a dramatic solution. There is an unusual quality of document in a theatre. It is not merely a matter of short or half-formed sentences; these certainly occur, but in the performance of one actor, Matthew Guinness, the form reaches out to incorporate great rolling cadences.

Mr Guinness plays a youngish doctor, an out-sider in what is otherwise a family group. He is eager and well meaning and out of his depth. He sits on a sofa, politely refusing and then consuming innumerable cups of tea and, as afternoon gives place to evening, glasses of Scotch. He seems reluctant to go home, though he often gestures towards it, and we may guess that he is not happy there. He finally breaks out into an extraordinary tirade about his own profession, lashing himself as a licensed drug-peddler, backtracking to reassure himself that after all he is a good deal charier with the prescriptions than most of his colleagues, then taking off again into horrible premonitions of the world his daughter will inherit.

Mr Guinness's tumbling eloquence (underlined by the elaborate unceasing gaucherie of his arms and legs, which betray him whenever he tries either to relax or to persuade) is a tribute to the improvisational method; no writer could set down the stops and starts, the furious emphases, that make up his speeches (or would even dare sprinkle them with the `goshes' and `supers' that seem so bashfully accurate here), and no actor could derive them from a conventional text. Emlyn Williams's The Corn is Green might have a young man rather like Mr Guinness's doctor, but he would use well-turned English and Shakespearean quotations, not such things as a child's `I don't know' transformed into a diagnosis of a life-style.

The home which Mr Guinness invades belongs to a half-crippled old woman who sits beneath her roof, but hardly to address a word to her. And by appropriating an unoccupied bed on the ground floor, they unintentionally confine her to an upper room. It is a symbol of exquisite injustice, and rather horrible because no one seems aware of it. The acting is mostly remarkable. Jane Lapotaire's Viola should address the audience, seeking a sounding-board rather than a mirror. When Robert Lloyd's Sebastian appears he is, as he should be, Viola's twin sister's gallant whirlwind opportunism.

Miss Lapotaire, who races round the stage as if taking possession of it before demanding what she should do in Illyria, is the most believable boy-girl since Barbara Jefford 17 years ago; and she has the advantage of a production which explores all the play's bisexual tensions. The atmosphere is, in Auden's phrase, a bit whiffy. Here is an Orsino (John Price) as apt to fondle a page as a mistress, who at one point addresses himself to the young twin, and says, "Most wonderful!" in a squeal of exquisite recognition, finding herself confronted with two apparent boy-girls.

The scenes in which one heroine woos the other are exemplary, Viola alternately retreating and raising as Mary Rutherford's Olivia (the best I remember) sheds her defences and goes on the attack. From her first appearance, indeed, she shows signs of life and humour; it is because she is inclined to speak well of fools that Nicol Williamson's Malvolio -- a Welsh precision machine -- might so jealously fur her soul and ultimately for her body.

All this brings out the interdependence of the play's two plots. It is Malvolio's contemptuous description of Viola that excites Olivia's desire to see her. Olivia, embracing Malvolio with Illyrian carelessness, unwittingly primes him for his downfall. Mr Williamson's dreams of lust in the garden are terrible to overhear, his smile a major climax. His enemies are formidable: David Waller a malcontent Sir Toby, living on the edge of his niece's tolerance and his own, and Ron Pember a tattered clown who accepts tips, plaudits and rebukes with the steely disdain of an over-seasoned entertainer, insisting out on us until the last word of the last song.

A seductive rumour has it that the part of Feste in this production was originally offered to Max Wall; and in fact his demeanor in his one-man show Aspects of Max Wall (Garrick) is not dissimilar to Mr Pember's. Mr Wall ("I'm an mourning for my material") exploits, but does not indulge, his new cult status. He is a sweetness itself as he remonstrates with the musicians who have beaten him to the end of a phrase ("you were too fond and far too quick and everything went wrong"), but he is liable, with a flash of his teeth and an upward thrust of his chin, to dismiss them and us and the whole business of being the last surviving star of the music-hall.

Mr Wall is a battered cheerful misanthrope, a singing guitarist of great charm, a man of strange and sudden pleasures, a Beckett figure. (Compare the delight he squeezes from the word "stool" with that taken by Krapp in "spool.") I have insufficient space for him, which is a shame; that I have none for the Oxford Playhouse's disinterment of The Vegetable, a cheery, weary comedy by Scott Fitzgerald, is a mercy.
