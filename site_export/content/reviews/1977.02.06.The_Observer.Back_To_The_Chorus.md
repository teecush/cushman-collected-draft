Back to the Chorus

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

When the American company of A Chorus Line opened at Drury Lane last year the audience applauded before it had seen any one of them move a muscle. The mere sight of them, when the lights went up, poised in auditioning formation and reflected in the practice-mirrors beyond, brought down the house. This was not just first-night generosity; it was common form in the New York production, all nights of the week. When the British cast took over on Tuesday, that native electricity, visible even when latent, had gone back where it belongs.

After the visual impact, the aural: the most exciting line in the show, really, had always been the whiplash cry - part goad, part encouragement - of Zach, the director: "five-six-seven-eight." It sparked the whole opening sequence, made it an experience for which the show could be forgiven any amount of equivocation later. Now the vital words have lost their edge, become a muffle of unfamiliar vowels. In view of British Equity's bewildering vacillations over the proposed secondment to the British company of Donna McKechnie from New York, what is a Frenchman doing in the role of Zach? A French actor, too (Jean Pierre Cassel), who barely holds his own in the dance numbers, where his predecessor, the magnetic Eivind Harum, was their guiding force.

Replacing Miss McKechnie as Cassie, who struggles to get back in the chorus after an ill-fated attempt to leave it and become a star, is Petra Siniawski, who did better than one had dared to hope. She does not, as Cassie should, compel attention from the beginning; she lacks Miss McKechnie's generous features, her red dress, and her established reputation. But she plays the lines better and her big solo dance of frustration, hurling herself against the mirrors, is more powerful than it was in New York, where one kept waiting in vain for Miss McKechnie to take off, as she had in Promises, Promises and Company.

Actually many of the numbers looked better to me than ever before, perhaps because I was seeing them for the first time from above. (Critics are usually hustled down to the front stalls for a worm's eye view. Why? Don't answer that.) The long piece of rhythmic group autobiography, "Hello Twelve Hello Thirteen Hello Love," was a revelation; just as impressive as the group-movement with its rapturous climax is the sudden quick walk along the white audition line of a frightened dancer awaiting his turn at confession.

Michael Bennett's use of performers as mobile visual statements, each with his own identity, still outclasses everything else in London (though Tales from the Vienna Woods is a contender). Some of the new team sing well, though those who don't lack the American ability to brazen the thing out; one of the funniest songs goes for little and another for nearly nothing. Four of the boys are brilliant dancers, and Michael Stanniforth's long self-pitying monologue is excellent.

* * *

A Chorus Line began its life off-Broadway. Few fringe productions take so well to enlargement; the problem of what is to be done with them when they are good remains pressing and apparently insoluble. Barrie Keeffe's Gimme Shelter (Soho Poly) is due for a provincial tour; and Robert Holman's German Skerries (Bush) will probably have an afterlife in rep studios, those keepers of the regional theatres' conscience. But both deserve more.

Mr Holman's play indicates the richness and concentration available in a miniature auditorium. In Chris Parr's production every solid object from a lady's bicycle (ridden by a man) to a jotting-pad has a stunning, almost poetic, impact; a bank of mucky grass might have been transplanted from the Green across the road. Theatres apparently hopelessly equipped for realism are actually its citadels.

Of course we would all like the theatre to go beyond realism, but it does no harm to get there first. This Mr Holman beautifully does; his central characters, realised in loving detail by Paul Copley and Caroline Hutchison, are a young married couple poised between happiness and discontent. The setting is Tees-side, and in the central speech (which broadens the play out amazingly) Mr Copley evokes "a mass of industry ... stretching for 30 miles." He pays it a curious kind of respect, seeing that it has provided him with a dead-end job, and that its side-effects may include the dispersal of a flock of cormorants to which he, as a bird-watcher, is devoted. The German Skerries are the rocks on which they gather; they also, I fancy, stand for the shipwreck which menaces most of the characters' dreams, and which causes one death in earnest. The play has a distinctive flavour: bracing, lyrical, rooted.

Mr Keeffe has a harsher way with no-hopers, in whose predicament he involves most of us. Gimme Shelter is a triptych; its central panel, called Gotcha, is imbued with tremendous force. A 16-year-old kid, about to leave a comprehensive with no prospects and a damning report, holds his headmaster and two others to ransom. He falls into the situation, and knows that it is ultimately hopeless, but hatred keeps him going; he threatens them with a great explosion in a confined space, a powerful weapon of which Philip Davis's performance, one part snivels to nine of desperation, is itself a living theatrical example; his outbursts are the most overwhelming since Deborah Norton's in Kennedy's Children. Mr Keeffe, by probing the weak spots of all four characters, ensures belief and brings off the most guilt-inducing social play in years.

On either side of it stand two pieces about Kev, an ex-grammar-school rebel, now working for an insurance firm, scratching to keep revolt alive, the dupe of his own rhetoric and increasingly of his own compromise. In the last play he meets the piratical kid of the second, just out of Borstal and effectively brain-washed, and hails him as a brother revolutionary; he meets, of course, with incomprehension.

Mr Keeffe underlines too much here; the simple confrontation says everything necessary, and besides, Kev never quite convinces. But there is one masterly stroke of continuity; one of the kid's main grievances against his hostages was that in five years they never learned his name. But when his case gets in the papers his youth ensures that his anonymity is preserved, and when Kev wants to salute him he can only say "Day'll come, they'll chant your name. ... Whatever it is."
