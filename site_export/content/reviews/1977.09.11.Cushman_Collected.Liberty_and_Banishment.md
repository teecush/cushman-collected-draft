Liberty and banishment

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Stratford.

I visited The Old Country (Queen's) in a state of more than usual innocence. The publicity had been secretive, and I failed even to get a programme. All I knew when the curtain rose was that Alan Bennett had written it, and that Alec Guinness and Rachel Kempson were in it.

Sure enough there were Sir Alec and Miss Kempson married to each other, pottering around an odd-looking country villa (what a big veranda, and could those really be birch trees at the back?). They spoke of England in the special terms, distant yet possessive, familiar in the kind of play that habitually gets described as 'civilised'. There were many books, and the Guinness persona--here called Hilary--was appropriately literate. I began to wonder if he would ever stop being so serene; he can even be serenely irritated.

A young couple appeared, and were not, it seemed, too welcome. The boy was timidly ingratiating and the girl close-cropped and almost definitely foreign. I discovered in the interval that she was Heather Canning, so highly praised at the Aldwych a few years ago, and was surprised. Miss Kempson, noting Sir Alec's distaste, remarked that after the war he and his visitors had things in common. They were all, she said, traitors.

At which point the oddities in the situation came together, the villa revealed itself as having every right to resemble a dacha (earlier one wondered if the management had booked a Chekhov production by mistake) and the nostalgic meanings of the opening scene took on, retrospectively, an edge. I wished, and still do, that I could remember more of them.

Hilary the defector is visited, in his aching Russian retreat, by his sister and her husband, on a trip from the home country to take in the art (why else, they ask, does one travel anywhere?). Brother-in-law Duff is on the board of absolutely everything and also lectures on E. M. Forster. This prompts Hilary to endear himself to me for ever by asking just what 'only connect' is supposed to mean: the first time I have heard such doubts voiced in public. It could perhaps be done only by an exile.

There is no greater patriot than an expatriate, and Hilary plainly longs for home. Duff tries to persuade him to return, overestimating, it seems to me, both the amenities of Her Majesty's prisons (he compares them favourably to Trust Houses) and the compassion of the popular press. Hilary, though he never gives such a vulgarly practical reason, seems reluctant; perhaps, for all its discomforts, his present life of acidulous nostalgia fits him better than any other. It certainly suits his author, who has found just about the perfect framework for his preferred style and subject.

There are many delights. Guinness rouses himself from his elegant stupor to express real wit, real pain, each intensifying the other. Asked to come back, he sits with his eyes closed, as if suffering from some cosmic headache; then he turns slightly and the set of his shoulders shows that everything is hopeless.

Duff, as written by Mr Bennett and acted by John Phillips, is a monumental inside job on the establishment. Mr Phillips is intelligent and immovable and silly, vastly proud of a self-fostered and probably imaginary reputation as a maverick ('I have the name of being a little difficile') and very sad inside.

When Hilary remarks, apropos his 14-year absence, 'I'm so out of it,' Duff, with no such excuse, is plainly in the same predicament. He is in himself a whole television series on Civilisation. Both men are well mated, Miss Kempson being all patience and contempt, Faith Brook all glass. 'You're lucky,' she says, 'eating so simply. We would, only it's so complicated.'

I have quoted shamelessly and could continue; the one-liners beat the set pieces, which I would rather hear delivered by the author, out of context. They cannot keep the play going; Mr Bennett seems one of those playwrights (like T. S. Eliot) who can tell a story, but cannot make it matter. His natural mode is irony, and it is sadly characteristic of him that he proclaims the fact; Hilary says he will give it up and has failed within two minutes. It happens to be the dramatic quality to which I am most attracted, but I like it to be made flesh.

* * *

As You Like It (Stratford) presents us with the two faces of Trevor Nunn. The showbiz Nunn, late of the Comedy of Errors, turns as much of the show as possible into a musical. Likely bits of dialogue are sung--a bad idea, though an increasingly common one, since good dialogue is different in shape and pressure from a good lyric--and the real songs are turned, care of Gillian Lynne, into production numbers. This is fine, and 'A Lover and His Lass' becomes a properly joyous prelude to the closing nuptials. It even accommodates, very touchingly, the courtship of Oliver and Celia, which Shakespeare, to say the least, underwrote.

There is also the alert and scholarly Nunn, combing the text to amplify the story. Thus Orlando is allowed to practise his wrestling on an old Adam and even on the disguised Rosalind. (It is some tribute to Peter McEnery that he makes such an arrant bully likeable.) The stream of refugees to Arden is unending; Le Beau and Charles, the wrestler, both turn up there, on the strength of some longing lines uttered in their courtier days, and Duke Frederick's conversion (from sheer loneliness, I imagine) takes on a kind of logic.

'Now go we in content,' cries Celia, 'to liberty and not to banishment.' And Mr Nunn carefully juxtaposes the two. (So of course does Mr Bennett, who actually has a line about the Forest of Arden.) In winter Arden is palpably cold and Jaques seems the sanest man in the wood; come the spring, it rains coloured confetti, and his discomfiture--for in each of the comedies, someone has to get locked out--is inevitable.

In Shakespeare's own day, philosophy and musical comedy met in the masque, and this is Mr Nunn's solvent here, though he pushes it on a half century or more; cut-out forest with crooks for the shepherds (Phebe, sheepless, looks like Bo-Beep), an etched country chateau for Oliver, and the court of Versailles for the Duke, with Touchstone, jumping on in time, got up like Watteau's sad jester Gilles.

Hymen, who comes on to marry everyone at the end, now has a sung prologue, presiding at a dispute between Fortune and Nature. Rosalind and Celia, two scenes on, carefully enunciate their brief discussion of these two abstractions, but it remains an intellectual accretion, and though marriage is the climax of the play, it is not really its subject. Besides, the songs, the dances, and the costumes do not as yet become many of the actors.

If Nature means innate ability and Fortune acquired skills, Kate Nelligan's Rosalind is a natural, able with her superb whole-heartedness to rule a scene, but not to govern this stage, this language, and this over-complicated presentation. She is at her finest in the mock marriage where her page-boy masquerade disappears; it is always the woman whom we watch and for whom we feel. Emrys James's diseased Jaques similarly fails to ride the production. I liked him when he was on, but forgot him when he wasn't. Best of the rest are Judith Paris's Celia, Ian Gelder's Silvius, and David Shaw-Parker's unnervingly confident William.
