The Impression of Impotence

Robert Cushman reviews *H* / Old Vic

At the centre of Charles Wood's new play about the Indian Mutiny of 1857 General Havelock—the “H” of the title—gives a dinner-party. The mood is subdued since this is Cawnpore and the spirits of the Moveable Column, Havelock's expeditionary force, have been fatally dashed by their discovery of the massacre of the women and children they had come to save. Havelock himself, spry and chipper hitherto, is the most affected. He had entered Cawnpore in a state of modest self-satisfaction (“I am too old for fame, but I think I shall have it”) and he is now sunk into self-effacing depression. Conversation wilts in his presence, and since he frowns on military talk at table his dinner guests find the going rough. They include a Welsh captain with an authentic Fluellen blend of sentiment and professional ambition, hot for promotion but with no concern for glory; a mordant surgeon obsessed by the knowledge that though medical science advances he kills more than he cures; and Havelock's own son, dubious as to his father's preternaturally strict Christian principles and just beginning to come out of his shell.

Here, if anywhere, the disparate threads of Charles Wood's play are knotted tightly together. The nagging doubts as to whether this is a play about the dilemmas of being a devout Christian and a highly professional soldier, or about the plight of the English who loved India and were spurned by it, or just a group study of men at war are temporarily stilled. All the elements briefly coalesce. Then history takes over and chaos comes again. A chronicle play carries its own built-in curse. It starts any number of hares and has no time to chase them.

The style can be a hindrance too. The long solo speeches which give the play its sub-title are exhilaratingly good; the slightly stiff, archaic rhythms are exactly judged, and perfectly suited to the enclosed world the play depicts. They are capable too of fairly complex orchestration: witness the desultory chatter at the dinner-party. But though the language can set the characters in counterpoint, it seems incapable of evoking actual conflict.

A firmer production might have cut a clean swathe through the verbiage. But Geoffrey Reeves is inclined to let the oratory overwhelm him. In the early encounters between Havelock and Neill one's main consciousness is of watching two good performances which might as well be going on in separate plays; the gist of the argument comes through but not its shape. As the evening proceeds it gains greatly in cohesiveness but there are still blank patches, one of them occurring at the turning-point of the action.

The impression left by the English—by Jones-Parry, the father of another man's child, by the surgeon whose skill does not allow him to save lives, by young Havelock, the self-confessed prey of procrastination and sloth, and most crucially by H himself—is one of impotence.
