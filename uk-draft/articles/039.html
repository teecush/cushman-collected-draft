Today I improvise

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Pirandello and 'Pygmalion.'

THE Chichester season began on Wednesday with a prolonged blackout. Out of the darkness came an unknown voice, requesting Keith Michell, the theatre's new artistic director, to report to the control box. We all laughed, Mr Michell being such a famous name and all.

His expedition to the sources of power proves successful and the lights go up, in the auditorium as well as on stage, allowing him to come before and among us in his own person. He tells us that the Chichester troupe is about to appear under his direction, improvising its way through a scenario devised from a short story by Luigi Pirandello.

A glance at the programme (easy enough with the houselights on) is enough to inform us that he is lying. Tonight We Improvise is indeed emblazoned there, but it seems to be less a statement of intent than the title of a play written by--rather than based on--Pirandello himself. Furthermore there is a director listed and it is Peter Coe, not Mr Michell, who is billed as an actor--and what is more looks and talks like one. He does not chat us up, he harangues us. This is a result partly of his own stage personality, partly of the lines he has been given to speak, which move on stilts in the manner of bad Pirandello translations.

This is disturbing. Mr Michell is pretending to be himself (and we know at least that this is his theatre, as he claims) and he is also proclaiming--in a very literary manner--a theory of drama that has no time for texts; obviously he is generously lending his own name to an acid caricature of a director who behaves in the manner he affects to condemn. But this will not quite do, because in time other actors appear, and though they do not pretend to be directors they talk as unnaturally as he does. They are in fact giving highly stylised performances of themselves as actors, but they do not seem aware of the fact; they think they're being informal. To be fair, one of them, Alfred Marks, profiting from a training in variety, succeeds.

Let me try to fight my way out of this. (Pirandello's influence on the style of subsequent playwrights is widely acknowledged; less so his effect on the style of subsequent reviewers. No wonder he gets bad notices.) Pirandello wrote a play purporting to explore the nature and validity of theatrical improvisation without actually employing it. From this paradox all else springs. He posits a director who wants to do without a text and then lets the text take its revenge. The more the actors try to improvise the more obscure the basic scenario becomes.

Deciding that the fault lies in the director, who is coming between them and the truth with all his effects, the actors banish him from the theatre. The scene they invent in his absence is certainly more concentrated than what has preceded it, but it is no less artificial, deriving much of its effect from make-up and lighting.

But--and here again it is maybe the translator rather than the dramatist who is to blame--there is an extra layer of artifice. These actors' improvisations hardly sound like improvisations (or like good dialogue either). Pirandello wins his case--chaos reigns, sometimes enjoyably, when the author is dethroned--by not strictly fighting it; given his method, he never could. If you are willing to accept that method (and to overlook such confusing extravagances as the leading lady's supposed death from a surfeit of identification), the play remains a useful airing of some basic theatrical questions: the kind that are best considered early and then forgotten. If ever a play was a candidate for sober academic revival this is it.

But of course no director could bear to treat it that way. Mr Coe can hardly be blamed for trying to create a Happening and for calling his director Keith Michell (Pirandello called him Dr Hinkfuss, not, as far as I know, the name of a real person). He could even claim that he is helping to make Pirandello's point, by showing us how calculated all this spontaneity must be; and by loading the evening with impressively boring set-pieces--processions but no progress and stylised cafe scenes--he supplies a convincing rationale for the actors' revolt.

Finally given the chance to act, Keith Baxter and June Ritchie take it very skilfully, but for all their intensity their big scene is still fairly tedious, since it is far too late for us to take any interest in the characters they are supposed to be portraying. And maybe that, too, is part of the point--but I could go on for ever apportioning praise and blame between author, translator, director, actors, and the Chichester audience, whose applause for each familiar face (Miriam Karlin and Annie Ross are also in the company) is an integral part of the occasion. I at least can make it all up as I go along.

AT THE Half Moon Theatre there is an endearing, brave, but ultimately unsuccessful attempt to confine Brecht's vast Saint Joan of the Stockyards within the limitations of a slender budget.

Set in the Chicago of 1929, the play recounts the story of a latter day St Joan who is converted from the pious do-goodery of the Salvation Army to a real dedication to the poor. She attempts to transform Pierpont Mauler, king of the meat industry, but her naivete cannot distinguish between his genuine spasms of good will and the hypocrisy which gets him a better deal. After throwing in her lot with the workers she dies of pneumonia, and is canonised by those she despises.

It is a heavy play, crammed with abstruse financial knowledge, and the Half Moon company's addition of new scenes of battle on the stock market floor does not in any way lighten things. Nor does their propensity to shout their way too quickly through the blank verse in American accents of varying accuracy. But the set--an opening up of the theatre to its rafters in a welter of sawdust and meathooks--is intelligent and right, and the performances, especially that of Philip McGough as Mauler, are spirited. It's not the Berliner Ensemble; but it's a play worth seeing.

Off the Peg, an all-male revue at the Arts Theatre Club, has a sketch from Mahagonny, an awesomely banal parody of the parody. The premise for this unremittingly vulgar evening seems to be that you put the boys in the band in girls' clothes (rather good girls' clothes), make-up and wigs (rather good as well), play some recordings (on a bad sound system), and get the boys/girls to mime to them precisely, but pointlessly; and the whole thing becomes instant amusement. Add frequent gropings at crotches (many of them sequinned) and the whole thing becomes instant hysteria.

A first-class drag artist, like the American Jim Bailey, who impersonates Judy Garland, can tell us a lot about the star, women in general and men's aptitudes to both. But in Off the Peg, impersonations of Ella Fitzgerald, Billie Holiday, Jessie Matthews, Gypsy Rose Lee, Marlene Dietrich and even David Bowie merely provide a supply of coathangers on which to hang some costumes and memories of movies.

Nobody expected the new production of Pygmalion (Albery) to be anything but a success, but in some respects expectation has been outpaced. This is particularly the case with Diana Rigg's Eliza, a rare comic creation, compact of dignity, fire and mischief. This flower-girl, having grown up with nobody to talk to, has necessarily spent much of her short lifetime mumbling to herself, and she carries the habit with her into Professor Higgins's laboratory, where her aggrieved determination to remain a good girl takes on the force of an obsession. As her education proceeds, she floats into Mrs Higgins's at-home, the most bewitching automaton imaginable, and in the last scene, with her full humanity achieved, plays a surpassing duet with Alec McCowen's Higgins. Eyes aflame he circles her, hardly daring to comprehend the enormity of the change that has taken place in his creation.

This Higgins is a man with all the jollity in the world and none of the humour; when--and it is the turning-point of the performance--he accuses Eliza of making him lose his temper for almost the first time in his life, it can be taken as the literal truth. A man so unthinkingly and habitually rude can usually do without emotional outlets. Perhaps taking a cue from the description of Eliza as Higgins's "live doll," John Dexter's production gives her a final door-slamming exit like Ibsen's Nora. The intention may have been to deromanticise the play, but so far as Higgins is concerned it has the reverse effect; she may not be in love, but he is, too late and unavailingly.

Stanley Eveling may never become the major dramatist we once hoped, but he remains among the most engaging of the minors. Shivvers (Theatre Upstairs), a fable of degradation, is intermittently eloquent, chilling and funny, but it suffers from making insecure moral judgments. Two characters pursue a similar downward course, but while one (a recalcitrant vicar, joyfully played by Bill Stewart) is acclaimed a saint, the other (Tony Haygarth, in another of his portrayals of Eveling man, timorous, tubby and lustful--Tony Hancock with metaphysical overtones) is condemned as a villain and left to despair. So one of the thieves was saved, but who holds the scales, and she is well and wearily played by Deirdre Costello.

Alan Ayckbourn's Table Manners (Greenwich) boasts at least one hilarious scene, garnering more laughs than could be believed from the spectacle of a dinner-guest sitting on a chair too low for him, his chin just clearing the board. Perhaps it has to do with timing; we only notice the situation at the precise moment we are intended to. This play is the first of a trilogy. I shall return.
