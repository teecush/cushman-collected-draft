Further thoughts on the RSC's fascists

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN returns to Destiny and discusses the first production at the new Open Space.

I WAS dismayed on opening last Sunday's OBSERVER to read a rather niggling review of David Edgar's Destiny; especially dismayed since I had written it. The only excuse I can offer for my guarded declaration that Destiny may be the best play in London is that it opened at the Aldwych on a Thursday, and my copy was due on Friday; hardly time to give the competition a thorough screening.

A week later (a week undisturbed by any theatrical experience remotely comparable) I am surer. There are of course much funnier plays in London, and one or two which probe deeper into private agonies. But only Bedroom Farce is, on its own enclosed territory, better organised; and there is nothing in town to touch Destiny for its excitement as a story.

It is, most importantly, the panoramic political play that writers of Mr Edgar's generation have been straining after for years. Often they have strained too hard; Howard Brenton (in the last major contender, Weapons of Happiness) was a poet trying to turn himself into a journalist. Mr Edgar has actually been a journalist and his play is, among other things, excellent reportage; dramatising a Midlands by-election he touches lightly but not superficially on the Tory and Labour campaigns before homing in on Nation Forward, the Fascist group who are his main concern.

By being so conscientious a journalist - by accumulating details - he actually achieves theatrical poetry, as he never did with the easy metaphors of his agit-prop plays. There is an uncommon charge here in the wry detached soliloquies through which his characters reveal themselves and their predicaments, and in the controlling myth of India.

It is of course an outrageous coincidence that four men, present together at the end of the British Raj, should all subsequently surface in the same suburb. But in a play as large as this some structural artifice is not only permissible but necessary, and as a metaphor for a play about the far Right in Britain today it is very pregnant. The National Front sprang in large part from the League of Empire Loyalists, and they did not choose their name lightly.

For once Marxism has inspired a playwright to generosity. Keeping his exploiters for the most part off-stage, Mr Edgar is able to see all his characters as victims: a limiting vision if he were writing tragedy, but liberating here. Sure of his own position, he can take time to understand everyone else's. Even the aspirant Hitler of Nation Forward has a biting speech (delivered with uncomfortable rasping power by Bob Peck) about the fears of an old man surrounded by immigrants. This rebounds ironically into the later scene - the best in the play - where Turner, the party's stooge-candidate (played by Ian McDiarmid with a marvellous blend of timorousness and jocularity), discovers that to be anticoloured is a breath away from being anti-Semitic.

Mr Edgar only cheats at the end when Major Rolfe, the officer funding the movement, is revealed as head of the concern which evicted Turner from his shop and destroyed his livelihood. Politically I can swallow this; dramatically the play breaks its own rules. No link is established between Rolfe the father grieving for his son killed by an Irish sniper, and Rolfe the grasper; he is simply kept out of sight which (at the Aldwych, far more than in the confined Other Place) means out of mind.

I should have commended last week the performances of Paul Moriarty, Greg Hicks, Cherie Lunghi and (also a King Lear) Paul Shelley; they all keep Mr Edgar's townscape alive and convincing. Mr Edgar's stance and his findings, incidentally, are similar to those of Martin Walker's book The National Front; we may hope that he overestimates the dangers, but we would be unwise to bank on it.

Moving not very far along: the key-line of Charles Marowitz's Variations on the Merchant of Venice (opening attraction at the new Open Space in Euston Road) is "If you wrong us shall we not revenge?," which Shylock utters on this occasion at the end of the trial scene, just before having his enemies mown down by heavily-armed allies from the Stern Gang. This is a moment of tremendous release; one has dreamed of seeing him get his pound of flesh, but here the stuff must run into hundredweights.

It is salutary to be reminded, while simmering down, that Zionist fanatics once blew up hotels and, like the PLO, thought themselves justified. In Robin Don's stoned and latticed set, the action unfolds, once you have accepted Venice as being a district of British-ruled Jerusalem, with a breadth and firmness unprecedented in Mr Marowitz's Shakespearean adventures.

But. There is still a great deal of The Merchant left over, and Mr Marowitz, though shuffling some lines and cutting others, retains it with no very clear purpose, other than the familiar one of making everybody seem as ludicrous and disreputable as possible. So Bassanio, leaving nothing to chance, plays all three of the casket scenes himself, the first two in disguise. (Had he struck lucky when done up as the Prince of Morocco, would he, I wonder, have been obliged to remain in black-face forever?)

But once we have giggled our way through the opening lines, these scenes are more boring than when played by actors under some obligation to find out what their author meant. Portia is a bored bitch, whose one racist line is pointed up by the presence of a black Nerissa. Mr Marowitz gives momentary pleasure by voicing all our subversive thoughts about the play, but this is not the same as illuminating it; and only with Shylock (well-mouthed by Vladek Sheybal) does he use the play - with an assist from Marlowe's more spectacularly machiavellian Jew, the one from Malta - to illuminate anything else: viz., the festering resentment of the persecuted.

A re-run of Four to One (Cottesloe), Gawn Grainger's tale of group violence over a backroom pool-table, underlines the punch and rhythm of the dialogue and the lack of a sustaining structure. The National's strong-arm boys are in great form; Mark McManus, in particular, has never seemed freer. The new victim is the formidable Warren Clarke, who puts up a great fight against the eponymous odds; his role has been re-written to advantage, though some of his thoughts on man's inhumanity seem, in the bloody circumstances, gratuitous.

Idi Amin, the leading character of Michael Hastings's For The West (Theatre Upstairs), keeps saying he likes Jews, and is rewarded by nervous laughter. The trouble with Amin (I mean the theatrical trouble; I'm aware that there are others) is that, for all our awful knowledge, we have been conditioned to think of him as a comic character with a script written jointly by himself and Alan Coren. Mr Hastings is not in this league; he is not trying to be, but he cannot escape its shadow either. Despite the remarkable simulacrum provided by Rudolph Walker (bullishness running to flab, heavily begonged), the character and the play are indeterminate, sliding by default into updated cloak-and-dagger.
