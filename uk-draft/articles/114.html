The naked and the bed

Robert Cushman in London and Stratford

THE title Carte Blanche, which might well have been bestowed on a musical version of A Streetcar Named Desire, is affixed instead at the Phoenix to a new revue, the long-delayed successor to Oh! Calcutta!

Revue is a form that has fallen into disuse in the past 15 years; its skills have rusted, and the rust is all over Carte Blanche. The endings of most of the sketches are visible some way in advance, and the foreknowledge is not as a rule encouraging. The strongest card of revue has always been diversity; but though this show employs a formidable muster of writers--from Paul Verlaine down to Molly Parkin--it confines them to a single subject. That the subject happens to be sex cannot in itself prevent monotony.

Nor is there the compensating virtue of a consistent point of view: each writer gets through as best he can, like a panel-game contestant requested to fill a given time on a given topic. Unthinking hedonist propaganda the show is certainly not; some of the numbers are very black. One of the more effective depicts the female body as a toothless, eyeless cadaver primped up to fool the eternally gullible male. Another concerns a geriatric orgy, and might theoretically earn marks for bravery, the sexual needs of the old being a subject we are generally reluctant to consider. The trouble is that the author shares our reluctance; treating his characters as zombies, he comes up with the feeblest item in the show.

Elsewhere sex is shown as something rather laughable and degrading, indulged in by the upper classes and showing them to be no better than the rest of us. It is never shown to involve affection, or indeed much emotion of any kind. Its dramatic possibilities are thus severely limited.

Flesh and blood actors on a stage have limited potential as fantasy material; they and their medium are so specific. The odds against seeing someone you fancy doing something you fancy are pretty heavy. It happened to me in Carte Blanche only once; in refusing further details I imitate the reticence of the authors who take no credit for particular scenes. I was also touched by one scene (a lesbian seduction blushingly narrated by Jean Warren in the best performance of the evening), amused by a couple of the jokes, and dazzled by some of the lighting effects--particularly the patterns projected on to the actors' bodies in the opening sequence.

Sado-masochism is in, homosexuality, despite the advance publicity, is not much emphasised except as an ingredient in threesomes, which are dwelt on frequently enough to suggest evangelism; Troilist and Crusader, perhaps. The hearty humours of Oh! Calcutta! have been left behind, but the new landscape has not been very clearly charted. In selecting material, Kenneth Tynan must have lowered his critical standards considerably; and neither the staging nor most of the performers reflect much credit on Clifford Williams's direction.

I drag in Mr Williams unfairly at the end to serve as a bridge. His celebrated Stratford staging of The Comedy of Errors has finally been replaced, but is still worth remembering, most of all perhaps for the maniac fervour with which Diana Rigg as the jealous Adriana berated her supposed husband (Alec McCowen) to be greeted, after 50 passionate lines or so, by the puzzled inquiry, 'speak you to me, fair dame?' In Trevor Nunn's new production at Stratford, Judi Dench preserves the intensity and Roger Rees the bewilderment and the scene is nearly as funny as ever. But while in Mr Williams's production it was one of a steady stream of laughs emanating from the text, in Mr Nunn's it is comparatively isolated.

Mr Nunn has turned the play into a half-cock musical; individual lines balloon into songs and dances after which we return to find the play exactly where we left it--except that we cannot quite remember where that was. With a story as intricate as this one that is fatal, and by the end I had lost interest; if the play were to work as a musical it would need a more ruthless adaptation, and a more distinctive score than Guy Woolfenden has provided. (Under the title The Boys from Syracuse, it has in the past received both.)

Many of the performances are pleasurable, and made more so by our awareness that this is the famous RSC letting its hair down. (The evening indeed depends on this knowledge.) Michael Williams as one Dromio is a virtuoso gagman, and Nickolas Grace as the other a fair singing-and-leaping match for him. Mike Gwilym makes a long piece of plotting into a farcical-forensic showpiece and Francesca Annis's Luciana is charmingly myopic. Richard Griffiths, an accident-prone Officer, is both the victim and the beneficiary of much invention. But the effects are not cumulative, and festive song and handshakes all round at the close--recalling other productions in and out of Stratford--are no compensation.

Meanwhile the RSC company continues to triumph at Stratford's Other Place, where David Edgar's Destiny is a wide-ranging examination of the far Right in Britain now. It begins simply, with the independence of India, whose returning British troops furnish Mr Edgar with many of his main characters; and it ends simplistically. But what comes between is astonishingly complex; no play of this generation has had so powerful a sense of the criss-crossing of ideologies. 'Drop the wog-bashing and it could be Tribune,' complains a National Socialist of the manifesto drawn up by a National Socialist.

The man for whom they have least time is the local Conservative, whose Labour opponent is brought by his wife to realise that racism cannot be laughed away; it exists in unfanatical British minds. A Left activist looks at a Right, and reports that it was like looking in a mirror. The spirit, and the peculiar passion, of the play are crystallised by an old officer who speaks of the terrible moment 'when you realise you have more regard for your enemies than those you view as friends.'

This speech, which is nearly crushed by tears, is the crown of a superb ramrod-spirited performance by Michael Pennington. Bob Peck as a Fascist visionary and Ian McDiarmid as a Fascist dupe add to their already remarkable records. Ron Daniels's production gives sharp and solid reality to a script short on human detail and even sometimes on narrative, but brilliantly adept in scenes of debate, manipulation and intellectual shock.

There is something of the same excitement in Joint Stock's Light Shining in Buckinghamshire (Theatre Upstairs). Caryl Churchill has delved into seventeenth-century records to uncover a revolution crushed almost at the birth of the middle-class. Her subjects are the extremist sects--Diggers, Levellers, Ranters--suppressed by the Cromwell, whose victory they had helped to win. As a set-piece we have the Putney Debate with Cromwell (played fairly and very squarely by Colin McCormack) listening, not unsympathetically, merely uncomprehendingly, to their suggestions and demands. (Can he believe his ears? Do they really want votes?)

Miss Churchill's linking-passages are a bit strained, so that one's thoughts turn to such matters as the decline in colloquial speech over three centuries, but she illuminates both the nature of Puritanism (restraint twin-brother to licence) and the irregular jumping course of history.
