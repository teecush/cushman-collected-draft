BAM goes Shakespeare

BAM is the pleasantly explosive shorthand used, on letterheads and occasionally in conversation, to denote the Brooklyn Academy of Music. This is actually an arts complex: easily accessible from Manhattan by subway, safely accessible by cab.

BAM is the current focus of one of the lesser-known American dreams: that of establishing a classical repertory company, in New York. Frank Dunlop, fresh from the Broadway triumph of Scapino, was put in charge of such an attempt a few years ago, but his reign was short-lived. BAM is now trying again under another British director, David Jones.

He has made a qualifiedly hopeful start. It was venturesome to begin with one of the trickiest of all Shakespeares, The Winter's Tale, and heartening that the audience seemed ready both to follow the story and to laugh at the jokes. They made the average British spectator seem blase. But as such a spectator myself, I was disappointed to find nothing -- except a lively and well-sung Autolycus from a black actor, Joe Morton -- that suggested a distinctively American approach to Shakespeare.

The production and design seemed merely pale replicas of standard Stratford practise; if one more RSC director introduces a rocking-horse into the early scenes at Leontes's court, people will begin to think that Shakespeare put it there. Expectedly, the two most authoritative performances came from the two players with the greatest Shakespeare experience: Marti Maraden (from Stratford, Ontario) as Hermione, and Sheila Allen, who injected some refreshing wit into Paulina's remonstrances, though I felt she erred in not wiping the smile off her face when "hastily married" at the end. The rest of the playing suggested that the actors chosen by Mr Jones from the resources offered by America's regional theatres needed more experience in the genre.

Hopes were raised by the company's second production. An American rep has to discover -- if necessary to invent -- American classics; and it has become clear that an endless series of O'Neill revivals is not enough. Vintage American farce (and, to a lesser extent, melodrama) is being explored; and BAM came up with a neglected example from the Forties, Johnny on the Spot, written by Charles MacArthur, for once without Ben Hecht.

Dealing with political chicanery in the South, the play lacks the forward drive of the Hecht-MacArthur Front Page, but is sustained by the same uplifting cynicism and by spurts of crazy invention. The state governor, a self-licensed autocrat presumably based on Huey Long of Louisiana, has died in a sporting-house on election day and his aides have to conceal the fact until the votes are in. (At one point they need the old boy's signature, and have to enlist the services of a champion forger, who has unfortunately got religion while in jail. Even the promise that he can sign his own pardon fails to move him from the paths of righteousness.) The production started slowly, but it was solid and by the end I liked it a great deal.

One of the attractions of the New York theatre, at least to a starry-eyed visitor, is its apparent homogeneity. The town seems aware of the theatre as London never does; there are recognisable theatrical restaurants. Even the uniformity of Playbill, the magazine programme sold at all Broadway theatres, is reassuring. There is a tourist audience, but it consists mainly of out-of-towners rather than foreigners (though there has been enough of a Japanese influx to prompt a revival of Oh! Calcutta!), so actors and playwrights still have some contact with the people to whom they are supposed to be talking.

Off-Broadway, at the Village Gate (a jazz-club, originally, now resembling a cavernous King's Head), I watched a revue called Scrambled Feet, devoted entirely to satirising the current theatre, and reflected that such a thing would now be impossible in London; you could never gather together enough people who would understand the jokes. This is our loss; a theatre needs to be able to laugh at itself. Scrambled Feet did so with great good humour; it was far less campy or inbred than I had feared, and had the grace to sympathise with the travails of audiences as well as actors.

One of its jokes was, perhaps temporarily, out of date; there is at the moment no British invasion of Broadway. The only British plays on view are Harold Pinter's Betrayal and Brian Clark's Whose Life Is It Anyway?, and both have American casts. Mr Clark's piece now has Mary Tyler Moore in the Tom Conti role. Night and Day and Filumena have both folded, though A Day in Hollywood, a Night in the Ukraine is about to open.

New York most resembles London in its paucity of new plays and musicals, and its dependence on revivals: often the same revivals. They have Oklahoma! and West Side Story, and are shortly to have My Fair Lady. A less expected treat is a musical version of Peter Pan, first staged 25 years ago. As a musical it is no particular shakes; even the choreography (originally by Jerome Robbins) is more energetic than inventive. But it benefits from the thoroughness and professionalism that New York expects in the genre, and which shames almost any children's entertainment (certainly any operating under this title) in London. The sets are smashing, the flying breathtaking (with an extra thrill saved for the curtain-call) and Sandy Duncan's gamin Peter, once you have got over her resemblance to Tom Sawyer, a joy.

The less acceptable face of nostalgia is represented by Sugar Babies, the hugely successful tribute to burlesque with Ann Miller and Mickey Rooney, which I visited in expectation of guilty uncritical enjoyment, but which doesn't even work in its own terms. There is a destructive imbalance in blowing up what was essentially a cheap and intimate form of entertainment to modern Broadway proportions at prices to match (and incidentally in amplifying the hell out of it). Near the end there's an animal act: a recalcitrant dog, with lugubrious master -- the only performers to make real contact with each other or the audience.

Animals are big at the moment, even small ones. The title-role in Scrambled Feet is taken by a black-tied duck, who shares the stage with an actress who warns her colleagues, in song, of the dangers of sharing a stage with such a creature. Strider, among the most esteemed new shows, is about horses' inhumanity to horse. It is an American version of a Russian play, based in its turn on a story by Tolstoy, and came to Broadway via the Chelsea Theatre Centre, one-time tenants of BAM. The beasts are mimed by actors, Taina Elg, as the mare of the unfortunate hero's dreams, is a really stylish high-stepper. The best and worst you can say for the enterprise is that it's cute.

I suspect that the most interesting play in New York is Lanford Wilson's two-hander, Talley's Folly, about the seduction of an impoverished daughter of an old American family by a self-satirising middle-aged Jewish accountant. London managements are reported to be queuing for it, and rightly.
