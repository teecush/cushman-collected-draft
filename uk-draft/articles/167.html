And 'Rosmersholm' makes five

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Ibsen, Shakespeare and Feydeau

IF statistics mean anything, the most important man in the West End theatre is Clifford Williams, director of five current productions. He is represented at the top end of the market by Wild Oats and The Old Country, at the bottom by Oh! Calcutta! and somewhere in the middle by Man and Superman and, as of last week, Rosmersholm (Haymarket).

No clinching conclusions may be drawn from this oeuvre other than that Mr Williams, like a good Briton, is better at plays than at musicals, happier with comedy than with tragedy, and conscientious about narrative. In his Rosmersholm you want at any rate to know what happens next, or, this being Ibsen, what happened last.

The production also boasts a striking portrayal by Frank Middlemass of Ulric Brendel; seizing on the line about this vagrant philosopher's association with actors, he turns him into a Scandinavian Crummles, fluent of hair and of tongue. He seems rather a wild mentor for the chronically sober Rosmer, but his two scenes ring out like nothing else in the play.

He amply performs the most crucial function of the Ibsenite intruder-figure--one coyly neglected in the text-books--which is to wake the audience up. As it happens the home he invades is more than usually soporific: this despite a distinguished Rosmer by Daniel Massey, shy, keen and bursting with conscience. The strongest thread in the play is his disillusion with the smug opportunism of the left, as with the defensive pomposities of the right. He still ends up, though, an undeniable sap; this may be inevitable, but I should still like to see a Rosmer played tough-mindedly against type, just as I should like to see a Kroll less stolid in his bigotry than Michael Aldridge's competent buffer. (Why not give him a touch of Ian Paisley? An Irish actor anyway might give the play a charge.)

The indications that Kroll is motivated by lust for Rebecca go for nothing here, as does the sexuality, and indeed the ambition, of Rebecca herself. Claire Bloom's performance is entirely neutral; she is a famous actress, Rebecca West is a famous role, and that's about it.

Perhaps she was intimidated by the sheer overloading of the play. Rebecca is a woman who drove a rival to suicide; she then feels remorse, as well she might. It hardly needs exposure to so elevated (and unspecified) a code as the Rosmer view of life to explain the fact. Ibsen in fact is engaging in double bluff; "the Rosmer view of life ennobles," so we have tragedy, "but it kills happiness," so we have a social drama about the evils of entrenched belief, and the two cancel each other out. The top dressing of incest, which so impressed Freud, merely adds to the confusion. Mr Williams tactfully dogs the footsteps of a play which needs to be firmly collared.

Ibsen usually gets higher marks than Shakespeare as a craftsman, but if Rosmersholm is the attempt, Coriolanus (Stratford) is the deed; and equally likely to confound its interpreters. A play so perfectly proportioned, so devoid of overflow, presents an unfriendly prospect. The central scenes of conflict between plebeians and patricians are an unrivalled dramatic picture of a society on the move, but the mass and energy of the text all too often contract and evaporate in the theatre. Terry Hands's production draws a neat diagram of the conflict, but turns a "mutiny" into a scrap. Perhaps the trouble is simply lack of manpower; anyway the production style is compact, typified by the presentation of the opening crowd as an orderly phalanx.

The play is ensconced in a familiar RSC mould (leathery costumes by Farrar) and Alan Howard's Coriolanus is a natural extension of his Henry V. From a man striving to turn himself into a war machine, he has become a war machine: also, and I mean no disparagement, a rhetoric machine. His clangorous tones are exactly tuned to this play's peculiar music, and some of his special effects--the echoing taunt of "Your voices," the incredulous long-drawn "sh" with which he picks up the presumptuous tribune's "Shall"--are thrilling, ingenious and appropriate.

One misses the excitement (as in Nicol Williamson's Coriolanus or Mr Howard's own Henry VI) of an actor going out to meet a role, and finding himself in the process, but that is the penalty of ideal casting. I have only two major cavils: (a) he fails to differentiate the happy warrior at Corioles from the disgruntled civilian whom he brilliantly embodies in Rome; (b) he gives little sign of the psychological wound that exposes him to his mother's entreaties--and Maxine Audley's Volumnia is not of sufficient stature to do the job alone.

The other actors group themselves round the leading man as the other characters round the hero. Jeffrey Dench's Cominius keeps an honest respectable distance, Graham Crowden's Menenius, though he scents one urbane possibility (which could be a director's touch), still gets insinuatingly close; only Julian Glover's Aufidius seems disposed to set up in competition, and, though commanding, he is canny enough to stay in his own less elevated yard. The tribunes, Tim Wylton and (especially good) Oliver Ford-Davies, go their own way, coolly toppling the protagonist before themselves succumbing to inflation.

Time was when the National Theatre offered the classiest laughs in London; what with Bedroom Farce and now The Lady from Maxim's (Lyttelton) time may be again. As a taxpayer I have no objection to subsidising Feydeau as rich, and as richly done, as this. There are perhaps no performances to compare in poetry with those of Geraldine McEwan and Edward Hardwicke in A Flea in Her Ear, though Mr Hardwicke is in this one too, very reliable if less hilarious without his cleft palate, and Sara Kestelman approaches Miss McEwan's standard of outraged propriety while adding an aggressiveness of her own.

She plays the pious spouse, subject to saintly visions, of a wild-living Paris doctor who gets entangled with a cabaret tart, whom he has to pass off as his wife in polite provincial society in order to appease his long-lost military uncle who believes ... I shall stop while I'm ahead, and commend Morag Hood's ebullience, Stephen Moore's pace, Michael Bryant's bluster (a turn-up for the casting directory), Timothy Davies's sketch of prolonged pubescence, Harry Lomax's welcome return as a dilapidated butler, and the drilling of Christopher Morahan's production, which was ruthless, doubtless.

Suicide in B Flat (Open Space) is one of Sam Shepard's doodles on American mythology, and more beguiling than most. A jazz musician may have killed himself, and the investigating fuzz suspect suicide due to creative exhaustion: "his improvisations were lasting for days on end." On the edge of the music and the detective story there transpires, inter alia, a Western; an arrow shot from one world transfixes a character in the next. Deborah Norton, as a lady bass player, again demonstrates her mastery of spaced-out soliloquy. Nobody else sounds quite right, but Christopher Benjamin's cop--bovine and juddery--looks absolutely marvellous.

Given a certain carbon-copy element which is inherent in the operation, the restoration is remarkably faithful.
