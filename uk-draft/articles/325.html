Jugular vein

Robert Cushman on rare Marlowe

The Glasgow Citizens' company, off-form in their recent work, bounce back characteristically with The Massacre at Paris, Christopher Marlowe's brusque celebration of the slaughter of St Bartholomew's Eve, 1572.

The play, likely Marlowe's last, has come down to us in mangled form, and was probably no paragon even when intact. Speeding through the adjacent reigns of three French kings, it devotes only its first half to its ostensible subject. Catholic thugs murder Protestant ninnies in a succession of brief, heartless scenes, masterminded by the Duke of Guise, whom the Elizabethans took to be the very model of a modern Machiavellian.

The action then continues more diffusely, but in the same vein (half jocular, half jugular); Guise continues to knock off all who have offended him until King Henry III knocks him off. A surviving brother then has the king killed, by a friar; Henry dies, denouncing the Papacy and paying devout respects to the Queen of England.

Philip Prowse's production seizes on these last compliments, and on the probability that the play as we have it is a text cut for touring purposes, to bring on Queen Bess herself, silently smiling from a balcony while the piece is performed for her by a touring troupe of limited resources. The Citizens' can be very scholarly when it suits them. I had heard that they were performing the play with a cast of four, which, for a play with 28 named characters, seemed perverse. In fact they have added three supers, but one of these is permanently occupied as Elizabeth.

They still play a full text, with much frantic and amusing quick-changing, helped of course by the fact that few people in this play stay around very long; it has the briskest death-rate in English drama. The production manages to place the play as a piece of Tudor propaganda while still entering fully into its spirit. Marlowe plainly regarded murder with schoolboy relish. Reprehensible it may be, but the play tells you more about the springs of atrocity than any more tasteful piece.

Acting in Glasgow seems to be looking up. Of the four principals, Steven Dartnell is rather stiff but John Breck neatly differentiates a decadent king who sniggers from one who merely shakes, and Jill Spurrier honks imposingly as all the women and several of the men, the latter including a bespectacled massacre victim who stammers out an eloquent summary of Protestant theology before being despatched. Robert Gwilym, one of the Citizens' recurring pale young men, is chilling and capable in the guise of Guise.

It remains a weakness of this company that they cannot cope coherently with long speeches, prose or verse, but Mr Prowse's set is of the expected richness: carpeted stairs sweeping down past the proscenium to be met by a series of black-draped plinths topped with candles. No other theatre in Britain would have the cheek to do this play so well.

Marlowe's glancingly sardonic dismissals of religion are considerably more effective than the more solemn pummellings administered by Nick Darke in Joint Stock's Say Your Prayers (Riverside Studios). Here the particular target is St Paul, seen converting a Roman slave with a weakness for the sound of his own voice (i.e., a born preacher) and incidentally disabusing him of the absurd belief that his girl-friend might be worthy of serious consideration.

Paul's other adversary is an atheistic girl artist in love with a frog; the frog comments on the action while sitting, like the one Danny Kaye used to sing about, on a lilypad. Paul thus stands convicted not only of sexism, but (the ugliest coinage yet in the progressive lexicon) speciesism.

Mr Darke doesn't go in for jargon and his debate might gather some rhetorical force if he didn't keep interrupting it, either with rock-numbers or with flashes to the present. Here we behold an American pop evangelist, enlisting God on the side of law and order, and a beleaguered English priest glumly awaiting a congregation of four, while--since he is plainly, in worldly terms, on the losing side--confusing the argument. We start thinking of other recent activities of the church: in El Salvador, for example--that might confuse it even more. As it is, Mr Darke's play appears as blinkered and arid as its target. It is in the main limply performed, though Philip Donaghy does a good fanatic's turn as Paul and Richard Howard is together as the object of his attentions.

Nikolai Erdman's The Suicide (Aldwych) is not the near-masterpiece it seemed two years ago at the Other Place. There is a lack of sustained brio in the blow-up of Ron Daniels's production, and a fuzziness in Peter Tegel's translation. But greatness is achieved in the last half-hour when Roger Rees as Podsekalnikov, driven to contemplate if not actually to take desperate measures by his failure either to find a job or learn the tuba, delivers his glorious tirade defying all ideologies and proclaiming the last human right: the right to say that life is hard.

There is now a beautiful physical as well as vocal fluency in Mr Rees's performance; watch him, in one arc of movement, bring a pistol heroically to his head and end up quietly depositing it in a packing-case. There is a similar command of instant gear-change in Susan Tracy's performance of his wife, uncertain whether or not she is his widow.

There is, too, a fair amount of amusement still in the Jonsonian crew of predators, each hoping to benefit (morally, of course) from the suicide's suicide. Sharpest among them are Edward Petherbridge, Ian McNiece and (one to watch) Nicholas Gecks. Some other performances are below RSC standard; at least I trust they are.

Suburban Strains (Roundhouse) is Alan Ayckbourn's musical. His lyrics cover the intended ground, though sometimes out of breath, and they rhyme when they mean to rhyme (a relief after some of those in Say Your Prayers), but they lack shine. Paul Todd's music seldom soars, and its most memorable phrase recalls one from Evita. (The idea of pastiche Andrew Lloyd Webber is a disturbing threat to the accustomed order of things.) But there is a quintet number 'What Do They Expect' that lifts the whole show.

The schoolmistress-heroine is glimpsed simultaneously at two stages of her life entertaining two different men, one of whom will become her lover. Meanwhile her estranged husband is seen with an old flame who may possibly be re-lit. All are asking the same eternal titular question. And it is clear that the presence of music, never mind its handling, has done something for Mr Ayckbourn; it has taken his chill off.

It hasn't made him sentimental (if you except a rather glib ending), but it has let his characters speak out, becoming surprisingly likeable in the process, and it has given a new glow to his durable furnishings.

In Hobson's Choice (Lyric, Hammersmith) Arthur Lowe, sounding very much like Les Dawson, is an unexpectedly gentle patriarch: often funny but his slow-burn timing infects the rest of the production. Ronald Pickup acts Willie Mossop impeccably, but since he is obviously nobody's worm there is not much surprise in his turning. Julia McKenzie is a commanding Maggie and the play is virtually indestructible.
