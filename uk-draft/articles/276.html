War, murders and gods

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'The Greeks.'

Despite the physical magnitude of the enterprise, John Barton's stated aims in his 10-play cycle, The Greeks (Aldwych), are modest: to retell the story of the Trojan war, as scattered across the surviving corpus of Greek drama, and to capture on a stage the spirit of Euripides, original author of seven of the 10 instalments.

He has succeeded magnificently in both. The story first: Part One, The War, begins with Euripides's 'Iphigenia in Aulis,' in which the Greek general Agamemnon sacrifices his daughter to the gods in exchange for a wind to blow his ships to Troy. Next comes 'Achilles,' Mr Barton's own adaptation of Homer, plugging the gaps that the playwrights left open; we see Agamemnon's quarrel with Achilles, the latter's consequent sulk, and his re-emergence to kill the Trojan champion Hector. Then the most familiar of all the plays, 'The Trojan Women.' Troy has been taken (destroyed, as in all RSC productions, by saturation attacks of dry ice) and its men killed. The women, led by Queen Hecuba, await their departure into slavery. We witness the killing of Hector's child, torn from the arms of his mother Andromache, to have his brains dashed out. This is the single most appalling act in the play, maybe in all drama; the other murders are acts of revenge, self-defence, perverted piety. This one is undertaken for cold reasons of state, and it can symbolise all the crimes that were ever committed in a national interest.

Part Two, The Murders, commences with 'Hecuba.' The queen has not been ennobled by suffering (it's difficult to think of anyone in these plays who is); she puts out the eyes of a Thracian king who killed her last surviving son. There follows Aeschylus's 'Agamemnon,' in which the returning hero is killed by his wife, Clytemnestra, partly in retaliation for the death of Iphigenia; and Sophocles's 'Electra,' showing Clytemnestra's death at the hands of her children Electra and Orestes.

In Part Three, The Gods, the jokers are dealt. In 'Helen,' the beautiful casus belli proves never to have been at Troy; an insubstantial look-alike took her place while she languished in Egypt, sunbathing on a sarcophagus, waiting to be rescued by her puzzled husband. (He duly arrives.) In 'Orestes,' Clytemnestra's assassins go berserk, killing Helen and threatening her daughter; chaos is averted by the descent of a smoothly double-talking Apollo, dealing out inconclusive happy endings. 'Andromache' reveals a bereaved heroine who has graduated beyond grief, and reunites a bewildering variety of characters. Finally 'Iphigenia in Tauris' pulls the rug from beneath the first calamity; Iphigenia was miraculously saved and is reunited with her brother, Orestes. The ending is qualified joy.

I must depend for my knowledge of Euripides on what translators tell me. Mr Barton and his literary collaborator, Kenneth Cavander, offer us a Euripides flip, angry, puzzled, enduring; never serene or piously accepting. This coincides with the view expressed by similarly enthusiastic scholars; and come to that with the baffled distaste of some others. I do not doubt that Mr Barton (quite apart from writing-in lengthy bits of his own) has gone beyond his author in some respects. Euripides seems to me to have written Helen as a housewife forced into the role of a siren; Mr Barton does her the other way round. It doesn't matter much. Either Mr Barton has reclaimed an extraordinary playwright, or he has invented one. Either way it's an achievement. And to have revealed so much of him within one piece of work; that really leaves you gasping.

The guest-shots by Aeschylus and Sophocles are the least successful (the famous scenes of pride and retribution are not lampooned, but there is little heart in them), so the resident writer is left unchallenged. Mr Barton has remorselessly ironed out narrative inconsistencies, but contradictions of tone and thought abound. The plays were written at various stages of a long career, and Euripides changed his mind.

But it is this quality of a sustained meditation that provides the power. There are shocking dislocations; finding Iphigenia alive at the end throws the whole experience into startling jeopardy; it is as if everything has happened, but nothing has. And seen in perspective the contrasts even out; the farcical mayhem of 'Orestes' and 'Andromache' (which are very advanced plays indeed) is no further from pure tragedy than the spectacle of the mad bitch Hecuba on all fours with her sightless quarry. And both follow logically from the reasoned killings of the first plays.

The progression of cause and effect is remorseless. 'Who is to blame?' ask the chorus in the very first line, and they're talking about the creation of the world. Different speakers blame different gods, and so it goes throughout. In fact when Apollo arrives and says to Orestes, 'What you did was right, and what Electra did was right,' he is, for all his infuriating silkiness, talking sense. There are no answers, and belief in the gods merely removes the uncertainty to another plane. To the familiar 'until he is dead call no man happy,' we might add, 'or miserable either.' Until they die people survive.

As adaptation 'The Greeks' is superior to (as well as more necessary than) Mr Barton's previous essay, 'The Wars of the Roses.' As production it is less accomplished -- which is forgiveable; there is no tradition to work from. The over-riding virtue is lucidity: The brandings and the clanking fetters of 'The Trojan Women' are the most memorable effects. The costumes begin classical and end modern; I found I could accept anachronism when it was comic (Helen's handbag and suntan oil for example), but not when it was designed to nudge me into noticing a perfectly obvious contemporary parallel (viz, Orestes and Electra as terrorists).

John Shrapnel excels himself as the vacillating Agamemnon and as Apollo, whose delivery suggests both a Python parody and Mr Shrapnel's own experience in the voice-over market. Janet Suzman's Helen (dismissing Aphrodite, purse-lipped, as 'sick and sexually obsessed') is brilliant, but her emoting as Clytemnestra, like Mike Gwilym's as Orestes and Billie Whitelaw's as Andromache, is routine. (I know that's hard, but so are the plays.) Eliza Ward's Hecuba bites as hard as she is bitten, Tony Church is a good fluffy Menelaus, and Peter Holmes as a messenger is a pleasant surprise. Lynn Dearth's uncompromising stridency is right for Electra, but it goes on.

In the long line of unfortunate princesses I also noted Deirdra Morris's Polyxena (in love with death, an interesting variant) and Avril Carson's flighty Hermione. The Chorus speak singly and, despite some atrocious individual readings, have never been so easy to listen to. The last play, done up in furs, is probably meant to remind us of that other enigmatic comedy of reconciliation, 'The Winter's Tale,' but the final dance that should tie it up is sadly below par.

I've left out too much. So has Mr Barton; there are at least three other plays he might have included. But we should both have time to make amends. This show should be around for years.
