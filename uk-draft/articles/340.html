The mad and the military

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a musical and a new play in Scotland.

PHILIPPE DE BROCA's version of 'King of Hearts' is a cult film, about a soldier who on the last day of World War One walks into a French village to find that everybody has fled except the inmates of the local asylum. He gets to love them because they have no interest in killing people, which makes a change from the circles in which he has recently been moving.

I have now seen the stage musical version. A Broadway flop a few years ago, it has been given its British premiere at the Lyceum, which is Edinburgh's rep. Enterprising of them to try a new musical, but this one is a horror.

You can guess from contemplating that story-line the kind of writers who are going to want to put songs into it and what kind of songs they are going to put. When our doughboy hits town, the first person he meets is a cute mute. The other merry villagers are glimpsed playing games in white nightshirts as in nursery 'The Marat-Sade.' They also sing, rhyming 'scandalous' with 'abandoned us,' twice. Later they have a number called 'Deja vu'; the lyricist (Jacob Brackman) keeps flourishing his title-phrase, as if pleased with himself for being able to speak foreign.

The loveable lunatics start their own brothel, and the hero finds himself in love with a very young virgin whore. The madam warns him against being 'nice' (one moment more and she would be using really filthy words like 'bourgeois'). The crowd choose him as their king and he decides to stay with his subjects, there being apparently only two kinds of people--the mad and the military.

Why do men who have made a pile in the pop market feel entitled to hit us with the virtues of nonconformity? The tone, predictably, is sententious and sentimental. The music (Peter Link) has its pretty passages, but lacks cohesion; with no style of its own--let alone any sense of period--it wanders from one more or less modern mode to another.

The recurrent sound is a woeful compromise between soft rock and 1950s ballad; what the music press calls something like contemporary-adult-crossover-(mind-the-traffic)-middle-of-the-road: simplest perhaps to think of it as hyphenated music. It has no contours, and it's killing the musical theatre. It accounts for the worst bits of 'Evita,' 'Annie,' 'Cats' and 'A Chorus Line' and for nearly all of 'They're Playing Our Song' and 'I'm Getting My Act Together.' Those shows have, bar the last, enough going for them for us to turn a deaf ear; they are sophisticated, but 'King of Hearts,' as exposed at Edinburgh, is the thing itself, unaccommodated pap. The production has a resourceful lead singer in Martin Smith and a first-rate mime in John Moreno.

Up the road at the King's I saw the new Scottish Theatre Company in Civilians (this week they play Glasgow). Bill Bryden's play, self-directed, shows us Greenock during the war. It is excellently acted in the best Scots manner (hot and strong, with the laugh lines indecently well timed), predictably evocative, frequently funny (my favourite line was about somebody 'saving up to vote Tory'). But it is also aimless, so that its very virtues start to tell against it: both the jokes and the nostalgia come too easy.

Situations and characters are thin and foreseeable: a soldier's wife, first faithless, then pregnant, then bereaved; her air-raid warden dad (Fulton Mackay) a tartar with a heart o' gold. At the end it becomes another play about the lost electoral hopes of 1945. That--from a playwright who has kept his characters boozing, scrounging and amateur operaticising for three hours--is shamelessness worthy of O'Casey.
