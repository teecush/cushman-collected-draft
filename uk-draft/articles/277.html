Lousy sausages

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN returns to Greek territory.

IN Steven Berkoff's Greek (Half Moon) a London couple are warned by a fairground gypsy that their son Eddie will grow up to kill his father and make love to his mother.

So, when he is a young man, Eddie is cast out from the room in which he has been accustomed to curl up with available girls or Stan Kenton records. Deciding against flying abroad he strays into a cafe, gets into a fight with the manager, kills him, and dutifully sets about consoling the widow.

From his new position of catering eminence he rises to great wealth, incidentally dispelling the plague which is ravaging the city, if not the country, at the time, and which he sums up as 'sloth, inertia, lousy sausages.' Later he kills a sphinx which seems to represent militant feminism. After this he rather belatedly invites Mum and Dad over. They reveal that he is not really their son; they found him. Their description of the finding leaves Eddie's lady in no doubt that he is actually her long-lost. Eddie declines to blind himself.

Some of this must sound familiar. The sharp-eyed, though, will have spotted at least one crucial, and destructive, departure from the Sophoclean source. It was Oedipus's real parents who heard the prophecy, and accordingly abandoned him. His adoptive ones would have had nothing to fear. The story no longer makes sense; the plague is no longer linked to royal guilt. In Mr Berkoff's scheme there is no guilt, and no king. The plague is just there; and it is unlikely that a restaurateur, however virile, could have such power over it.

And what is a sphinx doing in London, apart from providing a gloss on the values of the new version? Eddie's answer to her riddle again departs from tradition. Man goes on three legs in the evening not because he is old and needs a stick, but because he has an erection. That's potent enough to finish off any sphinx.

Mr Berkoff cleverly directs a cast of four who sit white-faced round a table and play everybody in Mr Berkoff's own style; Barry Philips as Eddie is a particularly trenchant surrogate. As author, Mr Berkoff (writing, as in East, in joky blank verse) has an equally firm control of sexual and scatological imagery and rarely--apart from an entertaining sequence about the difficulty of getting your coffee and cheesecake served at the same time--tries anything else.

Sometimes he decides it's time to write lyrically and at great length about love; Mr Philips then drops his Cockney and starts talking what used to be known as BBC, which is a dead giveaway. (It's strange--well no it isn't--how apt our supposedly revolutionary theatre workers are to come up with literary drama at its deadliest.) There are odd spurts of self-pity, but at least there is none of that compassion nonsense with which other writers are inclined to fill the occasional holes in their aggression.

Greek, being skilled, was, mind you, the brightest thing of the London week.

Before glancing at the surrounding debris I feel like expressing my bewilderment over the reception of the longer Athenian business at the Aldwych. John Barton, unlike Mr Berkoff, had me recanting my lifelong prophylactic against pretentiousness ('Beware of gifted directors bearing Greeks'), but others seem to have resisted, and for odd reasons.

In the New Statesman Benedict Nightingale described Helen as 'a superfluous anecdote' and blamed Mr Barton for including it. He might more straightforwardly have blamed Euripides for writing it. But he did write it; therefore it formed part of his vision of the Trojan War, the vision that this production is supposed to be presenting to us. Nor is it true that, as James Fenton said in the Sunday Times, 'the idea that the Helen of Troy was a wraith postdates most of the plays which we are shown and contradicts their spirit.' It was Euripides's Electra, written--according to textbooks to hand--a year earlier, makes explicit mention of it.

And why does Mr Fenton call Helen a satyr-play? Satyr-plays are quite easily recognisable; they have choruses of satyrs. We have one Euripidean example, the ripely farcical Cyclops, which also counts as part of the Trojan story and which Mr Barton might well include next time round. It would in no way spoil the painful emotion of, say, The Trojan Women; I fail to see how, once you have been moved, anything can take the experience away from you. To be able to sustain two emotions simultaneously is a sign of maturity; must we assume that the Greeks couldn't manage it?

Two more recent foreign classics have been dismally treated. Goldoni's Country Life (Lyric, Hammersmith) is the tripartite comedy about romantic entanglements turning bitter on vacation, which was patchily but powerfully done at the Glasgow Citizens' last year. This production uses the Glasgow translation (by Robert David Macdonald), which is a trap. It mingles standard archaisms with camp quotations, ranging from Polonius's 'wrung from me my slow leave' to Beatrice Lillie's 'two dozen double damask dinner napkins.' It requires either complete innocence on the part of its performers or total command.

David Giles's cast flounder somewhere between the two, with the exception of Ellen Pollock, who battles through as an Italian Lady Wishfort. Julia Foster also has one poignant moment of direct address to the audience. The pace is brisk but, through lack of underpinning, still gives the impression of sloth.

Paul Marcus's production of Hauptmann's The Weavers (Roundhouse) seems slow because it is: an apparently demoralised cast leave nervous pauses before every speech. The play deals--in terms which a brace of hectoring programme notes think is naturalistic, but they can't quite be sure--with a workers' uprising in Silesia in the 1840s. They're downtrodden all right; they live in pits sunk into the stage, which means the audience never get to see or hear them properly, and they are hardly livelier when they get to walk about. But the production is even-handed and allows the oppressors to be every bit as tedious.

There are some colourless performances in Marcel Achard's Rollo (Royal Exchange, Manchester), but with Leo McKern in the driving seat this 20-year-old comedy still puts on a good turn of speed. Mr McKern, bubbling over as a character and superbly controlled as an actor, plays a life-long failure whose survival depends on his envious contempt for a rich school-friend. He finds the friend to be his daughter's seducer: splendid, now he has the man at his mercy. But he cannot decide what to do with him once he is there; having your enemy on his knees soon palls.

The play spends a little too long running on the same sore spot, but it produces a good many nervous twinges along with the laughs, and Mr McKern, with Jack Hedley as his cool antagonist, floods every defeat and every triumph with comic energy.
