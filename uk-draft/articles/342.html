Just two

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

THE SAMUEL FRENCH drama catalogue has traditionally offered help to amateur casting-committees by publishing succinct estimates of a script's manpower requirements: as it might be, 'The Two Gentlemen of Verona' (12m, 3f, 1dg, supers), or 'The Caretaker' (3m, no doubling), or perhaps in some future edition David Rudkin's seven-hour study of incest and colic in ancient Cardiff, 'The Misconceived' (63 indeterminate).

Playwrights have recently taken to supplying these necessary statistics in their titles. Americans have been especially informative. A recent musical began its life off-off-Broadway as 'Four Jews in a Room Bitching,' though when success and transfer struck, it opted for the blander 'March of the Falsettos.' ('So we know their range and their rhythm, but how many do you need?')

The trendsetter here has been 'A Coupla White Chicks Sitting Around Talking,' which could qualify as a generic title. It would do for several recent plays, and certainly for Mayo Simon's These Men (Bush), whose name is downright mendacious. The only characters are Shelly and Cloris. Shelly, in her late thirties and self-conscious about it, lives in the Los Angeles suburb of Laurel Canyon, theoretically with an endless succession of easily disposable boy-friends; that means that in practice she lives alone. Cloris, younger, shyer and prone to calling her psychiatrist at unsocial hours, comes to stay, in flight from her own lover, whom Shelly shoos from the door with a shotgun (though he never, for reasons connected with symmetry and economics, comes remotely within range).

Cloris is prim and house-proud and keen that her boeuf bourguignonne be appreciated, while Shelly is a raunchy slob who will take TV-dinners from the freezer and eat them unthawed. Much of their conversation is devoted to strategies either for getting men or doing without them. So we have a female version of 'The Odd Couple,' with the important addition of sexual explicitness. (This is purely verbal, and has its limits; Shelly, who narrates, refuses to tell us whether they become lovers; that's none of our business. At the time I thought she was copping out; in retrospect I think she has a right.)

What happens is unsurprising. Shelly gives Cloris advice on picking up men, though it's obvious that her own promiscuity leaves her just as desolate as her room-mate's celibacy; Cloris gets involved with one of Shelly's stable; the girls decide to settle for each other; eventually habit drives Shelly to destroy the menage. But the predictability does no harm; it provides a cast-iron frame for the exceptional warmth and buoyancy of the writing. If the plot reinforces Shelly's contention that 'all women are whores,' the texture of the writing denies it. (I'm not sure that the jokes, delightful in context, will bear extraction.) The play has been impeccably directed by Sarah Pia Anderson in a pine-timbered set by Sue Plummer so apt that it took me half the play to register it. Nicky Croydon is both amusingly and touchingly tense as Cloris, while as Shelly, Stephanie Fayerman hits exactly the right tone, an odd blend of strident and confidential, for the part, the space and the play.

What she actually gets is the amused naturalism I think of as Louisville style. It shone through a quartet of short plays the Louisville Actors Theatre brought to the Toronto Festival: most notably 'Chocolate Cake,' another female duet, this time for a pair of maritally frustrated compulsive munchers, whose feminism is more explicit than that of 'These Men,' but whose unforced funniness compound the evidence for a modest revival in American drama.

Toronto has remained on my mind with the arrival in London of two small Canadian musicals, each a vehicle for a single magnetic performer. Both display the naivete which can be a strength of Canadian theatre (it leads to shows getting on, in great numbers), but which can hamper its appeal outside. Billy Bishop Goes to War (Comedy) is about a World War One air-ace with an unrivalled record as a hit-man; its opening lyrics -- 'we were off to fight the Hun/And it looked like lots of fun/somehow it never seemed like war at all' -- are simply but hauntingly set and suggest irony. But it never materialises; ambiguities are bypassed, the evolution of a hero-killer unexamined.

The real preoccupation is with the alternate petting and patronising of a colonial figurehead by the British aristocracy; Eric Peterson, playing everybody, does a capital job on them. An actor of great delicacy, he takes full advantage of the show's strategy, which resembles that of the high command: put Bishop up there and let him get on with it. One of his transformations -- a smoky chanteuse -- he brings off simply and brilliantly by an arch undulation of his shoulders.

Supporting him as author, composer, director, narrator and pianist (he likely plays piano better than any other living playwright) is John Gray. The great Canadian musical will probably precede the great British one, and Mr Gray might conceivably write it, if he stays off intractable subjects and monotonous presentations. A previous show, 'Eighteen Wheels,' was about long-distance truckers; though it was full of exhausting monologues, it also included a pungent lament on 'hauling chicken-guts in the hot August sun,' which included the sobering reminder that 'someone has to haul the parts that ain't so finger-lickin' good.'

An immortal moment in 'The Blues Brothers' (a film not bursting with immortal moments) had a barmaid explaining that her establishment featured 'both kinds of music -- Country and Western.' The implicit monotony is hardly avoided in Hank Williams -- The Show He Never Gave, which comes to the Criterion from Vancouver via Liverpool and Islington, though Williams's lyrics (not his music) were cuts above the standard whining of the genre.

Maynard Collins's script intersperses the songs with biographical material dispensed by Carl Chase in a cracked twang more unnerving than Williams's song. But he adds to it an extraordinary haunted presence; when he says that playing one-nighters 'is as close as you can get to hell on earth,' you believe him. The sound he makes at moments of high emotion is extraordinary: between a belch and a croak.

Miriam Karlin's suggestion that the Old Vic be turned into a multi-racial theatre is the best idea anybody has had for that theatre since the National left: it should make the Arts Council think again about its determination to take on no new clients. Meanwhile Ed Berman's examination of that body, Show Trial (Tricycle), suffered from the compulsion to question, explain and entertain all at the same time. Mr Berman himself makes a ferocious DA (appearing as a witness, I felt in definite need of Perry Mason), but a straight inquiry format might have accomplished more.
