Betrayed by nerves

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London, Glasgow and Liverpool

MY EARS may have deceived me, but almost my first impression at Tuesday's premiere of Richard II (Aldwych) was of Alan Howard in the title role addressing his uncle Gaunt as 'old John of York, time-honoured Lancaster,' a dynastic confusion that if spotted in time might have prevented the Wars of the Roses.

As the evening progressed Mr Howard did other weird things to the text; sometimes, after a misplaced word or omitted line, you could see him worrying about whether the speech would still make sense or whether the audience would notice. (It didn't, and neither of course did they).

These temporary aberrations jibed uncannily with what seems to be Mr Howard's abiding view of Richard: a man who regards kingship as a license to say and do outrageous, even meaningless things, and get applauded for them, but is cripplingly uncertain as to whether he can carry it off. This Richard insults Gaunt on his death-bed as if he were doing a turn, then turns to Bushy, Bagot and company apparently asking 'Didn't I do well?'

The nervousness betrays him. Before he has said a word, true or false, we see him in this production trying on his crown with an expression of fascinated distaste; plainly he is not getting job-satisfaction but he also sees no prospects in alternative employment. Then he turns to confront an impassive row of courtiers. (Gaunt is actually sitting down; you have to be really time-honoured to get away with that).

The ensuing scene, the quarrel between Bolingbroke and Mowbray, is brilliantly handled. Richard is almost in control, unleashing contempt on both contestants from behind a mask of royal raillery. David Suchet's Bolingbroke is playing his own version of the same game, unwearyingly polite to his king while plainly hating him.

The development in counterpoint of these two performances is the great strength of Terry Hands's production. Bolingbroke's ambition dawns as Richard banishes him: 'such is the breath of kings.' When Richard is virtually his prisoner he sends an ultimatum beginning grandiloquently with his own name: 'Henry Bolingbroke . . .'--here Mr Suchet breaks off for a moment, bemused and excited. (Hark at me, I'm talking royal). He has found himself; but so for an ironic moment has Richard, in the 'hollow crown' speech which casts a silence over the house of a different quality from the respectful hush which otherwise prevails. You understand why Richard is so angry at being diverted from his sweet road to despair: for him it has been the true meaning of kingship. All that remains is to teach it to Bolingbroke, which he does in the deposition scene, loading him with regalia, teasing him on to the throne.

The legacy is felt through the remainder of Mr Suchet's performance; the atrocious couplets of his last scene ('thy pains, Fitzwater, shall not be forgot/right noble is thy spirit, well I wot') are delivered as if in a daze, which is doubtless how they were written. But though Bolingbroke can reasonably be played to the end as a political case-study, Richard, like it or lump it, is a tragic hero; and his deeper triumph 'in being nothing' is one that this production has no room for.

It is otherwise distinguished by the friezes of Farrah's set, a lament for the passing of the Middle Ages, and by variously effective stabs at comedy. The best are in Tony Church's York, though even he is too knowingly conversational at times; more open to question are the gardeners' sending-up of the Queen, and Northumberland's rattling-off of the names of his co-conspirators, perhaps in settlement of a dressing-room bet. At such moments I wonder whether current Shakespeare is much improvement on, say, the Old Vic of 1959, which was where and when I first saw this play. Mind you, the Old Vic in 1959 was pretty good.

Dryden wrote a comedy called Marriage a la Mode which is half realistic sex-play in the customary Restoration prose vein, and half high romance in verse. The Glasgow Citizens' Theatre has inspected the text, kept the prose and discarded the verse, replacing it with a condensed version of Dryden's Antony and Cleopatra tragedy All for Love.

We are supposedly present at a Restoration rehearsal of this, done with toy-theatre attitudes through which real emotion occasionally, and with disconcerting effectiveness, breaks. Between scenes the actors step down and--as in the film of The French Lieutenant's Woman--conduct their own intrigues, in the words bequeathed them by Dryden's comedy. These words turn out to be extraordinarily good; if Restoration comedy is a meditation on marriage, then this is its high-point, exceeded only by Congreve in elegance of phrasing but with far more immediate impact. Wires are crossed between two gallants, the wife of one and the mistress of another. Their animadversions on the merits and disappointments of sex before, during and outside of wedlock, convulsed the audience with the laughter of recognition.

The ladies are given performances of sharp, controlled extravagance by Julia Blalock and Sian Thomas, with Rupert Frazer as a casually sardonic raisonneur. In the imposed theatrical setting an innocent quotation from Macbeth has, elaborately and hilariously, to be exorcised. This is one of the best Citizens' productions I have seen, though, and, mysteriously, the first to be directed by the theatre's chief, Giles Havergal.

From the old firm at Glasgow to the new one at Liverpool where the Playhouse has been taken over by a quadrumvirate of playwrights, most prominent among them Chris ('Sweeney Todd') Bond. He has co-director credit, with Pip Broughton, on A Tale of Two Cities adapted, it says, by the company. This, not to shirk the obvious comparison, is a far more hurried work, both in playing and rehearsal time, than the RSC's Nicholas Nickleby, and has less of a theatrical identity.

Some scenes are picture-book illustrations but others come alive: the storming of the Bastille with the whole cast on scaling-ladders urged on by Gillian Hanna's Madame Defarge; the same actress periodically crossing the stage with bits of Dr Manette's narrative (an inspired linking device); anything involving Chris Darwen as Jerry Cruncher; and, inevitably and infallibly, Sydney Carton (Alan David) taking the Only Way. (The audience are sent out to the strains of Frank Sinatra singing My Way, which suggests that Mr Bond is being cheeky, or just irresponsible.)

I suspect that the play started out with more serious thoughts about the French Revolution than were eventually realised but then so did Dickens. There is an invigorating medley of theatrical conventions employed as the need arises; and splendid life-giving sets by Voytek, sometimes (as in the trial scenes) reinforcing the cartooned action with Hogarthian malevolence, at others, as in an extended recap of the Doctor's tale, framing it with perfect artifice.

An even less manageable novel is far more neatly done in Richard Crane's version of The Brothers Karamazov which inaugurates a new policy of fringe transfers at the Fortune. The cruciform set is impressive now that it's fully visible, and Will Knightley's, who has taken over Ivan, adds a new dimension by looking like Stephen Boxer who plays Alyosha.

Seamus Finnegan is a playwright worth encouraging, though in Soldiers (York and Albany, Camden Town) his kaleidoscopic method of presenting Belfast is beginning to come apart on him. Stark scenes for British squaddies or IRA men are betrayed by a stereotyped presentation of 'the media' (i.e. television) and a suggestive but excessively laconic view of the Church. Good acting by Sam Dale and Derek Thompson.
