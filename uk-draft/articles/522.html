Theatre's living-isms

Robert Cushman · The Daily Telegraph · Arts · 1994-03-12
Opinion Piece
People
DirectorPeter BrookDirectorRichard EyrePlaywrightHarold PinterArtistMichael Billington

HAROLD PINTER and Peter Brook have gone beyond being legends in their own lifetimes. They are myths.

There hardly seems to have been a time when Pinter was merely a successful playwright. One moment - 1958, actually - The Birthday Party, his first full-length play, was a three-performance flop savaged by nearly every critic. The next - well, almost the next, 1960 - his second full-length play, The Caretaker, was an instant classic, and incontestably The Play To See In London.

No subsequent British play has quite achieved that status, and no subsequent British playwright has been accorded the same cultural authority. We talk occasionally of something being Stoppardian or Ayckbournian, but never with the same confidence, the same certainty of being understood, as when we dub an action, a line, a moment - in life or art - Pinterish or Pinteresque. The man is a fact of English literature.

This can make meeting him in the flesh daunting. When he first spoke to me - I don't think I would have had the nerve to speak first to him - it was a little like being accosted by T. S. Eliot. There was this thing abroad, this body of work called Pinter, and you didn't expect to encounter it in a suit. You certainly didn't expect it to start quizzing you about your own work, which is what he did to me, asking me to clarify something I had said in a column the previous week. He really seemed to want to know. I felt elated.

When it comes to talking about his own work, Pinter has been famously unforthcoming. His own reticence has fused in the general imagination with the celebrated opacity of his plays; their refusal to supply what others would regard as essential information.

This was what most infuriated critics about The Birthday Party and, it seemed, what most impressed them about The Caretaker. The turnaround was lightning-fast; it was eased by a TV production of The Birthday Party that played up the sinister ballooning cross-talk of the dialogue to the point of gimmickry, but was totally riveting.

The concept of Pinterism was born, I suspect, in that production, which prepared a large audience for the subtler virtuosity of The Caretaker whose star role - the lying, scheming, pitiable tramp Davies - may be the last great character creation in British drama ("You were in the colonies, weren't you?" "I was over there, I was one of the first over there"). He takes on independent life through his author's mastery of language: his ability to take familiar speech rhythms and make them sing, to build up long tirades and crown them with catchphrases.

This mastery of tone remained when Pinter - perhaps reflecting his own material success - moved into glossier stage surroundings; the speeches of Spooner, the poetaster of No Man's Land, are quirky and elegant at once, and frostily witty. No other current playwright writes as well; he established his authority by insisting, almost to the point of perversity, that his plays speak for themselves and by giving them a triumphant voice to do it in.

Pinter, however the academics may celebrate or condemn him, is an exceptionally adroit nuts-and-bolts playwright, who works through comedy and suspense. Peter Brook, who ranks among theatre directors as the guru's guru, has a similar command of showbiz values. In his youth, he directed musicals, drawing-room comedies, and pictorial Shakespeares, and even the most ascetic or exotic of his more recent works are designed to have maximum impact as shows.

The Ik, his study of a starving and predatory African tribe, was as casually stark as he could make it. Ubu Roi, the foundation stone of the Theatre of the Absurd, was as anarchically funny as he could make it. Both displayed a very light touch. These latter-day Brook productions originated from his International Centre for Theatre Research, a multi-ethnic troupe based at the Theatre des Bouffes-du-Nord in Paris. There is a sense of Brook holding court, either in the theatre or the cafe opposite; he enjoys being a sage.

The Bouffes-du-Nord is an old suburban theatre with much of its interior torn out. It looks at once the essence of all theatres and the negation of one: the paradox is obviously intentional and very Brookish. He seems sometimes to want to get away from the idea of theatre altogether, and once took his actors on a tour of African villages, playing to people who had never seen anything approaching the European idea of drama.

The trip was much publicised, and widely attacked as a piece of fake primitivism. But Brook has never claimed that what he was doing was superior to playing to bourgeois audiences, in orchestra stalls, just that it was different and might shed some light on the irreducible essence that keeps audiences looking at actors in the first place.

Brook started the tradition of the brilliant post-war just-graduated director, a line over which he still presides. He still seems like a prodigy, having been the premier director in the British theatre probably since 1946 when, at 21, he directed a ravishing Love's Labour's Lost at Stratford.

His austere Measure for Measure and horror-comic Titus Andronicus were the leading Stratford productions of the Fifties; and, in the Sixties, he moved into the reconstituted Royal Shakespeare Company with a monumental King Lear - you could practically smell greatness on it - and with The Marat-Sade, a historical-philosophical piece which he staged like a musical.

In 1970, came the legendary Midsummer Night's Dream which Brook described as his return, after a spate of fearsome Vietnam-era productions, to the light.

It was also virtually his farewell to Britain. But, despite two decades of apparent exile, everybody thinks of him still as part of the British theatre. He never went away, just as Pinter, despite writing little more than playlets in the Eighties, was never seriously thought to have dried up. They still set the standard.
