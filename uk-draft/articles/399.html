In love with 'Earnest'

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN
in London and Exeter.

I always knew the National Theatre production of The Second Mrs Tanqueray would come in useful. Pinero's piece is the best walking example of late-Victorian society drama, the kind of play at which Wilde had three ill-fitting attempts before settling on The Importance of Being Earnest for burlesquing all its conventions.

Last year at the Lyttelton, Aubrey Tanqueray was brought by a long, hard road to recognise the immorality of there being one law for men and another for women. So it's all the more refreshing to find Jack Worthing, on the same stage, reclaiming those same sentiments to the woman he momentarily believes to have borne him.

Insufficient tribute has been paid to Mr Worthing's nobility at this moment; by so enthusiastically embracing a governess as his mother he surely denies himself any hope of gaining his Gwendolen, guarded as she is by the most snobbish of dragons.

The first-night audience often laughed, as one claps the opening bars of a favourite song, before a line. That is the trouble with the play. One falls in love with it, memorises it, and then can never respond spontaneously: except in certain scenes -- the girls' tea-party squabble, most notably -- where the humour is situational, rather than just verbal.

The construction is of course superb: that handbag, for example, which not only successfully satirises farce (an almost impossible achievement), but allows Lady Bracknell to reveal herself, and her class, in their truest colours. Wilde lets his characters condemn themselves out of their own mouths, and for 70 per cent of the time he does it brilliantly. The rest is mechanical inversion, with some scenes, such as the courtship of Algy and Cecily, where the satire runs irredeemably thin.

Peter Hall offers a straightforward production which wins more than it loses. Its prime attraction is Judi Dench's Lady Bracknell: a source at the premiere of quite remarkable tension. Everybody loves Miss Dench; nobody could quite see her in so unsympathetically formidable a role. We waited with bated breath for her solution, and our joy when she revealed it knew no bounds.

Physically, she makes no concessions to gorgondom; she is trim, and immaculately preserved. What terrifies is her vocal manner, which is hard and fast and dismissive. (She talks of Lord Bracknell like Edna Everage discussing her prostrated, prostated, Norm). Really, we were there to hear how she would say 'a handbag'; she does it quietly, as if morally outraged, thoughts on the French Revolution already fermenting. Only the suggestion of marital (i.e. financial) eligibility will soften her; sofas are patted, and room made for the lucky winner. Every laugh Miss Dench gets (and she misses only one, through mis-stressing the line about 'the social habits of Markby, Markby and Markby') enhances her portrayal of a blinkered, therefore all-powerful, woman.

This is probably the best acted Lady Bracknell there has ever been. (Edith Evans's was a Happening.) And somehow, through sheer technical prowess, this uncompromising portrayal floods the auditorium with more warmth than has been generated by any actress since Miss Dench played O'Casey's Juno. But then, we all wanted her to win.

Anna Massey's Prism is perfection, Paul Rogers's Chasuble, with his lolloping walk and eternal puzzled silences, scarcely less so. These (along with the Jeevesian Lane, well done by Brian Kent) are the sure-fire roles, bred from healthy stock and invariably cast with actors who have had time to master the manner. With the youngsters you take more of a chance. Martin Jarvis's Jack is the best here, a predestinate Ernest. He has to leave the sparkle to Algy; Nigel Havers, who promises but does not deliver. Zoe Wanamaker is a fierce Gwendolen, not just a chip but the whole old block, and her idea of passion is beautifully posed, but she sometimes punches too hard. Elizabeth Garvie as Cecily may not punch enough.

John Bury's sets move oddly from real to stylised to in-between; but his geometric arrangement of furniture in the last act allows Sir Peter some satisfyingly calculated grouping. The end is messy, though.

Some of what Wilde was lampooning survives in Coward's Bitter Sweet, long unseen but revived at the Northcott, Exeter, prior to a tour. This operetta is really the story of a song: a conceit (both senses) that Coward justified by coming up with 'I'll See You Again.' At the close the young man, to whom our heroine, Sari, has related her sad story of lost love and old cabaret turns, simply says 'what a tune' and rushes off to play it. Art has conquered life, leaving Sari with her memories and another reprise.

I am not sure that this moment compensates for what precedes; much of it flashback. Sari's tale is simple, and wilfully pathetic. Wit has been largely banished, erupting only in odd speciality numbers. The score includes 'Zigeuner' and 'If Love Were All' (the talent-to-amuse one), and many ensembles, the best of them an Act III set of regrets by the married women who were young gels in Act I. But the songs rarely forward the story; nor do they qualify, here, as events in their own right.

The two best performers depart too quickly: Richard Muenz as the hero and Jill Martin as Manon, the humble diseuse for whom life is very rough-and-tumble. Jan Hartley cannot cope with the acting demands of Sari; who is required to stand idly by while her husband virtually commits suicide in a duel and then to throw herself on the corpse sobbing, 'I'll always love you.' What stops you laughing is that Coward obviously believed it.

Whenever I am summoned to see dance, because 'it's really theatre,' I assume I am in for something violent. Not so with Pina Bausch's Tanztheater Wuppertal: not, at least, in 1980 (Sadler's Wells). This is described as a Proustian meditation on youth and age. Proustian, as understood in Glasgow: there is a verbal dinner-scene, very spread-out and seductive in the Citizens' manner. Youth and age; well, anything fits into that scheme, some bits more obviously than others.

We get, on the soundtrack, Judy Garland singing to her dog about the land over the rainbow, followed by the audience baying for her in Carnegie Hall 20 years after; certainly a chilling transmutation. But what, in this context, to make of the very funny beauty-contest sequence, with each lady in the multi-national troupe relating her background, or the related bit in which they free-associate on the word 'dinosaur'; or the hauntingly precise hand-jives which regularly accompany their descents into the auditorium to a Twenties dance tune and which I could have watched over and over, and did?

In the four-hour length my attention often wandered, but it did so in the comforting knowledge that it would have something worth contemplating when it returned. I didn't want to leave, least of all at the end. That's what long shows, when they're good, do for you; they give you an alternative world, cosier than the real one. The dancing, when it happens, is superb. The whole thing is like a cross between A Chorus Line and the People Show.

More Female Trouble (Drill Hall) is about three women in search of, oh, well, I don't know. They ended up identifying men with thinking and women with feeling. I don't approve of sexual stereotyping, but on their own showing they're 50 per cent right.
