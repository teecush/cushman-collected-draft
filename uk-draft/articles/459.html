Burton's Farewell

The last interview Richard Burton gave was to ROBERT CUSHMAN. He said: 'I can't keep away from the stage. I can't stop scratching the pimple.'

I met Richard Burton, three weeks before his death, at Shepperton Studios. He was filming an American TV series called Ellis Island. It was about immigrants in New York at the beginning of the century, so naturally it was being shot over here. The hook for the interview was the fact that his daughter Kate was co-starring with him; her role in fact was larger than his.

I went in trepidation, half-expecting to meet a wreck. I knew that drinking had once nearly killed him, and that a neck operation was said to have immobilised him. At all events it had put paid to his much-vaunted ambition of playing King Lear. (He said: 'I couldn't carry on Cordelia at the end.') In fact, the man I met was alert, relaxed and apparently unshadowed, his eyes and voice -- his prime acting assets, both hypnotic -- in fine working order. Kate Burton confirmed that, in recent years, she had never seen him look better.

There was, all the same, a tiredness about him: spiritual rather than physical. Shepperton had supplied him with a caravan ('comparatively glamorous -- at least we can have a fry-up') though it compared unfavourably to the 'super-glamorous' dressing-rooms he had known when stars were stars and film studios made films. Sitting hunched on a couch he pointed out of the window behind him at a housing estate where once, apparently, there had been a lawn.

'That's where we danced the pavane in Anne of the Thousand Days. Worst dancers I've ever seen. Brook was one of them, weren't you, Brook?' Brook Williams, son of Emlyn, smiled agreement, having graduated from acting to being a one-man Burton entourage, responsible for handling connections to the outside world. Next to Burton, and saying little, sat his fourth and last wife, Sally, English and young.

He spoke of the film career to which he had devoted most of his life as enjoyable but frustrating. 'You accept a part in good faith. The script seems all right. Then six or seven people re-adapt it and ruin it.' However, since he claimed to have seen only seven of his own pictures, he may not be their most reliable critic. He had made, he said, some 70 films, and done about 60 plays. The reference books suggest that he exaggerated on both counts, but his claim to be a workaholic was clearly justified. But -- more obviously even than most actors -- he was drifting where the work took him.

In Ellis Island he played an unscrupulous US senator. Watching a rehearsal did not convince me that it would rank among his greatest roles, but he went through it with grizzled cheerfulness. Kate said that acting was not the all-important thing in her life, and that this was a balanced attitude -- 'working very hard but not taking it too seriously' -- that she had inherited from her father. It may well be true of her. I was not sure about him. The man I met seemed kind, good-humoured, and a little desperate.

The self-destructive Celt is a stock figure in British mythology. Usually he is Irish, but two men, Burton and Dylan Thomas, have staked a solid -- or if you prefer, a liquid -- claim for the Welsh. Both were in part weighed down by the expectations placed upon them. Burton grew up as a figurehead for his family, his community, then his country, and eventually his profession.

He always retained a passion for the written word: the 'absolute passion' (his words) of someone who had once had to fight to master it. Home, in his last years, was officially either Switzerland or Haiti, but really it was 'where the books are,' which mostly meant a converted barn near Geneva. Here he had, among other things, all thousand volumes of the original Everyman's Library.

Perhaps because English was officially his second tongue (he still spoke Welsh every time he went home) he was audibly enraptured of it. His most enduring performance may well be his radio narration of Thomas's Under Milk Wood, with its imperious sense of wonder, its delight at what another Welshman had done with the language, and its whimsical pride in the village whose 'lazy, lecherous day' he was describing. His bookishness never hampered his performing.

The young Burton had the voice of a Gielgud, the virility of an Olivier, and an 'aura' (a favourite word of the time) all his own. His Stratford season in 1951 crystallised it.

Burton himself said to me, 'I'm not as at home in film as on the stage. I can't keep away from the stage. I can't stop scratching the pimple.' He started outlining plans for next year, which he said was still 'relatively free so far.' He talked of doing two plays in repertoire, one Shakespearean and one modern, and doing them on both sides of the Atlantic. He said that as a classical actor he was luckier than most, able to come to the plays absolutely fresh, 'rather than being pushed into the mannerisms that come with the sort of workload you get at the National Theatre or the RSC.'

This sounded to me like rationalisation on a large scale, and I did not feel much confidence that his theatre plans would actually materialise. Nor was I very sure that I wanted them to materialise. When an actor returns after a long absence, the expectant audience are on trial as much as he is; it's difficult to make an honest response. We have seen it with all the men who have, wholly or partially, followed the Burton trail, all the dynamos: Albert Finney, Peter O'Toole, Richard Harris, Nicol Williamson, Anthony Hopkins (something of a Burton clone), maybe Jonathan Pryce. The only heroic actors who have followed the Scofield line and stuck to the stage are Ian McKellen and Alan Howard.

Burton's most recent, and most notorious, stage venture was Private Lives, which he and Miss Taylor, professionally reunited after two divorces, played all over America. It broke, as he said, box-office records everywhere; although he admitted in the same breath that most of the audience were there for the wrong, voyeuristic, reasons. (You've read about the Burtons fighting; now see it on stage.)

But he also claimed to have discovered a comic finesse he didn't know he had. While Elizabeth, he said, 'is a natural clown.' He brought her name up, warmly, casually, and early in the conversation, knowing perhaps that I would have to if he didn't.

Another subject he brought up early was money. He was 'amazed at the money being so good in TV' but that 'truly didn't influence me at all.' In the same vein, he's never had any 'ego problems or bothers about billing; it's only agents who care . . . once I hit the jackpot as 'twere (a favourite phrase) no one ever had billing over me except Elizabeth.'

This can only have been amiable doublethink; he simultaneously wanted the money and prestige (and indeed the Dick-and-Liz notoriety) and felt he should despise them. But, given his early struggles, that was inevitable.

This kind of dichotomy ran through his life. The rolling sonority of his television Churchill, simultaneously weary and indomitable, was a perfect match for the massive folds of Churchill's rhetoric. But once it was shot he felt he owed it to himself to write an article in the New York Times attacking Churchill as a mad, sadistic warmonger.

I saw him a few years ago on Broadway playing the psychiatrist in Equus. He seemed to be sleep-walking through it. He only roused himself at the end, for a long speech riddled with self-disgust; there the actor and the orator in him caught fire, and it was thrilling. And from his films it is the guilty, tormented, disillusioned moments that one remembers best.

His drunken, eloquent (and tempestuously married) academic failure in Who's Afraid Of Virginia Woolf? must have tapped a large part of him. Few actors can have made the decline-of-civilisation aspect of the script so convincing; it must be one of the best bespectacled performances in movies.

Best of all perhaps was his double agent, used and abused by both sides, in The Spy Who Came In From the Cold; it is impossible to forget the ravaged deliberation with which, at the end, he exposed the rules of realpolitik to his idealistic girlfriend. (She was Claire Bloom, the actress -- with one obvious exception -- whose career has most frequently intersected with his; from their joint West End debut in The Lady's Not For Burning, right down to Ellis Island.)

And we have still, most of us, to see the nine-hour Wagner in which, by his own account, he 'never stops talking' -- and the new 1984 in which he plays the interrogator O'Brien. In the novel Orwell emphasises his intellect and his weariness; it should be ideal Burton material.

In The Sandpiper, the most fondly remembered abomination of the Burton-Taylor period, he played a lust-tormented priest. It was oddly appropriate; there has been an element of the defrocked saint in most of his best work. At Stratford a fellow actor described him as 'bringing his own cathedral on with him.' That is apt and impressive, and also a tribute to the transforming powers of art and of ambition. For Burton himself, of course, was strictly chapel.
