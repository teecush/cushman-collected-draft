The rusty chain

ROBERT CUSHMAN
on new plays in
London and (below)
New York.

THAT STEELY and humorous Irish actor T. P. McKenna spends a lot of time in The Communication Cord (Hampstead) tethered like a cow. He plays a modern doctor and senator with a sentimental liking for the antique; and he finds himself in an old Irish cottage.

One of its features is a chain with which the peasantry once used to secure the beasts who were their most precious possessions; imagining an ancient domestic scene, Mr McKenna assumes the milking position, and cannot get out of it. The clasp has rusted. Moral: sentimentality about the past merely ties you down. Or in this case up.

The senator's presence in the cottage, and indeed in Brian Friel's play, has to do with his daughter Susan. A young man called Tim Gallagher, an untenured university lecturer, wants to please her by impressing him, and to that end borrows the cottage for an hour from a barrister friend called Jack. But this, as you may have gathered, is a farce, and so everything goes wrong.

Unwanted visitors turn up; compromising bits of Tim's past raise their pretty little heads and display their pretty little underwear. The house itself is unhelpful ('Did you ever have a sense that a place hates you?' asks Tim rhetorically), what with fireplaces that breathe smoke, beams that give way and lamps that refuse to stay lit. The entire cast are frequently reduced to groping around the stage, like the characters in Black Comedy.

The resemblances to Peter Shaffer's piece are, overall, pretty startling. There is the central situation of boy, girl, and father. There is the chum from whom things are borrowed, and the old girlfriend who arouses the new one's jealousy, while obviously wanting our hero back. There is an intrusive old woman. There are even people imitating Germans and Germans imitating people.

Mr Friel's dialogue is of course his own, and much of it is extremely funny, especially when delivered by Mr McKenna or by Martin Cochrane's Kraut, both of whom end up believing that 'Gallagher' is a synonym for 'mad.' Unfortunately, in Nancy Meckler's production, it isn't.

Stephen Rea, who plays Tim, seems too capable and even stolid to be the hero-victim of a farce; indeed he looks more assured than Brian Protheroe, who plays worldly Jack. With the balance wrong, the two of them make heavy weather of the exposition; and though the comedy does come to a satisfying boil, it lacks foundation. It also fades too long before the end.

Underlying everything is an impatience with the exploitation of Irish myth, which is seen as very rickety. This is fine when implicit, but it does not provide the climax that was obviously hoped. Another theme, announced in the title, is even less helpful. Tim is seven years into a Ph.D on linguistics; his thesis has to do with the fragility of discourse, the ease with which life can destroy the shared assumptions on which comprehension depends. This of course is what farce is about, what it depends on; but a running philosophic commentary does not increase the fun.

West, in this vein, is only east from another angle; and Steven Berkoff's West (Warehouse) is certainly his 'East' revisited. Once again disaffected London youth prowls the streets and the dance-halls, only this time in packs. Gang-warfare is the theme, the upshot a single fight between Mike, our star, and his arch-foe, the Hoxton Fiend.

This, or the preparation for it, is all the plot there is, and it is unconscionably protracted. I am as tired of writing complaints about Mr Berkoff's joky-Shakespearean manner (or, in this case, manor) as he is of reading them. At least there are no couplets this time; the real frustration of the verse is that Mr Berkoff can write terrific prose, as here in Mike's account of a day in the life, with special reference to travelling to work on the tube.

This is later reiterated in group-mime, but the observation is quite strong enough to go round twice. Also, though nobody would call Mr Berkoff a feminist, he writes extremely well for women; Stella Tanner as Mike's mum, Susan Kyd as his girl, are both given sequences of reminiscence, complaint and--in Miss Kyd's case--erotic fancy, which they take with gratitude as obvious as deserved. I believed in them as in very few female characters from male writers.

The machismo is monotonous by comparison, and Mike's underlying self-pity allowed to break all bounds at the end. Mr Berkoff does not appear himself (though there is a Berkoff-clone among the lads), but there is brilliance in his staging, especially in the close-drawn quartet that tours the playing-area in a cross between a march and a lurch, heads well down and forward, belching rhythmic obscenities. It says more than the speeches.

Incidentally the Warehouse may well have to close. A small, central and (since the RSC left) comfortable space, ideal for new work, it deserves rescue at least as much as Riverside or the Roundhouse.

Nick Darke, author of The Body (Pit), has been quoted as believing that all writers should be writing about the bomb. The corollary of that is believing you can plant a bomb in any old play and achieve significance. 'The Body' may not have been written like that, but that is how it plays.

World War Three will probably start for farcical reasons, but not, I think, because of the combination in one Cornish village of an American marine dropped, dead of boredom, a male and female yokel possessive about his corpse, a farmer brainwashed into replacing him on a missile base, a rector who dresses as a Chinaman without his parishioners noticing, and a marine lieutenant who goes apes when he runs into sartorial competition at the local hop.

There is no comic logic to any of these details; they are willed into existence so that all the English characters may be corralled in the second act by the Americans who have access to doomsday. There is some excitement here (well squeezed in Nick Hamm's production), but it is not difficult to achieve suspense by training a gun on the audience and dicing with the end of the world, especially if you have actors like these.

Christopher Benjamin, Clive Wood, Jenny Agutter, David Shaw-Parker, Derek Godfrey, John Bowe and Pete Postlethwaite are among those contributing to the familiar RSC claustrophobia effect: infinite riches in a little room, furnished by 'Dr Strangelove,' 'Man is Man,' 'Cold Comfort Farm' and didactic fringe musicals.

Nigel Williams's The Adventures of Jasper Ridley (Half Moon) is about youth unemployment and is appropriately hopeless. Mr Williams's sentiments--rage and (unless he's kidding) respect for gentleness--are unimpeachable, and his attitudes to official philanthropy interestingly ambivalent. But he tells an aimless, picaresque story in a string of dilapidated sketches; any hope of a sharp cartoon effect is dashed in Pam Brighton's production for Hull Truck (yes, Hull Truck, read it and weep), which is fuzzy and underpowered. There are some glints of acting, especially from Billy MacColl; the riot scenes, involving local kids, deserve to be done over by Mr Berkoff.

A Briton salutes NY

A NEW YORK season that in January was being pronounced the worst in living memory had, when I returned only four months later, brightened unrecognisably up.

There are two new plays on Broadway better than almost any in the West End. Neil Simon's Brighton Beach Memoirs has been generally and justly pronounced his best play. The beach is the Brooklyn district, in which Mr Simon grew up, and the piece, set in 1937, is in some measure autobiographical. How completely does not matter; the central character, a vociferously pubescent Jewish boy called Eugene, would engage us even if we had never heard of the author.

He comes from an overcrowded home. The first act shows this menage facing minor crises and surviving; the second shows them facing major ones, and again surviving. At the end they are all, including those who threatened or attempted to leave, gathered round the table, awaiting the incursion of more relatives, miraculously escaped from Nazi Europe. This finale could be taken as deceptively bitter; and I have heard it persuasively described as such. But I have to admit that it plays soft. It does not, however, vitiate the truthfulness of the characterisation, or the extreme dexterity of the craftsmanship.

The play tells three or four stories, and goes all over the house, and the smoothness with which the scenes interlock is very satisfying. It is also, of course, funny; and for once the wisecracks are unforced and unembarrassing. They all belong to Eugene, who tells the story as if writing a book while it happens. This, we are persuaded, is how a precocious gagman would think, write, talk. The role is brilliantly squeaked by a 21-year-old actor, Matthew Broderick.

Marsha Norman's 'Night, Mother' is also a family play, though the visible family is here confined to old mother and middle-aged daughter. The former is looked after by the latter, an epileptic divorcee with a disturbed delinquent son: with, in her own persuasive view, nothing to live for. She begins the play by telling her mother she is going to kill herself, and in the end she does so.

The bleak implacability of this is a greater achievement than anything in the Simon play, but the whole is less satisfying. I always gag on delayed revelations between long-time intimates. Kathy Bates gives a marvellous account of a lumpy, determined woman who has set deliberate limits to her consciousness.

'Night, Mother' was the only Broadway show I saw not set, or revived from, between the wars. 'You Can't Take It With You,' Kaufman and Hart's celebration of a family who opt out of the rat-race (and apparently have the money to do it) has been lovingly revived under the direction of Ellis Rabb. His starry cast -- Jason Robards mellow and crackling as Grandpa, Colleen Dewhurst enjoying herself as a Russian princess now working in a pancake house -- have thoroughly ensembled themselves; charm, humour and even romance ebb and flow unimpeded.

The National Theatre is to mount You Can't Take It With You in the summer, and had better, as is said in another Kaufman and Hart play, look to its laurels.

The latest hot musical (I meant to type 'hit,' but let it stand) is On Your Toes, which in 1936 brought ballet to Broadway in the shape of George Balanchine's 'Slaughter on Tenth Avenue.' Better still is the title-number, a song described to me by a New York singer as 'the meaning of jaunty' and turned by current choreographer Donald Saddler into an uplifting extended encounter between jazz dancing and classical: first contest, then union.

It makes the show seem briefly like an ancestral concept musical: song and dance not merely the medium, but the subject and certainly the message. Unfortunately there is also a plot; not only wearing in itself, it fails to frame the songs, so a prime Rodgers and Hart score (their best, I think) goes by without anybody noticing.

The original librettist-director George Abbott directed again (at 95), but he has some lack-lustre principals, notably excepting the ballerina Natalia Makarova, who has as much fun as Miss Dewhurst while going in the opposite cultural direction.

A more elaborate musical resurrection is My One and Only, which started out as a revival of the 1927 Gershwin show Funny Face, but has arrived, after a troubled passage out-of-town, as a new show with Gershwin songs from all over. When I saw it in preview I felt that visually nothing more needed to be done; on geometric -- indeed formalist -- sets, Tommy Tune has devised a spectacle that has all the inventiveness of his hit show Nine without the pretention.

The immensely tall Mr Tune is also a revelation as a performer: a proven dancer with a strong voice and personality. Twiggy, his co-star, disappointed me; but then she has not been given much to act. The abiding problem was the story, or at least the lack of something to make us care about it.

At the other extreme of Gershwin, there was Porgy and Bess, staged almost complete in Radio City Music Hall: my chance to catch up with two ancient monuments at once; since Porgy has not been staged in London for 30 years, it must be the most neglected of modern theatrical masterpieces. Catfish Row looked well on the vast stage--the show is the study of a milieu and we may as well see as much of it as possible -- but the unimaginably cavernous auditorium created severe sonic problems.

But the production was able, and the music -- the sheer, dramatic weight of it -- won through. There is a doggedness in this score that amounts to inspiration, then to greatness, and finally--when the crippled Porgy sets out on his crazy journey to New York in a goat-drawn cart--to myth.

Robert Cushman
