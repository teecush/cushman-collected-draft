The new Jenny

ROBERT CUSHMAN meets a Brechtian songstress.

IN A dance studio in Battersea, the familiar reed-laden orchestrations sounded more sinister than ever. This was the first and only orchestra rehearsal for the Oxford Playhouse production of 'The Threepenny Opera.' Gordon McDougall directs with, as he says, 'the same back-up team' who a year ago achieved a critical success with another Brecht-Weill show, 'Happy End.' They opened last week in Stirling. This week they are at the Cambridge Arts Theatre, then Torquay, and after that home to Oxford.

It's one way to see Britain, and the cast of virtual unknowns happens to be headed by a couple of visitors. Macheath is a Canadian, Cedric Smith; Jenny is a 32-year-old West German songstress, Eva Meier, whose sole previous appearance here was in a programme that played for one week at the Open Space just over a year ago. There she was seen by a BBC TV director planning an 'Omnibus' film on Brecht called 'Song of Mandalay'; he put her in it, singing a couple of numbers, and she made a considerable impression.

Brecht and his progenitor Wedekind--two writers who were linked in London last week in a 'cabaret' at the Theatre Upstairs--are the staples of Eva Meier's repertoire when at home. She admits, quite rightly, to being prettier than the average Brechtian: 'I'm not the type supposed to sing Brecht--you're supposed to look hard.' Singing the familiar Brecht-Weill songs in concert she broke with tradition, refusing to look 'sinister or whorelike or always in bar-rooms.' Instead she 'distanced' the material, which sounds a good Brechtian concept.

As for the later Brecht songs, the more overtly political ones with music by Hanns Eisler (whom she prefers to the more obviously appealing Weill), for them 'you can look as you look.' All that is necessary is to get the meaning over. As for the continuing importance of that meaning: 'I think the songs are still right in the big sense, but I don't think they can do very much, as theatre on the whole can't do very much. I think that's realistic.'

There is no shortage of Brecht singers in Germany; and German actors and actresses are bred on the songs. (In the same way, it struck me, British actors grow up with the songs of our own theatrical archetype, Noel Coward, and will sing them on any provocation.) Styles, however, have changed with the years. When she heard the original twenties recordings of 'The Threepenny Opera,' Eva Meier was surprised to hear how smooth it all sounded, with 'Lotte Lenya singing like a bird, very high. I thought it was very comical. It was only later that the style changed to being very harsh, and everybody did the political songs and forgot about Brecht having humour.' (A lapse of memory that persists to this day, certainly in Britain.)

Eva Meier started as 'a normal actress,' going the rounds of the German metropolitan theatres: Trier, Dusseldorf, Stuttgart, Munich. She got into singing almost by accident, when, disillusioned by the theatre (she had trouble with dictatorial directors), she approached Bavarian Broadcasting for work. A producer there asked her if she could sing, to which she replied that she had learned something about it at acting school. He decided she was right for a Wedekind programme he was mounting at the Piccolo Teatro in Milan. She spent six weeks practising, eight hours a day, and five days before the opening reckoned she was just about ready to get through the show.

That 'spectacular' debut (she laughs at the description) was followed by a Brecht-Eisler evening, and the realisation that this new career, in which she could do everything herself, was more enjoyable than the old. Brecht-singing being a crowded field in Germany, she has not been able to work at it full-time; she has filled the gaps studying jurisprudence. The Oxford production marks her first return to the theatre since the start of her concert career and, surprisingly, her first performance of this material. But then all Brechtian chanteuses do the Jenny songs, so she has kept off them. At least she hasn't had to unlearn the German before learning the English.
