Restoration vaudeville

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Oxford.

The praise deservedly heaped on Peter Nichols's Passion Play has had one bemusing aspect. Mr Nichols has been widely credited with inventing the device of the on-stage alter ego: a strange claim when no less than half a dozen of them are cavorting about the stage--even if to no particular purpose--in They're Playing Our Song. Brian Friel's Philadelphia Here I Come!, built around an identical device, is of course ancient history: a dozen years old if it's a day. Even further back, authors used to save the management the cost of an extra actor by employing the aside. Asides came in two categories--the conspiratorial and the conscience-stricken--and the employment of both is the most rewarding feature of Vanbrugh's The Relapse, as revived at the Old Vic.

The Relapse is a late Restoration comedy (first production 1696) with honestly confused ideas about morality. The reformed rake Loveless has married the virtuous Amanda and is attracted by her friend Berinthia, who encourages him off to bed, to her satisfaction and to the author's obvious amusement and probable approval. Meanwhile Amanda is being pursued by everybody's old friend Worthy, but rejects him; again to cheers from her creator. The last scene, naturally, includes a masque, which proclaims that 'the joys of life and love are in variety.' (In this production the words are repeated at the final curtain.)

This variety might conceivably include fidelity: each to his or her own kick. But what turns Loveless on may--in fact, does--hurt Amanda; Vanbrugh is too truthful to deny this, too nervous to give it full expression. So dramatically their relationship, which starts the play, must crumble away; by the end we have no idea what they think of each other. But on the way through Vanbrugh has given Loveless his full share of conscience-stricken asides; they anticipate Mr Nichols's male double-act, and they are delivered by Richard Kay.

The divided lady--again as with Mr Nichols--is less well served; when called on for heart-searching, Maureen O'Brien delivers, but she has a habit of demonstrating a line, like a salesman, instead of speaking it. (She is lumbered with a newly written prologue, which makes agonisingly arch jokes about arts subsidy and, yet again, The Romans in Britain. The Relapse is a tough play to do, and it hardly needs extra handicaps.)

The main action tells us that some enjoy themselves, others suffer; the positions may be reversed and meantime the only unforgivable sins are to crow or to whine. This is the only link with the second action, whose central character, Lord Foppington, the self-made dandy, is guilty of the first offence and barely avoids the second.

Foppington begins the play as a specialty act--consulting and insulting an army of costumiers, reeling off the peacocking pleasures of his day--then develops into a person steely, selfish and unscrupulous beneath the enveloping wigs (one of them here the colour of a Florida grapefruit). John Nettles's performance, the surest of the evening, is best when he gets to the undercoating: his are the conspiratorial asides, with the audience as his partners in crime.

Both sections of the play are interesting, but this is almost an additional hazard; one continually resents being dragged away from either of them. Their rhythms, too, are totally different: one a tight-knit farcical intrigue, the other a discursive essay on sex. Maybe a director could find some striking visual metaphor to link them (Adrian Vaux's set here is a pleasingly discreet affair of black and white), but Michael Simpson is not much to be blamed for failing to find one. This, like most Restoration comedies, is a vaudeville bill and he has not--with a few promising exceptions, such as Julia Swift's bumpkin Hoyden, ambitious for London life and lust--the actors to fill it.

I suspect that one sign of a weak theatre is an obsessive verbal harping on class. It is a fine subject for comedy, as Vanbrugh shows without ever mentioning the subject.

Philip Barry's 1939 comedy The Philadelphia Story (Oxford Playhouse) does nothing but mention it, but has no way of transferring its talk into action. The immensely wealthy Tracy Lord decides not to marry her stuffy fiance (up from the mines into management), but to return to her first husband, C. K. Dexter-Haven, noted yachtsman. En route she has a flirtation with newspaperman Mike Connor, also up from poverty, and a touch embittered, but all right because he has style.

Barry's values are not so different from Vanbrugh's, but (a) Vanbrugh doesn't go on about them and (b) he justifies them in the texture of his writing. Barry's wit is woolly, and his passionate declarations painfully lush; he even, in passing, preaches. So at least it seems in Gordon McDougall's woefully ill-timed production; two film versions have suggested different, but they were suffused in stardust (incidentally adding two further layers of mythology to complicate the lives of future interpreters).

Only Robert Beatty and Tenniel Evans, in sideline roles, come off with complete credit; add, maybe, Paul Herzberg in a role axed from the movies to fatten the part of Dexter, who in his original emaciated state seems more chorus than protagonist. Lewis Fiander does well by the first function, but cannot rise to the second. Mike's frigidity is well dispensed by Alan Rickman, but it ends the entire role. Gayle Hunnicutt offers an intelligent reading of Tracy's lines, but there seems to be nothing flowing beneath.
