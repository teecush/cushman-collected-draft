Good Wood

AFTER all her hard work, Victoria Wood deserves an evening to herself, and she gets it at the King's Head in a show that she self-deprecatingly entitles Lucky Bag.

Gone — on a tour of remote places like Scotland — is her conjuring husband the Great Soprendo; and though I miss him, and trust that she does too, her new show is a triumph for simple arithmetic. Two hours of Miss Wood turns out to be twice as funny as one.

Miss Wood comes before us in an ensemble of shocking blue and electric pink. Her hair is cheeringly flaxen, her figure reassuringly stocky. (She says unkinder things about it herself, but then she's a comedienne.) She alternates monologues with music in the manner of Michael Flanders, with whom — technically if not socially — she has much in common.

I doubt if any performer since Flanders and Swann has raised so much mirth from a programme of songs. Generally you expect a lyricist, however witty, to provoke smiles rather than guffaws. Miss Wood is cunning; by getting you laughing at her spoken material she loosens you up for the numbers. She makes you laugh till you cry with most of them, and then in one called 'Love Song,' supposed to be sung by an old man to his just-dead wife ('Made your breakfast this morning like any old day/then I remembered and I threw it away'), she makes you cry outright.

There's a point. Miss Wood is known for her dismissive attitude towards the opposite sex. Her most celebrated song is called 'I've Had It Up To Here With Men' ('Perhaps I should phrase that again'), but she can empathise marvellously with old men and small boys. 'Music and Movement,' about a male mixed infant suffering a 1959 schools broadcast, remains the most endearing selection in her repertoire.

A new non-musical item is a speech delivered at a schoolboy debating society in favour of the wearing of school uniforms. She does it in an eager self-righteous squeak, hurrying nervously past non-existent jokes ('but to be serious for a moment or two') and leaping suddenly forward ('but there is another point of view to this vexed question') from statements that have never been made. This is a brilliant piece of writing, and of acting.

Indeed Miss Wood's new prowess as a stand-up comic is the great feature of the show. She comes out of northern music-hall as refined by television (well, she lives in Morecambe and she's wise) and she injects into it not only detailed observation — nobody else has summed up the experience of a nightmare InterCity journey as sitting opposite a man making a model of the Forth Bridge out of used lager cans — but human feeling.

A lot of it concerns sexual exploitation, but she links it to other things. Her most powerful song, 'Don't Do It,' is a warning against marital waste. It deals, though, in individual cases, not generalities.

That is her strength throughout. It makes her, as well as a fount of genuinely good advice, the acutest social critic now working in the theatre; that, and the fact that she doesn't try to impress us with anger. She mocks a plastic world, but she does not rage at it; she even recognises that it is, in certain lights, fun. She is the poet of Tesco's, 'Crossroads,' and the launderette down the street with one functioning tumble-dryer.

I don't really mean that. Songwriters are not poets. A couplet about letters from dull girl-friends, 'Those of whom you are not fond/Relentlessly wield the Basildon Bond,' probably looks nothing on paper. Set to music, the rhyme reinforcing the reference, it is a killer. Miss Wood is no great composer or piano-player, but her tunes support her words, which is what they are there for. I want to write about nearly everything she does.

As a designer, Ultz has set his stamp on a number of fantastical comedy productions, notably the RSC's Twin Rivals and Comedy of Errors. Pericles (Stratford East), however, is the Ultimate, since he has directed it as well. He does it as a gentle, mocking cartoon that can turn serious, indeed moving. Reverend even.

It may be camp, but it is not camped-up; and it is dead right for the play we have, of which two-fifths is not only un-Shakespearean, but plain terrible. The dialogue, anyway; the story is fascinating.

Gower, the narrator, talks in dire couplets, here charmingly transformed into half-song half-speech by the composer Martin Duncan. Designers and musicians have been threatening to take over Shakespearean production for some time now; here they do it, with encouraging results.

There is much doubling, done according to rank. Brian Protheroe shuffles a whole pack of kings, to excellently various effect. Only one man in his time plays a single part; this is of course Gerard Murphy in the title-role. He still clenches up, physically and vocally, at moments of stress, but he finds more development in the role than any actor I have seen.

The play unfolds around a set of outsize cupboards. In the physician scenes one of them opens to reveal a staggering array of drugs and surgical instruments; in the brothel it is a bare cell, with an off-white wall smudged with red, and a shelf on which crouches the terrified but obstinately virtuous Marina, played with radiant goodwill by Felicity Dean. When it comes out of the closet, the production is liable to reveal such wonders as an endless green net, hauled ashore from the wings by a trio of fishermen.

Snoo Wilson can make me laugh more heartily and unexpectedly than almost any other playwright, and he does so again in The Grass Widow (Royal Court), which is a sort of transplanted Volpone. The title-figure who, following a recent death, owns a marijuana farm, is besieged by creditors, lovers, seekers of shelter.

Bizarre and exciting things happen, though Mr Wilson seems to be laying them on in deference to his own reputation. I stayed with the play, but did not feel it had altogether rewarded my loyalty. A strong cast: Alan Rickman, Ron Cook, Leslee Udwin and (apparently playing Linda Lovelace) Tracey Ullman.

At the Ambassadors, a double-bill by the American Christopher Durang. ('Durang Durang,' we said wittily in the interval.) The Actor's Nightmare is proverbially to find yourself in a play you have not rehearsed; it falls flat here because making it a dream enables Mr Durang to change the rules at will, and because the production and Christopher Timothy's performance are stolid.

Sister Mary Ignatius Explains It All For You is a monologue for an exceptionally bigoted teaching nun, followed by a confrontation with some of the pupils whose lives she has warped. Excellent black comedy, it suffers from anglicisation (national, not sectarian) and from the fact that Maria Aitken's well-pointed performance is half the requisite weight and age. Both she and Mr Timothy are actually at their best as supports in each other's plays.

The show is presented by the Theatre of Comedy, an imposing collection of talented players, many of whom were visible on the first night, though only in the audience. At the Cottesloe, mainly for schools, there is a workshop Antigone that begins promisingly tight and taut, but finishes up fast and featureless.
