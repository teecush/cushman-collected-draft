Who cares who dunnit?

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a murder mystery, and revivals of Brecht and Odets.

IN THE golden days of Beyond Our Ken and Round the Horne there used to be sketches which began with a genial narrator saying something like 'my name is immaterial: Sir John Immaterial' and proceeded to a 10-minute lampoon of the country-house murder mystery.

Tom Stoppard played the same joke at greater length in The Real Inspector Hound; but he diversified it with a chorus of critics and even so turned in less than a full-sized play. It has been left to Anthony Shaffer in The Case of the Oily Levantine (Her Majesty's) to spin the jest out over an entire evening.

The curtain rises then on the traditional panelled setting, and the actors arrive. At their head is Hywel Bennett as the eponymous (not to say oleaginous) Middle Easterner, come to spend a country weekend and to blackmail all his fellow guests, to wit: a crooked lawyer, a retired naval officer ('a bluff bugger' as somebody unaccountably forgets to put it), a she-dragon, a flapper and her go-getting fiance, and a prim lady archaeologist. Also the butler. All are deliberate stereotypes, and the cast grapple with them vigorously and even wittily. All the same, an hour of funny-voiced acting is rather more than flesh and blood can stand.

As it happens, the second act does induce a wholesale change of style. The reasons for this I may not, of course, reveal; but I am giving away hardly more than the programme does if I say that the oily Levantine is now the late Levantine, and that Mr Bennett has switched to playing the inspector come to ascertain which of the amply motivated survivors done him in. The inspector has fantasies of being a detective, and he conducts his investigation after the fashion of his favourite novels.

This is all very well for him, but it leaves the rest of the cast -- deprived first of reality and now even of artifice -- in a most unenviable position. You suddenly remember why Agatha Christie plays are always so badly acted: they offer nothing to act. But Miss Christie always kept her plot going, so that you got involved anyway. Mr Shaffer, intent on jokiness, keeps neglecting his, so that the cry of 'Who cares who kills Roger Ackroyd?' is all too easy to echo.

It may be a comment on Mr Shaffer's cunning, or merely on human nature, that when he remembers we do care, even while kicking ourselves. Some of the jokes are good (especially in some silky passages of narration), but many more are not; even if their ghastliness is intentional, it soon becomes wearingly predictable. Mr Bennett is supported in the second stage of his dashing double by Roger Hood's sergeant, a performance of welcome sobriety, while the others -- among them Adrienne Posta, Anna Quayle and Bernard Archard -- do their manic, and valorous, best.

The Private Life of the Third Reich (Open Space) is one of Brecht's least assuming plays, and also one of his most powerful. It is merely a sequence of episodes -- not all included in this production -- delineating the progress of 'fear and misery' (the original title) and most of all of universal mistrust in pre-war Hitler Germany. The scenes are offered to us as pieces of a parade, each representing a different section of society. The play is contemporary propaganda, but nobody is likely to quarrel with it on that score; it is in fact refreshing to encounter Brecht working journalistically rather than through some laboured parable.

There is one sense in which his political bias is a handicap; he is unable to face the idea of German workers actually being enthusiastic Nazis. They are all shown to us either as brave if unwary protesters or as terrorised into silence. He is sounder on the intelligentsia, showing us doctors refusing to ask dangerous questions when confronted by a victim of official brutality, or a teacher and his wife terrified that their young son might be informing on them -- a scene that is rather daringly tilted towards farce.

A Jewish wife preparing to leave her Gentile husband is movingly, and unsentimentally, detailed; and there are two sardonic masterpieces. One shows us two bakers meeting in prison, accused of breaking the food laws in precisely opposite ways. The other depicts a judge under conflicting pressures from the SA, the SS, popular opinion and (vestigially) his own conscience, and finishing by crying out, 'If the authorities won't tell us what they want, how can there be justice?'

Brecht was always good on the entwining paradoxes of the law, and this scene shows him at his best. It could do, I think, with a drier treatment than it gets here; Roger Kemp plays (believably enough) the confusion of the judge's situation rather than its irony. Mr Kemp and Susan Engel head a resourceful cast, in an equally resourceful staging by Nikolas Simmonds.

The Cambridge Theatre Company are on tour with Clifford Odets's fifties piece, Winter Journey. I saw them at the Ashcroft in Croydon; this week they are in Darlington and after that they return to base. The play is about the reclamation of an actor, brought out of drunken retirement by a young director who saw and worshipped him in his greatest days. But to get the performance he wants, the director has to battle with the wife on whom the actor is wholly dependent; so at least he thinks.

Formally the play is a melodrama; it is built on a technique of revelation and if you have seen the play before (or if, like me, you once caught the film, which adhered to the play's original title of The Country Girl), you may complicate your reactions by importing knowledge before the play is ready for it. This is a shame, for the story is tautly and amusingly built up, and the marriage at its centre is searchingly portrayed. The picture of the actor who has quarrelled with his wife for the hundredth time subsisting in his chair and sobbing for her -- his 'country girl' -- is unforgettable.

Joan Kemp-Welch's production suffers from lengthy scene-changes and, more crucially, from a pervasive restraint. These players are acting with their gloves on; the play's form, style and milieu call out for naked aggression. Laurence Payne manages the alcoholic jitters and the false bonhomie of the returned star very movingly, but he lacks the temperament that should underpin the whole. He plays the role, English, which immediately suggests a man who harbours his grudges, rather than one who ferociously displays them.

Eleanor Bron, though she is shrewdly and imaginatively cast and returns the compliment in her performance, could simply nag and domineer more if his accusations and the director's suspicions are to be in any way believable. Nick Tate is on the ball as the director. Smaller parts are undemonstratively well done.

The Yellow Dogs of Texas (ICA) is billed as a play about, and with, country-and-western music. The author, Alan Williams, did rather a good job on pop in The Cockroach That Ate Cincinnati, but what he's up to here eludes me. There are duologues between the put-upon owner of a vandalised C&W club, and an equally beleaguered lady. In the second act (and belatedly) the music starts, but raises the spirits only momentarily; it does enable Alan Aldred to give rather a stylish account of a fading singing star. I may have missed something (the proceedings are so low-key that attention easily wanders) but, in point of pointlessness, this seems to be the fringe's answer to The Oily Levantine.
