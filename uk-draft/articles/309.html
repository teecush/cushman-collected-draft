Hard times for the Celts

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on plays by Howard Brenton, Nigel Williams, Alan Bennett and Alan Ayckbourn.

HOWARD BRENTON'S The Romans in Britain (Olivier) is about imperialism. Its last event, chronologically, is the shooting of an English army major by Irish republicans.

As he is dragged off he shouts, "When will peace come?" His killers are not impressed: "What right does he have to stand in a field in Ireland and talk of the horrors of war? What nation ever learnt from the sufferings it inflicted on others? What did the Roman Empire give to the people it enslaved? Concrete."

That sounds uncomfortably like the Judaean Liberation Front in "Monty Python's Life of Brian" excoriating all the enemy's works except transport, sanitation and public safety. Overlapping with the 1980 scenes in Mr Brenton's play are two moments from ancient British history: the arrival of the Romans in 54 BC under Julius Caesar, and that of the Saxons 550 years later. Caught in the middle every time are the Celts: their homes burned, their crops destroyed, their women raped and - as is made graphically apparent - their men too. The Roman invasion takes up most of the first act; it is a fine moment when, just before the interval, Caesar's army returns to the stage this time dressed in khaki.

Unfortunately we never progress far beyond that moment of plus ca change. Mr Brenton has written a fierce, funny and maddeningly inconclusive play. All he has to say in the end is that conquest causes suffering and ought not to be allowed. He brings off his version of Celtic civilisation - not exactly an easy subject - with vigour and ingenuity, but the only time we feel ourselves inside a man's head is when we meet Caesar, meticulously grooming himself for history.

Mr Brenton's Celts incidentally (including a couple of comic Ancient Irish who begin the play) are no models of moral behaviour. They kill strangers on sight and would obviously be as murderous as their conquerors if they had the fire-power. The dim view of humanity taken throughout suggests that war breeds imperialism rather than the other way about, and that both are basic to human nature rather than unexplained mutations of it.

I agree with this, but I don't think it's what Mr Brenton set out to prove. Anyway he seems to be enjoying himself with the play's omnipresent violence: gloating not so much over the thing itself as over his ability to make the audience sit through it. Michael Bogdanov's production solves most of the play's problems (with a couple of farcical lapses) and incorporates good acting from Michael Bryant as Caesar; from Stephan Moore as the major and James Hayes as his nemesis; and from John Normington and James Carter as the clowns.

Nigel Williams in Sugar and Spice (Royal Court) repeats the pattern of his last play "Line 'Em." A belligerent character harangues everyone for an act; after the interval the opposition arrives, and does its own sounding-off. In this case the initial focus is a teenage punk, Sharon, who arrives with friends in the flat of a Chelsea tart and arranges the ritual humiliation of a boy who has been following them with obvious designs on one of them.

Both as written and played (by Toyah Willcox) Sharon is quirkier, and more compelling than Mr Williams's last mouthpiece, and the aggro she expresses is real and painfully rooted. But apart from the fact that she goes on too long, she is not believable; she is too ornately articulate for her background, and her background is the point of the play. I will take Mr Williams's word that he is not trying to write naturalistically, but I think he chooses the wrong subjects to get stylish over.

Anyway there is nothing hieratic about Bill Alexander's production, which tries, with varying success, to find convincing things for the other characters to do while listening. (Text and production have sketched out individual lives for them, but they only function in fits and starts.) The acting too is variable, to put it politely; the actor who inherits the rhetoric in Act Two seems to have no idea what to do with it. There is action as well as talk: boys and girls alike being maddened by their sexual conditioning, a broken whisky bottle is flashed and so are quantities of male and female flesh.

The delectable Gwyneth Strong (coping admirably with a bewilderingly inconsistent role) is slowly stripped in Act Two; this, given the play's moral drift, is having your cheese-cake and eating it. There is an entertaining performance by Carole Hayman as the unwilling hostess, wondering why the sexes can't just get on together. If she says it once, she says it a hundred times. All concerned spend quantities of time volubly reacting to situations and ideas that are never actually established.

From the Howards and Nigels of subsidised youth to the Alans of free enterprise. Not that no wires are crossed between the two: last week I witnessed two bodies dubiously dead, two unanswered 999 calls, and two young ladies proclaiming, "I'm a slag." Well one, being from the north, says "I'm a slag, me." She figures in Alan Bennett's Enjoy (Vaudeville), and her parents' fond belief that she is a respectable secretary is one instance of their incurable (and exhaustively paraded) silliness. Their bland acceptance of the news that she is off to Saudi Arabia to marry a prince tells us more about them than about their author's allegiance to the post-Orton comic mode, in which the joke consists of taking outrageous events for granted.

Mr Bennett provides a few hilarious variants; nevertheless his first act had me furious. He seemed to be patronising his characters and his central device was cumbrous; he introduces into his protagonists' decaying Leeds home a silent official who will take notes on their behaviour that may help in resettling them. ("Don't take this down yet, we're not being typical yet.") Later one recognises Mr Bennett's strategy; resettlement turns out to mean incarceration in a museum of local working-class culture, a sort of human zoo, for mum, and in a home for dad, paralysed by a car-crash and a juvenile assault.

The sour taste, in fact, is intentional. So, yes, this is an uncompromising play for today's West End; and, yes, it is finely acted by Joan Plowright (though Beryl Reid could do it more effortlessly) and Colin Blakely (though to cast so vibrant an actor as a physical and spiritual cripple is a definite waste), finely supported by Susan Littler (she's the slag, her) and Liz Smith; and, yes, Mr Bennett is a superior writer. Not really though a writer of plays; his characters are museum exhibits, well displayed but with no interior life, and they are presented to us at exorbitant length.

Alan Ayckbourn seems to keep writing the same play. I don't mean that he repeats himself, but that he explores different areas of the same family canvas. Season's Greetings (Roundhouse) takes off from "Absurd Person Singular" by being about Christmas and from "Sisterly Feelings" by being about sisters. Again they fight over a man, but in this case one of them has only platonic (which does not mean unpassionate) designs. Marcia Warren plays her with a good blend of decisiveness and finesse. Other pearls are Ronald Herdman as a worm who turns, with unusual passion for these latitudes, on one of Mr Ayckbourn's fascistic lawmen, and Susan Uebel as his wife, a walking accident.

Some scenes are surprisingly pallid. But if you are collecting the Ayckbourn set, you would hardly want to miss an instalment.
