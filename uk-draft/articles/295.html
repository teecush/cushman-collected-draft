Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Nicholas Nickleby.'

David Threlfall as Smike (lower left) with a galaxy of the RSC's Dickensian characters.

THERE IS a lot to be said for length.

The Life and Adventures of Nicholas Nickleby (Aldwych) is the equivalent of two bumper plays, and has a total playing-time of eight and a half hours. The public instinct to queue for an epic is perfectly sound; quantity may not be a substitute for quality, but it is quality's most powerful ally. I came out of the theatre, going on midnight, wrung-out and grateful.

The critical reaction has astonished me: hours of pleasure, acknowledged as such, and people start wondering whether it should have been done. What comes through Nicholas Nickleby, as through The Greeks, earlier this year, is that those concerned were excited by the material and couldn't wait to get on the stage. That is not just a valid reason for putting on a show; it is the only reason.

Not every novelist inspires such feelings. Dickens, though, always has and always will. Nobody but Shakespeare created such insistent characters, such swingingly speakable dialogue, or so palpable a dramatic universe.

The theatre lost a great deal when the best men took to writing novels, and it seems sensible to try and regain some of the ground. Dickens communicates with an audience more fully, more theatrically, than most playwrights; the first-night cheers came from an audience rejoicing at their release from starvation rations. (Someone has to quote Squeers's "here's richness," so let it be me.)

I had better name the adaptor, David Edgar, and the directors, Trevor Nunn and John Caird, without trying to divide credit; the actors apparently worked from the book and it is Dickens's text, not a separate artefact, to which they must be held responsible. They have preserved all the strands of plot, sometimes in dazzling counterpoint, and nearly all the characters.

They have even tried to retain some of the narration, with varying results; Dickens wrote in sustained paragraphs and breaking these up, one line to an actor, destroys the impetus. The first 15 minutes had me very uneasy, but the later use of a doom-laden chorus to hunt Ralph Nickleby to death was very effective.

What has to go, irretrievably, is the insouciant asides ("two live Members of Parliament looking so pleasant that it seemed a perfect marvel how any man could have the heart to vote against them") that make Dickens so literally irresistible a satirist. The loss is compounded by a certain native earnestness in the RSC production. Any pamphleteer could make people shudder at Dotheboys Hall (and many did); it took Dickens to make them laugh, and to get the Yorkshire schools closed down. If these scenes at the Aldwych are not horrific enough, the basic reason is that they are not funny enough.

Squeers is a juicy character; Ben Kingsley, who plays him, is a dry actor. (He makes amends later as a drunken member of the Crummles troupe.) Then, what misplaced integrity caused John McEnery to play the imposter Mantalini, whose joke depends on exquisite affectation, as dead common? Melodrama is softened too.

Roger Rees as Nicholas and Susan Littler as sister Kate superbly avoid any suggestion of pasteboard heroics, and Mr Rees's native quality of slightly sheepish impetuosity could hardly be more winning or more useful. But they might have risked some of his higher flights (his description of the Hall, say, as a place where "infant misery stalks precocious"); they might have made his mother's absent-minded remonstrances more effective (Jane Downs seems too level-headed for the role) and also have given greater weight to his uncle's antagonism, which gets lost. Instead of a monster with a chink of kindness for his niece, John Woodvine sometimes seems to be playing an affectionate uncle driven into villainy.

But as the day progresses, into darker and less charted territory, most of these performances ripen; and there is a marvellous setpiece at half-time: a Crummles performance of Romeo and Juliet with a happy ending in execrable rhyming couplets. This is apocryphal Dickens as well as apocryphal Shakespeare, but at this point both cast and audience deserve to stretch out and relax.

Graham Crowden's actor-manager is rollingly definitive; and Lila Kaye makes a formidable wife both for him and the schoolmaster. In other parts of the forest Bob Peck doubles a cheer-worthy John Browdie with a baronet too deep for hisses, Timothy Kightley is a properly touchy rate-collector, and Suzanne Bertish plays an Italianate actress with an uncanny resemblance to Dorothy Tutin.

Edward Petherbridge's Newman Noggs, shuffling out of his skin with excitement as the plot thickens, just loses first place to David Threlfall's perfect Smike, which is dogged and occasionally humorous and so moving as to force a revaluation of Victorian death-bed scenes.

The adaptation infuses some doubt into the happy ending: justified in view of the distance we have travelled, but rather confusingly staged. Otherwise the book's morality, with every episode dependent on money, is allowed to strike home unforced.

Dickens distrusted all politicians. So, up to a point, do Howard Brenton and Tony Howard, authors of A Short Sharp Shock (Stratford East), but they waver, expressing a sentimental preference for the Left; and in this production it is sentimental, since the workers uniting against Thatcherism at the final curtain are written and played with the hollowness that invariably infects the favoured side in agitprop dramas.

However, the heart of the play lies in the Cabinet, and the two Howards have managed some vigorous and imaginative lampooning; having Howe, Joseph et al played by women adds an icy touch of style to the proceedings, though it seems to promise something more apocalyptic than is actually delivered. Nor does it quite jibe with Gwen Taylor's Thatcher impersonation, which is the equal of Janet Brown's on The News Huddlines: though I'm not sure that Miss Brown, speaking funnier lines to a larger audience, isn't doing more damage.

The ludicrous threat of censorship has caused mention of Fielding, whose political satires brought the Lord Chamberlain upon us 250 years ago. But Fielding was writing in a tradition that took personal attack for granted; we cannot do it without unconsciously congratulating ourselves on our daring. So political satire remains fixated at Private Eye level.

Nostalgia creeps fast upon us. Dusty Hughes's Commitments (Bush) takes us back to the early Seventies, and the end of the last Tory Government. The characters are assorted politicos, mostly engaged in luxury trades (writing, acting and so on) and wittily observed. (Notice how on the fringe industrial brand-names produce the same infallible laughter as place-names do in Shaftesbury Avenue.) The uncommitted character, with whom the audience is likeliest to identify, is the one of whom the author has selflessly declared he most disapproves. Alan Rickman, who plays him, has perfected a sardonic glide to match his deep deprecating voice; in a good cast Paola Dionisotti also stands out as his spiritual opposite.

But for political drama that comes anywhere near Dickens in trenchancy you have to go to the first half of The Arbor (Royal Court), whose author, Andrea Dunbar, puts an appalling life on stage without ever losing her cool or even her humour. It is the kind of artlessness only possible, or indeed tolerable, from a teenage writer depicting her own world. In the second half the lack of form begins to get to you; but before that the play has left its mark. Max Stafford-Clark's production is admirably acted, with Kathryn Pogson a memorable picture of hugged solitude as the twice-pregnant heroine, aged 15 and named Andrea Dunbar.
