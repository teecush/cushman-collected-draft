Unexplosive bomb

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Stratford.

JOSEPH PAPP founded the New York Shakespeare Festival, which gives it away for free in Central Park, and he runs the Public Theatre, which comprises a multitude of auditoria in which have originated many hits.

His exchange plan with the Royal Court (new plays to cross the Atlantic with their original casts) could have beneficent repercussions; and its first round is certainly a tribute to his business acumen. We sent him Top Girls, and he brings us Buried Inside Extra. He got a good deal.

There is something undigested about Thomas Babe's title. It sounds like an amalgam of three others, and was probably meant to. But the play itself is a similar confusion, and this must be a mistake. Part of it is hard-boiled newspaper drama, with the traditional snap. Part is personal relations: the city editor has long been involved with a lady journalist for whom a younger reporter now makes an inconclusive play. An ancient employee of uncertain function turns out to be her father, giving great scope for recrimination and also--since he has a radical past--for Arthur Millerish reminiscence.

Overhanging all this, there is apocalypse: not only is the paper about to fold, but somebody has planted a bomb in the building. An atom bomb. Mr Babe's real theme is probably that lives are carried heedlessly on, whatever the threat. It sounds good as a subject, but it has a way of defeating itself dramatically.

Mr Papp is here his own director, and a lax one. Both the text and the actors are indulged in the belief that they are being terribly smart. The cast chew the lines as if they were scenery. Dixie Carter, a doubly southern name with accent to match, acts like the wisecracking sufferer was going out of style, and almost makes one wish she were. Hal Holbrook, playing a man who has squandered his talent, abuses his own.

Vincent Gardenia and William Converse-Roberts are old and young respectively. Sandy Dennis appears as the editor's drugged and cheated wife, her face funereally wreathed in smiles; she even has nervous teeth. She speaks in a numb monotone that gave me a headache, but that also has an edge of truth. For her one scene Mr Babe has stopped fancying around; we are shown one person's life and fantasies and through them a relationship. After she leaves, everyone goes back to striking attitudes.

Father and daughter feeding one another's fantasies, that good old American theme of the family lie, can also be found in the late O'Neill play, A Moon for the Misbegotten, revived at Riverside Studios. Ian Bannen resumes the role he played 25 years ago in Long Day's Journey Into Night of James Tyrone, alias the author's elder brother, now drinking and whoring more than ever, struck by an everlasting guilt over his behaviour after his mother's death.

Those of us who are not relatives may feel that O'Neill rattles his skeletons at excessive length, but the heart of the play is with Tyrone's tenant farmer, Phil Hogan, whose scheming blarney shows O'Neill to be a surprisingly good comedian, and his daughter Josie. Their common aim, pursued for widely differing motives, is to get Josie into Tyrone's bed.

O'Neill saw life as composed of two slabs labelled reality and illusion. His characters see themselves and each other with unnatural clarity (not necessarily accuracy) and they keep harping on what they see. Josie, a virgin and in her own words "a lump," pretends to be the lay of the land. Her father believes her; Tyrone, nursing his own dream of big-breasted maternal purity, does not.

We get a tragi-comedy of humours; to each his or her own idee fixe, leading to banal missing on a Chekhovian scale. Add a dash of Irish fatalism (Phil's sons leave home as regularly if not as irreversibly as Synge's riders to the sea) and overlay everything with the remembered melancholy, beyond sorrow, that O'Neill discovered in the great last act of Long Day's Journey.

David Leveaux's production is journeyman work, but it keeps the mood alive; one can think of it, per the title, as a romantic comedy for lost souls. The droop in Frances de la Tour's voice makes her sound very fey when she raises it, but for most of the time she is a creaking and commanding Josie, and she gets good enough support from Ian Bannen and Alan Devlin.

Massinger's A New Way to Pay Old Debts (Other Place, Stratford) was for centuries the most popular Jacobean play outside Shakespeare. This was partly due to its tidy construction and partly to its high moral tone; virtue is unambiguously triumphant and there is not even much bawdry. But beyond this the character of Sir Giles Overreach, land-grabber and extortioner, was reckoned a great star role, one to make women faint in the gallery.

It is difficult now to see why. Sir Giles is not frightening because he never looks like winning. The play's decent characters get together early, and they are continually a jump ahead of him. Emrys James does what he can to restore the balance, a fine figure of a businessman radiating contempt for the goody-goodies and ne'er-do-wells who make up the supporting cast. "Why?" you keep him expecting to snarl, "must I be surrounded by fools?"

Mr James is technically in complete control, but the only victory he is allowed is the offstage intimidation of a refractory cook. Adrian Noble's production relies heavily on such venerable gags as the loud crash in the wings and the interrupted conspiracy, but usually turns them to account.

He has given the action an anti-morality tinge; characters strut in black and white against a surround of grey, decaying furnishings: you feel that, historically, the victory of the Lord Lovells and Lady Alworths over encroaching capitalism will be short-lived. Miles Anderson plays the spendthrift Wellborn in the most picturesque tatters; thrown out of an inn and laden with luggage, he looks like Lucky in Waiting for Godot.

The other outstanding performance, at the opposite emblematic extreme, comes from Anthony O'Donnell, amazingly furred and feathered as a crooked lawyer. (Which, in the drama of the time, is a tautology.) Colin Sell has composed a very amusing score. It remains a high pleasure to see RSC actors, between grappling with the big one up the road, relaxing in the coterie plays of his successors; easy professionals who wrote verse because it was expected of them, but were in essence good prose technicians. We may yet get round to the pre-Restoration comedies of Shirley and Brome.

The modern actor most at home in the period died last week. I cannot actually imagine a theatre without Derek Godfrey; he joined the Old Vic, making a victorious and prophetic debut as the Renaissance villain in Cymbeline, at the exact moment I commenced my education there. His speciality shifted from villainy to witty innocence; if he had a fault, it was an excess of good taste, but if this could be breached (as for example in Marston's The Malcontent) the gleeful demon was let loose. Earlier this year he was at the top of his form, suave, urbane and mellifluous--in Much Ado About Nothing at the Barbican. Personally and professionally, he will be greatly missed.
