Cymbeline transported

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

Productions transported from Stratford to the Aldwych customarily improve in their acting but suffer in their looks. The current Cymbeline is an exception. The performances remain much as they were: Susan Fleetwood's Imogen is lovelier than ever, but an unexciting competence is the mark of most of those who surround her.

Physically, however, the comparatively cramped quarters of the Aldwych work to the play's advantage. A proscenium arch provides a frame excellently suited to this fantastic fairy-story. Certain aspects of John Napier's set still perturb me: when Imogen arrives in Wales she speaks of mountains but the stage is dotted with molehills. At the back of it, however, there is a charmingly artificial blue sky, complete with puffy clouds, which we see in a most satisfying perspective. And though the play is bound to ramble--that is its charm or its curse, depending on your temperament--it does it no harm for its wanderings to be more circumscribed than they were earlier this year.

An acceptance, not to say an enjoyment, of artifice is essential to either the staging or the understanding of Shakespeare's romances. It abundantly marked the recent National Theatre production of The Tempest; after that cornucopia this Cymbeline, chastely set in glowing white curtains, looks ascetic. But asceticism is not always a mark of virtue: and though our companies may from now on be forced to spend less money on their productions, we should not kid ourselves that there is any artistic merit in a diminished budget.

Cymbeline still suffers from the bloodlessness that has marred much of the RSC's work at Stratford, and has also affected their season at The Place, though it seems to have been eliminated from the recent run of plays at the Aldwych. A year ago, I chid them for their lack of humour; they have got merrier since then, or at any rate more knowing. But in Cymbeline this knowingness can be irritating. It permits, for example, Ian Richardson as Iachimo to assume a comic pose and destroy both the tension and the sexuality of the scene in which he steals Imogen's ring while she lies sleeping. I suspect that Mr Richardson could play this role--a sophisticated Italian villain--in his own sleep; it may be unfair that an actor's continual accomplishment should make one tire of him, but there are no surprises in this performance.

Even if one concedes him the right to his little joke in the bedchamber, the profusion of such moments in other performances eats away at the evening. Wit here becomes a matter of decoration rather than texture (as if the RSC were showing off a new selection of ill-fitting funny hats) so that the most nakedly theatrical moment in the play--the descent of Jupiter on his eagle--is greeted with giggles. The directors (three of them are listed, with Barry Kyle now given top billing) flunk this scene despite their obvious awareness of its importance: conceptually, theirs is a very intelligent production.

Background attractions include a courteous Philario (Malcolm Armstrong) and a disturbing buffoon of a Cloten (Charles Keating), at his best immediately before he meets his death. And there is Miss Fleetwood, who has had a good year: meritorious in Comrades, sharp and odd in Summerfolk, and meltingly triumphant here. The role seems to make a princess out of anyone who plays it; I have never heard of a bad Imogen.

In Autosacramentales (Roundhouse) I must declare a lack of interest; this is a 60-minute account of the Creation and Fall derived from the religious dramas of Calderon (the Renaissance Spanish master whom, in the English theatre, no great dramatist is more neglected than) and performed in Portuguese. Naturally, I found it incomprehensible; but not unpleasant to watch. Its cast of 15--totally and permanently nude--have been well drilled by Victor Garcia with, as far as I could tell, not a trace of directorial exploitation.
