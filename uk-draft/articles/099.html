Farewell to that old acid dream

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

AT ONE point in David Hare's Teeth 'n' Smiles (now transferred from the Royal Court to Wyndham's) the following words occur: "It's not worth saying--nothing's worth saying--it's all so obvious." I know the feeling; nobody wants to be caught being obvious, that's obvious.

The situation--a rock group playing at a Cambridge May Ball in the last year of the sixties--automatically suggests a theme: the confrontation of two cultures. Mr Hare defuses our expectations on this score by more or less eliminating one side. Cambridge is represented on stage by one porter and one harmless medical student with ambitions of becoming a drop-out (after he's passed his exams).

If he is the opposition, then the battle is already won, the enemy within the gates. Only they are in no state to fight. The group is on the verge of breaking-up, their lead singer--self-cast in the self-destructive mould of Garland or Joplin--falling apart from drink. Our student asks her whether she believes that pop is any challenge to the established order; she obviously regards it as a silly question. So, I guess, does the author. But I have to guess because he is chary of making statements, even about not making statements.

A strange self-mockery permeates the evening. Sometimes it seems like irony; often, like the hedging of bets. A songwriter who escaped from the old values (he quit this same college) and is still attached to them (he lounges around singing Cole Porter) proclaims that "the acid dream is over," then undercuts himself by adding "it said so in the Daily Express," finally caps it all by telling the singer "but you and I still want to say something." What they want to say, he doesn't say, and she certainly isn't telling.

Mr Hare has perceived that the acid dream was never any use, but this perception is difficult to make into a play, at least into a play with this setting, so sometimes he forgets it and settles for the shadow of a conflict instead. He lets his characters jeer at themselves for paying so much attention to their exhibitionist star, who will survive, while forgetting their stoned bass guitarist, who won't. But after all, the author is the one who gave them the concern, pushed the lady into the centre of the play, and--since he is his own director--engaged Helen Mirren for the role, gave her the limelight, the billing and our attention. He then keeps telling us how boring she is (the character, not Miss Mirren, who is furiously good) as if by way of atonement. He also pads the evening out by engaging her in a romantic triangle which--despite sympathetic playing from Martin Shaw and Gay Hamilton as its other sides--never begins to take hold.

I suspect, too, that he became fascinated in researching the play by the life-style of the band, and threw in more detail than his story required. This, however, is his salvation; much of his dialogue is brilliant and its last-gasp cynicism has its own momentum and its own mood. These keep the play alive most of the time.

The production seems both less comfortable and more uncompromising in the West End than at its cosy old home in Sloane Square. (Its relation to its audience rather resembles that of its characters to Cambridge. I even heard shocked gasps; I only wish the shock had been caused by something more disturbing than the odd four-letter word.) The band's first number now sounds, as it should, really bad: a difficult effect to achieve in a straight play, where any outburst of music is generally welcome. They act contrastingly well, but the two most successful characters are Antony Sher's student, who significantly never finishes a sentence, because its ludicrousness always strikes him en route, and Dave King's manager, smilingly jaundiced about absolutely everything. If the play has a heart, he, effortlessly delivering all the best lines, is it.

Athol Fugard has also directed a play of his own which came to London last week: Dimetos (Comedy), of which I saw half, but heard much less, in Edinburgh last year. This time I stayed till the end: you do not walk out on Paul Scofield, who is awe-inspiringly audible and whose acting is inclined to suggest that he has a hotline to the Almighty. On this occasion he gives a great display, craggy and troubled, rather than a great performance, Mr Fugard having provided him with a situation rather than a character. Derived from an obscure passage in Camus, it presents a man paralysed by guilt over the suicide of his niece, after whom he lusted. He did not assault her; he merely upset her by playing the voyeur.

The second part of the play, which is Dimetos's agony and redemption, is considerably the better, but it sinks under the delusion that a writer may create a myth simply by taking thought. Camus may have achieved it in a casual paragraph, but Mr Fugard, excluding domestic detail in favour of conscientious metaphor, denies his play a chance of life.
