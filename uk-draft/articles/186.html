Mr Deeds goes to prison

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Nottingham, Manchester and London.

On the occasion of Richard Eyre's departure from the directorship of Nottingham Playhouse, four worthy playwrights, all in their time beneficiaries of his regime, have had a whip-round. The total of their contributions is a play called Deeds; described as 'by Howard Brenton and Trevor Griffiths with Ken Campbell and David Hare.'

There are belligerent, bewildered passages that suggest Brenton, others with a northern wit that could be Griffiths. Underneath everything is a wild anger, not exempt from sentimentality, that could be anybody's except, curiously, Griffiths's; his rages are generally more practical. Nor is he usually given, though most of his contemporaries are, to the profusion of incident that marks this play: it is not called Deeds for nothing.

Actually a pun is involved; the hero is called Ken Deed (say it fast) and the play is his quest. There is also a Mrs Deed and, briefly, a baby Deed. The child's death from dehydration is attributed to its being fed on powdered milk, wrongly made up, though Ken only learns this from hospital gossip. (The consultant either didn't know or wouldn't tell: strange behaviour, either way.)

Trying to get, by the nearest means, at the truth about the fatal concoction, he wrecks a supermarket and is sent to prison. Refusing his mates' advice to appeal, he devotes his time inside learning 'to climb': an accomplishment which later enables him to spy on a board-room meeting on the offending firm of Nuzzles Milk and later to break in on their master-mind in his Savoy suite and force him to sign a kind of confession. He also lobbies his MP and a supposedly friendly minister, to no effect, and finally decides to end it all by drowning himself in a railway-truckful of Nuzzles. In the nick of time his wife Mary reappears and persuades him that steeplejacking and self-immolation are no way to make a revolution.

As proof of guilt, the authors resort to showing us their villain frolicking in a bath with a couple of call-girls. Ken on the other hand is healthily monogamous--and at the end of the play Mary is carrying his second child, promising to raise him as a properly indoctrinated avenger. The Puritan backlash is gaining ground in unexpected places--though perhaps the authors are less against pleasure than the fact that it's the rich what gets it.

Mary seems actually a scarcely less exasperating character than Ken; she has communicated with him only through a riddling note, while working things out for herself. But her mental processes, which take place offstage, might have been more interesting than his--might even have included a measure of self-reproach since, however confusing the label on the tin, she was the one who misread it.

The best recent political plays have explored a specific issue and let it reverberate. Deeds skips the issue and jumps to the reverberations which, accordingly, do not reverberate. I doubt if any of the collaborators would have worked so sloppily on his own. The play, with its constant changes of scene, makes a good leaving present for Mr Eyre and he has unwrapped it, greatly aided by his designer, John Gunter, with justified enthusiasm. There is satisfaction in seeing so convincing a rail-yard; and a real thrill when an apparently genuine lift heaves in to view on the revolve, so that we see it first three-quarters on, then in full frontal. (The National Theatre should be so lucky.) The cast oblige too, with Mick Ford in full control of Ken's inflamed doggedness. His linking soliloquies, spotlit downstage, have the impact of a screen close-up; that sounds easy, but I have never seen it managed before. It remains true that a bad epic play is less boring than a bad naturalistic play, but that is a last-ditch justification; did anyone feel Comedians to be confined because it took place in a couple of rooms?

The Deeds intend to breed a killer. The idea of justified murder was tried out in the thirties (theatrically, I mean) by Irwin Shaw in The Gentle People, revived at the Royal Exchange, Manchester. Two refugees, a Jew and a Greek, face another expropriation by a Brooklyn gangster who demands protection for not sinking their treasured fishing-boat. They pay; he demands more. One of them protests; he gets beaten up. The worms really turn; and get away with it.

The unlikeliness of this, particularly the last part, is carefully pointed up; the play is an ironic fable for the age of appeasement--or a sane man's Arsenic and Old Lace; the killers being so endearing and all. They get added justification when the hood (if he's really a mobster, incidentally, where's his mob?) gets to messing around with the Jew's daughter--and she, incredibly, goes along with him, despite being fully in the know. Even Shylock never had it so bad. The play moves too slowly for our tastes (its three acts should be redivided into two), but it does move, and tickle. The Royal Exchange again proves its aptitude for plays set mainly on boats; John Bennett and Harry Landis as the goodies do well against flabby opposition.

Still with murder: at the Roundhouse Downstairs The Boston Strangler speaks disturbingly, mainly in his own words; the actor, John Cunningham, makes him Everyman. There are also some bits that the credited author, Mike Stott, must have written: brief businesslike re-enactments of the stranglings and precedent rapes, determinedly un-titillating, each of the 13 victims being played by the same actress who, mentally at least, is not infinitely undressable. An odd entertainment but, since it grapples with some kind of truth, not contemptible.
