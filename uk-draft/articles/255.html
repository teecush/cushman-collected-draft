Four courses from a Chinese banquet

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

DEMARCATION disputes have arisen over the arrival of the Peking Opera at the Coliseum. Nobody knows whether these productions are the province of dance or drama critics. (Nobody seems to regard them as the province of opera critics.) Alexander Bland, our dance critic, is also writing about them this week, so I shall confine myself to a few remarks on the programme of four short pieces I saw on Tuesday. On that evidence we have here a genre dazzling in form, but simple in content.

Two of the four were battle-pieces, and there were no exhausting moral issues to be faced. In each case one side was good, the other bad. Nor was there any worrying suspense over the outcome. The villains never stood a chance. Their adversaries were invariably fleeter, cleverer, more acrobatic as well as cleaner-living.

In The Phoenix of Fire the combatants are birds: white egrets and black vultures. All are lovely movers; the vultures at one point break into something that looks amazingly like a Cossack dance. Maybe Sino-Soviet tensions are being dramatised. At one point the egrets have a leading egret, but she has a secret weapon: her arms suddenly sprout streamers which she flourishes in their faces. There is one superb circus-routine in which the heroine deflects her enemies' knives faster than they can throw them.

In The Yandang Mountains we have two human armies. Though set in AD 589 this could be a post-revolutionary production. It certainly comes with a post-revolutionary programme note: "Meng Haigong, a hero of the people's uprising against the Aui dynasty, leads his insurgent troops against General Tienlong." Surmounting numerous difficulties and scaling the mountains, he destroys the enemy outposts. "Meng Haigong leads his men in a brave attack."

Actually both sides looked pretty brave to me, and I think the General deserves credit for fighting on when morally, if not physically, outnumbered. But again he just doesn't have his opponents' skills. These include leaping into the enemy stronghold in a ceaseless stream, like acrobatic lemmings, and then immediately rushing out again, victorious, through the front gate.

The bill also included At the Crossroads Inn, the piece that inspired Peter Shaffer's Black Comedy, by depicting, in bright light, actions supposedly undertaken in pitch darkness. The story is of the rescue of an imprisoned general (a good general, this time) by a couple of devoted servants, including an innkeeper who announces himself by turning an insouciant somersault.

Once again, you know who is going to win, but there is plenty of breakneck farce to delay the outcome, with the general's rival rescuers engaged in mortal combat at dead of night. Visually and athletically we have here a feast: a many-coursed Peking banquet.

Wild Oats has returned to the Aldwych with its good nature intact; enhanced if anything. Alan Howard's strolling player Rover, when not in thrall to what a friend calls his abominable habit of quotation, is not only kind, but humble, and his modulations from grandiloquence to homely quietude are the key to the complex playfulness of Clifford Williams's production.

Not only are the men and women merely players, but the player is the completest man among them, apt to regard his fellows as enthusiastic amateurs giving lively but one-dimensional accounts of their roles. Well done, old Abrawang, he calls out (that last nasal syllable is a gift to Mr Howard) as Norman Rodway's testy naval officer stomps about the stage like a man impersonating a testy naval officer. Apart from these two, Raymond Westwell's churlish farmer and Zoe Wanamaker's merrily impressionable maid are welcome survivors from the original cast; and among the newcomers Malcolm Storry soaks up every nuance of hurt pride from a valet who keeps his employer on a tight rein.

The rock Faust (Young Vic) is an updated version; indeed, since it envisions a Britain where the seduced Margaret can be condemned to death for killing her child, it seems to be jumping the gun.

Faced with damnation, this Faust declares that he has never believed in God, which presents a serious obstacle to the story and the character. It makes the pact with Mephistopheles considerably less momentous. The author, Jamie Reid, claims to have followed Goethe, but Goethe's Faust was a noticeably good man; hence the steepness of his possible fall. He was also redeemed at the end, which doesn't happen here.

Mr Reid uses Goethe's story, but his mood is much nearer Marlowe. Faustus begins as a terminally bored scholar, a Fellow of All Souls in fact, bemoaning his over-privileged surroundings in a monologue that the actor, Bill Wallis, does his best to endow with satirical edge. Power leads him to explore the underside of urban society (rotting precincts and plastic pubs) and tiring of that to become dictator, sacrificing human lives for dubious political ends.

Mr Reid has in fact written a reasonably well-observed standard modern play, to which the band occasionally add an authentic note of punk despair. There doesn't seem too much reason to drag Faust into it. Michael Bogdanov's production is efficient, but cannot supply any central vitality; James Carter endows Mephisto with the oily voice and leering demeanour of Leonard Rossiter in undertaker mood, and brings us closer to China by performing on a high-wire and a unicycle, though not simultaneously.

Two notable cabaret singers have arrived in London. At the Country Cousin, Morgana King deploys an unusual vocal range, but is often self-indulgent with the high notes and remorseless to the lyrics. Her interludes of straightforward swing (with superb guitarist Joe Puma) are the best. More importantly, the Playboy Club discotheque--an unlikely and discouraging setting--now houses Bobby Short, the dapper prince of New York singer-pianists. Mr Short is an enthusiastic performer whose pace and volume sometimes defeat themselves. But his repertoire is unbeatable and when he relaxes so is he.

He does a definitive version of I Can't Get Started, one of the great catalogue-songs, pouncing sardonically on every item in the lyric while affirming with his rapt, insistent keyboard work that it is also one of the great ballads.
