Comforting crises

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

REVIEWING `The Norman Conquests' I said that Alan Ayckbourn was not a natural misanthrope. That accusation (or, if you like, that compliment) is firmly refuted by his latest comedy to reach London. Absent Friends (Garrick) is the title, and it turns out to be not a toast but a tautology. Friends are by definition absent.

Standing in for them on the stage of the Garrick are five comforters, comprising between them two and a half marriages. Their prey is Colin, a young man whose fiancee has recently drowned. His old acquaintances, Paul and Diana, invite him to their house for a cheering tea-party, and they summon other couples to make the going easier. There are Gordon and Marg: Gordon cannot make it on account of illness (there is always something wrong with Gordon) but Marg is conscientiously present. And John and Evelyn have come; though Evelyn is not a great hand at cheering. When Colin arrives she hardly talks to him, perhaps because she never knew him. Evelyn, actually, is the kind of girl who has never except in the Biblical sense known anybody.

Evelyn and Paul have had it off, once and dismally, in the back of Paul's car. John knows, but since, for business reasons, he needs to keep Paul sweet he shuts up. Diana suspects, and though she has always been tolerant of Paul's infidelities, thinking herself too boring to deserve his continued interest, on this occasion her patience snaps. So, indeed, does she; she pours cream over her husband's head as if he were a trifle (in a sense I suppose he is) and soon afterwards collapses in hysterics. Marriage was a commodity which she only bought because it came so highly recommended; now she would like to give it back.

Before the tea-party is even under way Mr Ayckbourn has managed to stage simultaneous crises for all three couples (Gordon phones in his contribution) but they pull themselves partially together to console Colin. The joke is that he doesn't need it; Colin is a gladhand artist and though he may have lost his love (`a perfect person,' he calls her in passing) he still has his memories. He is only too willing to pass around the photograph album. He is happy; and he is glutinously eager to make everybody share his happiness. He lectures, cajoles, reminisces; altogether he is a dangerous character. One marriage explodes in front of him; the others ominously smoulder.

One feature that distinguishes Mr Ayckbourn's play from previous West End successes is that nobody ever applauds their sets. His suburban interiors are hardly squalid, you might call them comfy, but they are decidedly not elegant. The same is true of the actors; the author and his director Eric Thompson have provided employment for a string of solid, mildly eccentric performers, of the kind you see in the best situation comedies on television. Once again they do the text proud.

Pat Heywood's Diana, plump and rosy, looks reassuring until you realise how badly she herself needs to be reassured; her breakdown is at once appalling and absurd. Cheryl Kennedy, who used to be a winsome actress, now perches on a stool at the side of the stage, chews gum, and is unreachable. Mr Ayckbourn writes particularly good parts for angular actresses, and Marg provides a splendid outing for Phyllida Law; teetering round the stage with knitting-needles protruding from her torso; looking beadily down a beaky nose, and growing steadily less benign.

Paul is Peter Bowles, who has an individual vocal register; atop his rotund crispness lies an anguished, incredulous squeak on which he draws increasingly often as Colin's amiable posturing becomes increasingly difficult to take. Paul fancies himself as an athlete but Mr Bowles's preliminary prancing is soon eclipsed by the ceaseless activity of Ray Brooks as John. Crawling with tics, he looks as if he needs either to relieve himself or to tapdance.

Mr Brooks's demeanour caps that of the entire quintet; there was once an American musical called `The Nervous Set,' and they might be auditioning for it. They are afraid of one another, of themselves, of Colin. Fearful of saying anything to upset him, they find him impervious to hurt but innocently capable of wounding them.

Colin is altogether a problem, and not only to the other characters. Despite Richard Briers's energetic, rabbity performance he fails (like his catalytic equivalent in `The Norman Conquests') to fill the space required. Either Mr Ayckbourn's nerve or his skill has failed him with Colin. There is no theatrical taboo on death, only on grief; when the bereaved character is as unaffected as Colin, the comedy is no more daring than `Arsenic and Old Lace.' The play's success lies with the married people; Mr Ayckbourn takes an excessive time to set them up, but their collective presence (individually, none of them goes very deep) produces comedy of an unsettling, even saddening kind. There are no belly-laughs, but there is tension.

Matters are contrariwise in Tarantara! Tarantara! (Westminster). This stage biography of Gilbert and Sullivan is the Bristol Old Vic's answer to the Liverpool Everyman's John, Paul (those names again), George, Ringo ... and Bert. It too begins spiritedly, with a rehearsal row between its protagonists; and then plunges into flashback. For Bert read Joe, a stagestruck stagehand who knows all the words and all the facts. But where Willy Russell dared to dramatise the Beatles throughout, Ian Taylor rushes as soon as possible for documentary safety; G (Christopher Scoular) and S (Timothy Kightley) read letters at each other from opposite sides of the stage to denote conflict. They also, along with the rest of the cast, sing songs from the shows; and here lies the evening's salvation.

Since much of the feuding between the collaborators seems to have resulted from the value each attached to his own contribution, it is amusing to find Mr Kightley projecting the lyrics with greater clarity and wit than Mr Scoular. They make a good duo, nicely varied in temperament and whiskers. Mr Kightley is small and beaverish, delighted to find himself in the chorus line, a situation which Mr Scoular endures with lofty disbelief. He imparts a Groucho touch to the girl-chasing judge whom he impersonates in a potted `Trial by Jury,' first and best of the musical excerpts. Though David Horlock's direction is racy, with much adroit changing of costume, the numbers become in time as predictable as the dialogue.

Nobody Knew They Were There (Bush) is ostensibly about three geriatric ladies abandoned in an isolation ward. What it is basically about I am not too sure. Self-delusion, the battle for precedence, and the role of sexual memories in both activities are all present; the richness of detail, even when the structure wobbles, makes Terence Greer the most interesting new writer to appear for some time. His words are well spoken, and his characters beautifully played, by Yvonne Gillan, Marion Fiddick and Gaye Brown projecting a fiery, raucous spirit which never cracks the fragile shell.
