Fairy-tale time

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Shakespeare at the National and Camelot.

THE band strikes up 'If It Wasn't for the Houses In Between,' and we wonder what that has to do with a wood near Athens, or near anywhere else. It then segues into 'Lily of Laguna,' which is even more bemusing, and finally settles on 'Beautiful Dreamer,' which makes sense.

A Midsummer Night's Dream is, if anything is, the great English folk play. Its fairies are a compendium of Elizabethan lore on the homelier supernatural. I had expected Bill Bryden, whose productions at the Cottesloe are generally historically-minded, to explore the play's Elizabethan-ness. In fact, as may be gauged from that opening medley, he places us on the cusp of Victorian and Edwardian. The mechanicals might be the Lark Rise and Candleford Dramatic Society. The lovers are out of Jerome K. Jerome.

Theseus signals the onset of holiday by throwing his bonnet over the windmill, or at least his boater into the audience. Only the grey-green fairies transcend the period. They might come from a nineteenth-century picture-book, but they could equally come from a sixteenth-century one. They are free, and in control.

This production's great concern, and great excitement, is to bring the play's levels together, and yet keep them tantalisingly apart. The fairies are everywhere, overseeing the mortals from every level of stage and auditorium. They bring on the lights that mark out the mechanicals' acting area. Oberon and Titania come face to face with an unseeing Theseus and Hippolyta: a more powerful moment than can be produced by the now orthodox doubling of roles. When the fairies perform their dance of blessing at the end, the mortals return to do their own concentric routine.

The key line might be Puck's 'I'll be an auditor; an actor too perhaps.' When he and Oberon watch the lovers, when Theseus and Hippolyta watch the play, they sit with us. The audience, in a modified form of promenade staging, constitute the play's fourth estate; we too participate and we become part of the party at the finale when Puck's 'give me your hands' becomes the cue for handshakes all round.

Actually I suspect the line just means 'please applaud' and I also suspect that the 'general harmony' interpretation of the play is getting overdone. The different worlds brush, but they do not collide; only Bottom actually meets Titania (and cannot believe it afterwards), and while he and his friends are dismissed by the duke before the end. We are in danger of underrating the elusiveness of this play; I was longing for Puck to shake just one hand, and disappear. If we are to participate, this is as close as we should get.

Which is not to deny the delight of what Mr Bryden has actually achieved. It still lacks an overall rhythm, and there were moments on the first night when it came to an abrupt and puzzling halt, sometimes in the middle of a line. Paul Scofield as Oberon seemed occasionally overcome by the power of his own words; pardonably, since his grizzled, solicitous, sonorous and quietly self-mocking Oberon could raise some very potent spirits.

Susan Fleetwood's sensuous Titania matches him (their quarrel carries unusual weight) and Jack Shepherd knows exactly how to hush the theatre as Puck. Edna Dore, a dewdrop-bearing drudge, admirably rounds out the immortals. The clowns are led by Derek Newark's quietly self-confident Bottom and include a Snout by Tony Haygarth, who, as Wall, drops delightful bricks. The Bergomask is done, with superb devotion, as a four-man sand-dance: Wilson, Keppel, Betty and Friend.

The upper-class ladies--especially Marsha Hunt's Hippolyta and Bernadette Shortt's Hermia--are cowed by the verse; though Jennifer Hall as Helena has some nice vignettes of self-pity. The men are stronger, with Edward de Souza a jovially seignorial Theseus (who actually thinks his way through 'the lunatic, the lover and the poet'), David Rintoul a down-the-line juvenile Demetrius, and Karl Johnson's Lysander emerging, unprecedentedly, as the sharpest character in the piece: a priggishly ardent young man who gives lectures on the course of true love, and carefully arranges flowers for his pillow before sinking in a trance. At its multi-levelled best, this production makes Pirandello look a bit sick, as of course does the play. I preferred it, though of course I don't dare say so, to Peter Brook's.

I have always found the ending of Camelot very moving: on record, on film, and now at the Apollo Victoria. It has something to do with the continuity of tradition; the doomed Arthur, on the eve of his last battle, sending off the boy who is to grow into Sir Thomas Malory. (I know it sounds awful; I can't help it.)

It is also the moment when the title-song justifies itself. In the first scene it combines the worst aspects of jogtrot and jingle (and Camelot proves fatally easy to rhyme); in subsequent reprise it acquires wit; and, at the last, dignity. The one thread that keeps the story going is the birth and erosion of Arthur's idealism; he apparently invents democracy, and then watches it undermined. This is one of the very few male leads in a musical worth acting, and Richard Harris, happy or tormented, does it justice.

Elsewhere one's patience is, admittedly, tried. Alan Jay Lerner's source is not Malory, but T. H. White's Once and Future King, whose reverent jokiness turns to whimsy on the stage. Mr Lerner's lyrics are filled with cute abbreviations and patter-rhythms left over from My Fair Lady. The jousts are the Ascot Gavotte all over again. The best song, the ghostly 'Follow Me,' is hidden away, and others are given unwise prominence; in one attempt at celebration people bound on with flowered hoops and then sing a ditty that begins 'tra la.'

American Anglophilia runs riot; Pellinore, the funny man (well done by Robin Bailey), is an acceptable old stick, except that you suspect the authors of believing he really exists. Lancelot begins as an ineffable twit and in Robert Meadmore's performance stays one; Guenevere (Fiona Fullerton) is giggly. Frederick Loewe's best known tune, 'If Ever I Would Leave You,' does its work, but the resources for frustrated passion are otherwise lacking. The show is almost bound to look like pantomime and in Michael Rudman's production, with variable sets and awful dances, it does. But still.

Waiting (Lyric Studio, Hammersmith) is what people do when a simulacrum of the Yorkshire Ripper is at large. The men mock, fear or despise the women, so any one of them (or us) might have done it. The women fear or despise the men, but they're entitled, because they've been abused, though some women like their men, which just shows how they've been fooled. The story, designed to carry the thesis, limps, though the end has clout. Julia Kearsley writes well, but this play about oppressiveness is itself oppressive.
