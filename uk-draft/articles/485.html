THE EDINBURGH, THE FRINGS IS A PHENOMENOM
Date: 1983-10-02
EDINBURGH

Robert Cushman is theater critic for The Observer.
Walk down the streets of Edinburgh at Festival time and you are likely to get accosted. Two female comedians, brandishing balloons and speaking in well-rehearsed unison, will implore you to come and see their late-night show. Drummers and saxophonists will woo you with what they hope are the hit songs from their new satirical musical, which is all about unemployment but fun. You will undoubtedly meet a red-nosed clown engaged in showering both the sidewalk and the passers-by with leaflets. He may belong to a small home-grown circus; on the whole, though, he is likelier to represent some student theater group from the north of England with startling ideas about the characters in Chekhov.
None of these assailants have anything to do with the official festival of music and drama: the event that every year for three weeks, as August turns into September, transforms the sternly handsome gray-stoned Scottish capital into a tartan Parnassus, complete with hills. Outdoor salesmanship is the hallmark of the fringe: the unofficial festival that has grown up around the formal one.
Nor does the pressure ease off when all the street artists and the shows they promote have gone home. If anything, it intensifies. Planning the fringe is a year-long job, and for Michael Dale, the administrator of the Edinburgh Fringe Society, work on one festival begins as soon as the previous one has ended. Groups from all over the world are at this moment beginning to apply for performing space.
The Edinburgh festival is full of potential customers, but there could never be enough to provide audiences for everybody. ''Everybody'' this year meant 500 separate fringe groups, providing between them 750 shows. And that represents a decline from last year, when the attractions numbered 830. The 500, by the way, are only a small proportion of those who apply to come. Most of them have played on the fringe before; but this year the society received a thousand inquiries from potential first- timers.
Mr. Dale thinks that an important part of his job is to discourage them, or at least to warn them. ''I tell them, before you appear on the fringe, you must come one year as a spectator,'' he says. This is fine if you are based in London; harder if you have to make the trip from Texas. This year the Hip Pocket Theater, a non-professional group from Fort Worth, appeared on the fringe, having raised $83,000 in their community to do it. Mr. Dale, a shrewd 31-year-old former publicist, recounts the figure with some wonder. It is a new threshold in fringe economics.
There was a time when the fringe, as befits its name, was smaller than the festival it surrounded. In 1947, the year of the first festival, a half-dozen enterprising companies gambled on providing the official patrons with something to see on their nights off. The gamble paid off and the fringe grew. For 20 years it was mainly amateur. What really flourished on the fringe was undergraduate revue. The idea - a sound one - was that audiences stuffed with high culture during the afternoons and evenings would like to unwind with something disrespectful late at night. Star attractions have always been the Cambridge University Footlights, a revue club that this year celebrated its 100th birthday and whose alumni include Jonathan Miller and Peter Cook. These two, of course, were half the cast - Alan Bennett and Dudley Moore, both from Oxford, being the balance - of that most successful of all revues, ''Beyond the Fringe.''
It was so successful - in London and in New York - that most people took its title for granted. But in fact it originated, in 1960, as the official festival's attempt to beat the fringe at its own game by staging a late-night show. It worked, better than anyone could have hoped, but it has never been followed up. Revue was handed back to the students. Every year has brought to Edinburgh would-be successors to Mr. Miller and Mr. Moore; every year the commercial producers, and especially the television companies, have stood by to pick the winners. All the ''Monty Python'' team appeared at one time or another, with Oxford or Cambridge groups, on the Edinburgh fringe.
There has been drama, too. In 1958 an Oxford theater group, calling itself the Oxford Theater Group, brought to Edinburgh a military piece that was then entitled ''The Disciplines of War'' but was subsequently better known as ''The Long and the Short and the Tall.'' In 1966 the same company acquired a work by an unknown playwright named Tom Stoppard and gave it a perfectly calamitous production. One critic (Ronald Bryden of The Observer) managed to see something, and enthused. His review came to the attention of the National Theater, which bought the play. It was called ''Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are Dead.'' The rest is history, and a lot of money, some of it even going to the Oxford Theater Group. In subsequent years critics rushed eagerly to earnest amateur premieres of unheard-of writers, hoping that the lightning would strike them, too. Somehow it never did.
Even if they never found anything much, the critics of those days at least had time to look. Now, the size of the fringe overwhelms them. At the beginning of this year's festival, a dyspeptic panel of reviewers declared that it should, in some unspecified way, be curbed, made manageable, become once more a fringe fit for critics to live off. They missed the point. The attraction of the fringe is its monstrosity. Think of Off Broadway and Off Off Broadway gone into overdrive, and then double them. Quantity counts more than quality, and intensity more than either: a joyful kind of claustrophobia prevails. Bars and cafes are crowded and cacophonous.
The atmosphere is cheerfully incestuous; performers double as audiences. They have the chance, since the 500 companies are divided between a mere 133 performing venues. These are run, quite ruthlessly, round the clock, the first group starting its show at 10 in the morning and the last finishing at 3, also in the morning. But there are plenty of outsiders as well: Edinburgh natives of all ages who devour the official festival in a couple of gulps and then plunge into the fringe, eager to catch up with the theatrical world outside.
Your typical Edinburgh audience is sober, literate, enthusiastic and inquisitive. They are joined by the visitors - British, European and American; also enthusiastic, if taken aback by the size of the thing. To the official festival-goer the fringe, even if consumed in bulk, is a bonus and a bargain. It only costs around $3.75 to see the average fringe show. An official event could deprive you of six times the money. Few come to see the fringe alone, but many spend most of their time there. And to see them all jammed together - congratulating, commiserating or just arguing - is a stirring experience. It restores one's faith in the theater as something to travel for and to get excited about.
The intensity has grown with the professionalism. About 50 percent of fringe productions now have Equity casts. The students and other amateurs lost their majority in the years following 1968 when the British theater at large developed its own fringe. The festival frings really wanted to call itself ''the alternative theater movement,'' but the Edinburgh name stuck to it and has never been shaken off. Theater groups dedicated to playing in makeshift spaces at low prices naturally gravitated to the one event at which this kind of theater had already been institutionalized. The Edinburgh fringe and the national fringe have grown together: hence the population explosion.
These days, old plays are easier to come by than new ones. The Edinburgh fringe has never been very experimental or political. What it is increasingly about is entertainment, defined as something theatrical with songs in it. This predominance is partly to do with the national mood (in a recession we crave diversion), but more with the media. TV and radio producers, for example, flock to Edinburgh. There is always the chance one will be signed up for a series. Some names always sell. The Cambridge Footlights troupe still plays to capacity; so do television stars, if they feel like slumming.
Most people play the fringe for kicks and think it worthwhile. There is something pleasurable in being a Bohemian for three weeks; something intoxicating about doing it in the only beautiful city left in Britain. The main thoroughfare, Princes Street, has shops on one side and a castle on the other. It is a city of paradoxes; and the greatest of them is that, once a year, a puritan town can play host to a bacchanal, and hardly show the strain.
