The Rumpscuttle

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN
on two RSC productions

Two rowdy Elizabethan ladies are appearing in tandem at the Barbican, in productions by Barry Kyle.

The Roaring Girl is Moll Cutpurse, who actually lived. She was an adventuress and permanent tomboy ("a Tomrig or Rumpscuttle" -- Anon., 1659), who went through the stews of London living with men on their own terms and beating them: frequently up. She ran with the criminal classes and seems historically to have been one of them. But, since everyone loves a lively lass (at least from a distance), she became a popular favourite and was eventually mythologised upon the stage.

Her play is the joint work of Middleton, arch-realist of his period, and Dekker, the arch-sentimentalist; and the joins show. Dekker obviously lost his heart to Moll, and cannot bring himself to say a word against her. His roaring girl probably bears about the same relation to the original as Lionel Bart's Fagin to Dickens's.

The play is remarkably coy about what Moll actually does for a living; the plot keeps her fully occupied bringing true lovers together, rescuing improvident gallants from the claw of the law, and thwarting heavy fathers who want to have her locked up. Also smoking, of which she was a great pioneer, and, in a sidestep from her main business, violently disabusing a whey-faced gentleman who thinks he can have her: an especially heartening scene.

She is the comic spirit personified -- a transvestite blend of Falstaff and Cupid -- but playing a personification presents difficulties, and Helen Mirren's performance is almost submerged in the general rough-and-tumble. Either Miss Mirren should be more rumbustious or the production less.

She only comes into her own near the end in a bout of thieves' slang: a scene that reads impossible, but acts very well. She then has to translate what has been said for the benefit of some noble tourists, and her blushes are both pretty in themselves and a sudden revelation of character.

A virginal, maybe sexless, heroine is matched against a eunuchoid villain. Her would-be seducer spends most of his time in another, Middletonian, compartment of the play, blackmailing city wives for strictly financial favours. Meanwhile other, more orthodox, blades are trying to get shopkeepers' wives into bed, and their husbands are getting jealous.

It is a familiar routine, but Middleton was very good at it; the play keeps sprouting beguiling details. It is easy to get lost, but best to enjoy each as it comes. None of the performances leaps immediately into your lap, but by the end of the evening you have met a surprising number of people. Praise one, praise all; so let us consider that done and pass on to The Taming of the Shrew.

This is tricky. Shakespeare keeps as many plots going as Middleton and Dekker, but he is far clearer about them. Mr Kyle puts this to the test by adopting at least three separate styles of direction. The Induction is a bit of Merrie England, with extensive roistering, fully-equipped huntsmen led by a polished elderly Lord (Raymond Bowers) and strolling players singing carols.

Christopher Sly's somnambulistic state is carefully stressed (this is the RSC and they know that dreams equal themes) and from time to time he wanders into the play proper, with oddly haunting effect. For the production has now gone way beyond him. The Players prove to be prophetic souls, whose Elizabethanism does not prevent them dressing young lover Lucentio in Edwardian boater and blazer. They are also learned, and make Gremio the pantaloon into a figure of Roman comedy.

It would not be true to say that anything goes; Mr Kyle has the last scene in view, in which the professed romantics prove uxorious fools, and here his conceptions of Hortensio as clod and Lucentio as outright ninny come in very useful. That may console you as you watch these characters being built effortlessly up. The accredited low comics are actually played straight, by Pete Postlethwaite and David Shaw-Parker, and they are far funnier for it.

Most productions play Petruchio and Katherina as in love anyway; only Michael Bogdanov has treated the taming as outright sadism. I think myself that from the moment when Petruchio, at their first meeting, calls his lady "the prettiest Kate in Christendom," the romantics have an open-and-shut case; "Kate" seems to have been the most affectionate word in Shakespeare's vocabulary.

Mr Kyle tries to have it both ways. Alun Armstrong's Petruchio is, in intention, both lout and lover, making the benevolent break when, having denied her new clothes, he lectures Katherina affectionately on the virtues of "honest, plain habiliments." This is to place a lot of weight on one speech, and Mr Armstrong has not yet the vocal command to bring it off.

Sinead Cusack, though, has it to spare; and, after having lapped stick with tremendous intrepidity (falling successively into a pool of water and the front row of the stalls), she delivers her recantation with a transfixing suppleness and wit. Elsewhere in the RSC things have sunk so far that the actors cannot even fit syllables to metre. (Gallant and choleric are specific abominations.)

The playwright wins through. I share Mr Kyle's enthusiasm for the Jacobeans (with whom he has an excellent record), but, frankly, the worst of Shakespeare (The Two Gentlemen of Verona, right?) stands a better chance on stage than the best of his contemporaries: not through genius, but through competence.

Some of the magic has flown in the transfer of Mr Cinders from King's Head to Fortune. We lose the sense of collusion with the performers, the dances are harshly exposed, and some alterations in Tony Craven's production seem perverse; where are the heads that once peered so engagingly through holes in the drop-curtain, getting the show off to an irresistible start? It now seems a long time, and a lot of songs about tennis and blue blood, before Denis Lawson comes on.

Once he does, he takes over; there is still enough of Pal Joey about him to lend a discontented edge to his male Cinders, an edge which increases his charm. He has resentments to work off; he has to distance himself from his oppressors by transforming domestic drudgery into a Dying Swan routine. These are the resources that entitle him to sing "Spread a Little Happiness" (the greatest of cheer-up songs, winsome not aggressive) with hard-won conviction. His South American explorer's song, "On the Amazon" (full of lethal polysyllables), is now diversified with a sand-dance and he exits insouciantly across a table.

His new girl is Christina Matthews, who acts pertly and sings sweetly, and unlike her predecessor does not have to look her father full in the face as he sings "She's My Lovely" at her, which is anyway an interpolation from another Vivian Ellis score. There was an international musical-comedy style in the Twenties, with its roots in Wodehousian upper-crust farce; English Mr Ellis shares composing credit with American Richard Myers, whose rhythms syncopate after Gershwin, and whose ballads modulate after Kern.

The show plods at times, and I would not like to explain the thought-processes of the villain. But much of it is really funny, and the final glass slipper variation is a delight.

White Gloves (Lyric Studio, Hammersmith) is a full-length combination spoof of Sherlock Holmes and The Cherry Orchard that has about 20 minutes life in it.
