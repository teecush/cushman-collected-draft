A fine life, a velvet life

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Dekker's 'The Shoemakers' Holiday' at the National.

JOHN DEXTER'S production of Dekker's The Shoemakers' Holiday (Olivier) comes along 17 years after his revival of that other cordwaining classic, 'Hobson's Choice.' In a properly programmed world the National would be running the two in repertoire under such an appropriate generic title as 'A Load of Old Cobblers.'

Between the Elizabethan London of 'The Shoemakers' Holiday' and the Victorian Manchester of 'Hobson's Choice' the trade obviously shrunk. Henry Hobson had a staff of two, who were bullied, underpaid and kept chained up in the cellar. His ancestor, Simon Eyre, runs a happy and populous street-level shop, full of incipient trade union spirit. When Eyre, finding himself in an upwardly mobile situation, moves on up, his foreman takes over and the business expands, apparently giving employment to half the National Theatre company.

Hobson had three daughters to bully; Eyre only has a wife, but what he lacks in familial scope he makes up for in creativity. 'Midriff,' he calls her, and 'Dame Clapperdudgeon.' But they love each other; and are fairly benevolent towards everyone else.

The National's publicity describes the play as a 'romantic and bitter comedy,' the implication being that they wouldn't dream of wasting our time on comedy that wasn't bitter. Well, there are undertones. One of Eyre's journeymen is pressed to the French wars and returns minus a leg. Anyone considering turning 'The Shoemakers' Holiday' into a musical should note that here is a gift to lyricist and choreographer alike: a character who hobbles as he cobbles.

His homecoming coincides with Eyre's appointment as sheriff of London; the scene is masterfully orchestrated by Mr Dexter (Dekker having provided him with a wonderful score) and the crucial moment has the audience catching its breath. One of the subtleties is the guilty reaction of another journeyman who happens to be a nobleman, AWOL for amatory reasons and further disguised as a Dutchman. This is one of the few roles in world drama to combine the functions of juvenile lead and dialect comic.

At the end, after much banqueting and reconciliation, war closes in again; but the production makes surprisingly little of it. We are now in an unalterably happy mood, greatly fostered by the appearance of David Yelland as a King (unnamed but palpably Henry V), amiably bored with the royal routine and going through it at double speed.

Nor is a class-warfare theme easy to activate, since what the play exudes is class-content. 'By the Lord of Ludgate,' says Simon, now Mayor Eyre, 'it's a stirring life, a fine life, a velvet life.' Citizens are happy to be citizens, nobles like being nobles and shoemakers are almost insufferably chuffed about spending their lives making shoes. We don't meet any beggars.

There is some rancour when lines are crossed--when a grocer's daughter marries an earl's nephew or a gentleman tries to abduct a seamstress--but it is soon dispersed. All know their place; and Mr Dexter does a first-rate informative job of keeping them there.

Eyre himself is unexpectedly downbeat; the role is played by Alfred Lynch, a good actor of terrier temperament but lightweight. Whether casting determined interpretation or vice versa I wouldn't know, but though Mr Lynch is bemused, benevolent, prudent or shifty as required, his voice, a piping twang, deprives Eyre's rumbustious invective--and hence the play--of flavour and swing.

Prominent supporting members of the Lynchmob are John Normington, sober and shop-stewardly; Peter Lovstrom, hopping mad; and, pre-eminently, Brenda Bruce, who as Mistress, later Lady, Eyre (all right, the Eyress) copes wittily with hooped gowns of increasingly fearsome dimensions.

In the world outside too many roles go for too little. Exceptions are Selina Cadell, as ever; Nicholas Selby, a heavy father with a prodigy of a forked grey beard, (who scores the wittiest of the play's myriad double-entendres by remaining apparently oblivious of it; and Michael Beint, in the microscopic role of a swag-bellied Hollander. Julia Trevelyan Oman's three-panelled set handsomely summons the spirit of London, which the evening finally celebrates. You can have fun just collecting the place-names.

At the Theatre Upstairs a company magically entitled That's Not It presents a piece of feminine performance art, and manages to get the best of two frequently oppressive worlds.

The show is called Room and it takes off from Virginia Woolf's demand for one of her own. A writing wife, looking like a skivvy, asks for space, spiritual and especially physical, in which to live, evoking the social and domestic hindrances, invoking predecessors from Mrs Woolf back to Mrs Behn.

Natasha Morgan functions as writer-deviser, as soft-spoken doe-eyed leading actress and, most importantly, as director, in which role she makes excellent use of simultaneous action and of simple clutter. One moment in which sheaves of paper--presumably written by women--rise from the ground to the vertical where they proceed to hang accusingly, is inspiration.

Last Sunday the Oklahoma! cast gave a lively account of Minnie's Boys, a 10-year-old American show about the early days of the Marx Brothers. Confused as to whether it's really about star-struck Mom (pre-empted by 'Gypsy') or her brood (post--I suppose--empted by 'A Night in the Ukraine,') it nevertheless boasts some numbers which outclass anything more recent that's currently on view: music, Larry Grossman; lyrics, Hal Hackady.

Most recent of the recents is Wonderland, staged on a scale unprecedented at the King's Head and niftily choreographed by Tudor Davies. However, it too is confused with plot-lines going off into nowhere. It concerns Hollywood, chiefly during World War II; and it implicitly arraigns the movies for dealing in stereotypes. For issues the show itself has a Jew who pretends to be a Gentile and, for comic-pathetic effect, a vice versa. Quite apart from not being averse to a bit of tinsel itself, it cannot bring its own characters to life: The lummox who makes good by accident, the temperamental German director. (What, twice in a lifetime?) The cast are good, Dave Brown's music musicianly, William Fairchild's lyrics often the prisoners of their rhyme-schemes.

You couldn't say that about Donna Franceschild's song-words for Tap Dance on a Telephone Wire, but if I go on about their sloppiness you'll think I'm developing a nervous tic. At half-time they drove me out of the theatre, more in anger than in sorrow. A pity, since visually this is by far the best show yet at the Tricycle; Mary Moore's black-and-white set brilliantly evokes a Los Angeles telephone exchange, where none of the all-female workers is happy.

Again the cast are good; Susan Glanville, as the supervisor, offers a model of what I believe is Brechtian acting, meticulously separating nice person from nasty role. There are day-in-the-life scenes which are interesting, and gloom scenes which are gloomy. It's a trunk-call away from Judy Holliday and... Bells are Ringing; the brutal truth behind those switchboard messages of goodwill. Have a nice week.
