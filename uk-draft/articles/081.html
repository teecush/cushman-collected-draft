Rocky road for Bunyan

I LIKE the Edinburgh Festival; I want it to go on. But I cannot feel so benevolent towards the three plays which opened in the official festival last week. I wanted them to stop.

Pilgrim is a rock musical based on Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress. It is what likes to be known as a life-enhancing show, very loud and bouncy. It is odd to father such a thing on a seventeenth-century Puritan allegorist, who presumably abhorred the theatre; piquant to encounter it in that august and uncomfortable pile, the Assembly Hall of the Church of Scotland (has it ever been so wired and bugged before?); and downright perverse to cut the story from its historical and theological moorings, so that it becomes a series of pointless episodes. Bunyan would have found it, in the literal sense, graceless; the odd inexplicable religious symbol may be glimpsed floating in a sea of spurious modernity.

Despite their choice of title, the Prospect Theatre Company have essentially dumped the Pilgrim but kept the Progress. Christian is still the star; obviously, since he is played by Paul Jones at his most worried and well-meaning. But he is a Christian without guilt, which is absurd.

This year the Assembly Hall stage is saucer-shaped. The cast begin by bounding on to it, all jeaned and jubilant, letting us know that they are happy (after all this is the opening number) and that they have no room for prophets of doom. Here, they say, while keeping their balance on the edge of the saucer with admirable aplomb, is their world spinning round and round. On this slender provocation Christian (a flat-earth man?) decides to leave home and to forsake what he calls 'this life of spiritual death.' Well, all right, some people don't like musicals, and it is probably very vexing to live next door to a juvenile chorus.

Given these austere tastes, it is odd that Christian should leave home, at the prompting of the somersaulting quick-change artist (name of Evangelist). But he does. Perhaps he merely wanted to get away from his wife, though she haunts him through the show, causing him to pause in his wanderings and participate in long-distance duets. She wants to know why she has been left alone, but he can't come up with an answer; maybe wedlock and salvation are incompatible and he's really an Early Christian.

No, he really is just one of the roving kind, a compulsive musical-comedy tripper. Keep on Travelling Life unrevelingly is his motto, often repeated (sometimes without accompaniment, which makes it sound worse). If travel broadens the mind, it conveniently lengthens the show, though when undertaken without a definite object it can be monotonous, and Christian does seem to keep having the same adventure everywhere he goes. His meeting with Apollyon differs little from his meeting with Giant Despair, neither of them having any noticeable allegorical significance and both of them being the same actor. And he can't get away from that chorus; they keep coming on looking devoutly dynamic or devoutly decadent or just devout. At Vanity Fair they give their androgynous slink and in Prating Row their Hilly Billy stomp, in which they are led by Mr Talkative.

Camped up by Paul Nicholas (who has earlier rendered the same service to Mr Worldly Wiseman), he makes rather a pleasing impression, though the script breaks into genuine Bunyanese to express its disapproval. All his heart lies in his tongue, and his religion is to make a noise therewith.

This seems a fair description of the entire show. When Christian finally gets to the Celestial City, the gang's all there, looking holy. The show washes its hands of religion, and Mr and Mrs Christian declare, in a closing-piece of rhythmic uplift, that there is a time for everything: a time to live, and a time to die, and you know the way they go on.

The production (Toby Robertson) has drive and the music (Carl Davis) has drive and even melody. But both are synthetic; if the authors have treated Bunyan cavalierly (of course he was used to that), they have stuck all too conscientiously to a book of theatrical rules already musty. For a youth-oriented -- indeed child-oriented -- musical, with some original life to it, I commend Venus and Superskid at the Roundhouse in London.

Unlike Pilgrim, Bill Bryden's production of How Mad Tulloch Was Taken Away (Lyceum) can't be accused of excessive brightness. Mr Bryden puts his actors in pools of light but surrounds them with darkness. This first play by Tom Morris is written in short scenes, to each of which Mr Bryden and his designer Hayden Griffin allot the necessary minimum of suggestive setting, whisked on and off the huge bare stage. Actors are disposed in sculptural groupings.

General murk, though soporific and ruinous to the legibility of critical notes, is meant, I would guess, to signify the darkness in Mad Tulloch's mind; he is a Scottish soldier who spends most of the play in inner turmoil, only achieving clarity in the last scene for which, sure enough, Mr Bryden switches the brights up. Tulloch is finally sure of his sanity; when other characters, strong in the opposite belief, approach him he calls out: "They're not coming for me, they're coming to me." That's good, but he adds: "What beautiful things human beings can be," suggesting that, if he has not gone crazy, he has at least gone Hollywood.

It is just such idealistic remarks that have earned Tulloch the reputation for insanity in his own circle. The Highland Infantry must be a singularly repressive organisation to be so roused by routine expressions of trust in common humanity and distrust of social mechanisation. That is about all the provocation Tulloch gives, though he does give it at extreme length. He is a particular bete noire of a punctilious sergeant who seems, in Rikki Fulton's performance, to have come from another, funnier play; the audience perceptibly cheered up whenever Mr Fulton came on, thereby giving Mr Morris counsel as good as any critic is likely to offer.

There is also an officer's wife who stands in the spotlight opposite Tulloch and addresses him in the frigid tones in which high-born ladies in plays are accustomed to address orderlies. She gets him to bed, no problem, but ultimately he is the death of him. Her husband, whom she has taunted for his homosexuality, shoots Tulloch, proclaiming: 'I had the right.' The production may be blamed for the play's portentousness; perhaps it seems naive.

The challenge of Andy Fuggard's play is persistently sounded too literary for his characters. In Dimetos (Church Hill) he solves this problem by placing everyone in a mythological void. The words are resonant but the combination of labour and narrative makes action slack, and setting abolishes action. Both Mr Morris and Mr Fuggard have heroines called Lydia; that exact property was sufficiently promising to have reached the Festival fringe. I shall report next week.
