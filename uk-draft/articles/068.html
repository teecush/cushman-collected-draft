The obscurity of Miller's Earnest

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

A DIRECTOR who casts Irene Handl as Lady Bracknell is plainly not attempting a conventional production of The Importance of Being Earnest, but just what Jonathan Miller is after in his revival at Greenwich never becomes clear.

Miss Handl being nobody's grande dame, one assumes that he intended to take Wilde's characters down a few pegs, by both emphasising and undercutting their snobbery. The key line here becomes Algernon's "Really, if the lower orders don't set us a good example what on earth is the use of them?" and the key relationship is that between him and his manservant. Should Alan Hay's Lane wish to remain in domestic service, I can furnish him with a reference. He is meek but not obsequious when patronised or bullied and stands patiently, cynically by while the young master tears up the latest line of an obviously endless line of bills.

Greed is exposed as mercilessly as improvidence; the devouring of the muffins is as fine and furious a display as I have seen. Elsewhere the exchanges between David Leech's quite competent Jack and Robert Swann's Algy (whose nasal slur may be a gift from his director) are decidedly - or rather undecidedly - choppy. They fumble too many lines to be funny, but if it's laughter you're after, you don't go to Mr Miller in one of his Puritan moods. This bias might also account for Charlotte Cornwell's Cecily, not so much a pink rose as a big baby, so rude to everyone from the outset that her behaviour to Gwendolen loses all its surprise and half its comedy. This scene is, however, much enlivened by Angela Down, quelling with a shudder any suspicion that she might once have seen a spade.

Actually, by the time this point is reached any idea of sustained interpretation has long been abandoned, and both actors and audience are grabbing desperately at any passing joke. The chief beneficiary is Benjamin Whitrow's Chasuble, a rector whose ministry seems to be someone like that of Silly Walks and whose double-take at the news of Ernest's decease is quite stunning. No less memorable is Joan Sanderson's Miss Prism, passing judgment on the absent and the dead with the unanswerable precision of a spectacle-case snapping shut.

Why so formidable a female should cower before this Lady Bracknell is a mystery. In theory Mr Miller is entitled to treat a classic any way he chooses; in practice a director, if he wishes to offer anything more substantial than a parlour game, is obliged to make sense of a play. And in allowing Miss Handl to be described as a Gorgon, Mr Miller makes nonsense. There is, to be sure, some charm in her performance; she acts her way out of the legendary handbag with finesse, and (on the first night seemed compounded of nerves and little else) it is fascinating to count the lines that she can make work for her. She sounds mysteriously Central European, disarming opposition not through patrician obstinacy but by impenetrable gemutlich silliness. What Miss Handl does to Lady Bracknell is nothing to what the real Lady Bracknell would do to Miss Handl.

The Theatre at New End is liable to close, penniless, at the end of this month. On its past record I would not mourn it excessively, but the current production, Peter Handke's Kaspar, though it may not inspire a reprieve, certainly qualifies as a redemption. Actually it is rather surprising that one should have to go to Hampstead (to the Theatre Club for The Ride Across Lake Constance last year and now to New End) to find a Handke play; he should surely be in the repertories of our major companies. When these were founded there was an unspoken assumption that they would look to Germany for much of their modern work: to unperformed Brecht, to Max Frisch and Friedrich Durrenmatt. But the Brechtian supply ran out, and the plays of his successors no longer seemed very interesting. Handke is the first writer to fill the space they vacated: an authentic instant classic.

Kaspar Hauser was an autistic 16-year-old who appeared in Nuremberg in 1828 with the ability to speak a single sentence: "I would like to become a horseman such as my father once was." Handke strips him of even that much particularity. Kaspar's one sentence becomes "I want to be someone like somebody else was once"; the play shows how he is converted into someone like everyone else is now. The historical setting has been removed; the play takes place in a single, modernistic white room. For much of the time Kaspar is the only character on stage; in the later scenes he is joined by three silent replicas of himself. But his main antagonists are offstage voices; it is they who turn him into a model citizen. They pour words into his ears, so that he loses his hold on his one sentence; then set about teaching him new sentences, full of humane advice and cautious counsel. Not the least chilling aspect of the instruction process is that, for much of the time, it would be difficult to disprove that it is being done for Kaspar's own good.

The play demonstrates not the impossibility of communication (that hoary old dramatic alibi), but its fearful efficacy; language, the vehicle of thought, is also the most potent instrument for restricting it. What we call education can also be indoctrination, and in Kaspar history has presented Handke with the perfect metaphor. As enacted by Richard Tresson, he is his own dogged self, an individual before he is a symbol. At the beginning Mr Tresson flops about the stage, bumping into things and directing at them the only words he knows. At the close he is fully co-ordinated, but equally at the mercy of objects: he knows their names and the words oppress him. It is difficult to say which situation is the more poignant.

Handke's ability to give theatrical form to metaphysics is greater than that of any playwright since Samuel Beckett. Like Beckett's, his technique can be monotonous and the most conscientious spectator is likely to lose his grip somewhere. But he is also likely to recapture it; neither playwright generates the arid boredom of which I had cause to complain last week.

Events in an Upper Room (ICA) is a modern-dress version of the Easter story (Joseph of Arimathea, a confused liberal), which communicates mainly satisfaction at its own ingenuity. The actors helped to write it, but otherwise they have not done badly.
