Amy's love letters

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on two plays by novelists.

BERNICE RUBENS'S masterly novel I Sent a Letter to My Love concerns a self-consciously ugly woman in a small Welsh seaside town, whose life is devoted to tending a crippled, and possibly dying, brother.

In her late fifties, she places a lonely-hearts ad, with a box-number, in the local paper. She receives one reply: from her brother. So she invents a persona, under which she conducts an increasingly passionate correspondence with him. By the end of it she is in love with him, while his passions have been so awakened that he becomes an easy mark for the spinster down the road, his sister's best and only friend. When their engagement is announced, our heroine's legs fail her; it is her turn to look forward to a life of being fussed over.

Miss Rubens has a dry way of telling this bizarre tale, one that keeps sentimentality, with the characters themselves, at arm's length. But in her dramatised version, now on view at Greenwich, she has changed her approach. Sister Amy, brother Stan, now tell their own story, and Amy is especially loquacious in a style that wavers between conventional soliloquy and unbuttoned direct address. By the second act Rosemary Leach as Amy has progressed far enough in intimacy to address the audience as 'my lovelies' - an unwarranted liberty, I thought.

When Miss Rubens invents a scene from outside the book - as when Stan confronts the actress whom Amy has hopefully, if improbably, hired to impersonate his unseen beloved - she comes up with something vigorously, if a bit crudely, farcical. It entertains, but it also jars.

The meat of the story, in whatever medium, is the correspondence of Stan and Amy, and in this version we are a long time both approaching and retreating from it. There is also the question of Miss Leach's looks, which are certainly not Amy's (not as described in the book, at any rate) and which makes her long shelf-life difficult to credit. I know this is what reviewers always say about leading actresses in plain roles, but then it's usually true. And make-up alone would not solve the problem.

As it happens, Miss Leach gives as warm, expressive, witty, tactful and malicious a performance as has sprouted in London this year. All this vitality is as much part of Miss Leach's attractiveness as physical beauty could be, and she would hardly command our attention - could hardly in fact be an outstanding actress, or even an actress at all - without it. The supporting cast are good, and if Miss Rubens has impaired and softened her original creation, she has not destroyed it. If there is still a place for matinee plays, this is the best in a long time.

Another lady novelist, Fay Weldon, has contributed a far more economical piece in Action Replay (Orange Tree), which traces the lives of three women from their student flat-sharing days in 1954 to their widowed and divorced flat-sharing days now. Also presented are the three men with whom they are severally, and sometimes collectively, involved: no doubt it is chauvinism that makes me ascribe the comparatively lustreless impression made by these males (whose only scene together is conducted by candlelight, a dispiriting fact in itself) partly to deficiencies in the writing and in Julia Pascal's direction - both of which, where the women are concerned, are admirably taut.

Anyway, it is the actresses - Katharine Schofield, Stephanie Turner, Janet Key - who make the running. Miss Turner has a rare directness, every resource in perfect order, as a self-proclaimed easy lay progressing to marriage by increasingly embittered stages, and Miss Key complements her pungency with a befuddled warmth that eventually evolves into its own mode of attack. But the play's principal interest is its construction. Though central events - marriages, divorces, births and deaths - remain constant, each step between is presented as a series of variants; the same opening gambit is developed in three or four different ways.

Enough lines are thrown around concerning the operation of chance or destiny on human lives for us to believe that Miss Weldon has a theme here. I think myself that she comes down in favour of destiny, considering how little is the effect of what her characters say or what they actually do. (Here, perhaps, is a belated reply to J. B. Priestley's Dangerous Corner, in which a chance remark was shown to have the direst effects.) But it is the device itself which tickles the fancy, and keeps play, cast and audience on their toes.

The Guise was the title of a lost play by John Webster, presumably concerned with that favourite demon-figure of the Elizabethan drama, the architect of the St Bartholomew massacre. One of David Mowat's contentions in his identically-named piece at the Theatre Upstairs seems to be that Webster's was a work well lost; his pastiche of it, though conscientiously bedecked with double-entendres and images of pond-life, hardly does the master credit ('for years now I, Guise, have chafed at this situation,' says the hero-villain, carefully identifying himself as if he were in a radio production); though the scenes of actual butchery do have a carnivorous relish that seems to derive from Marlowe's earlier play on the same subject, The Massacre at Paris.

Mr Mowat's play-without-the-play posits a revival of the Webster (formerly suppressed for fear of giving offence to royalty) immediately before the closing of the theatre by the Cromwellians. This occasions much devotional talk of 'theatre' by the endangered actors. Now the Elizabethans and their successors did not, to judge from their writings, talk in this way; they spoke of 'theatres' (good concrete things), perhaps even of 'the theatre,' but they steered off meaningless abstractions.

In making them talk as we unfortunately do, Mr Mowat has rendered his play 'relevant' in the unhappiest way. But beyond this, and leaving aside the comfortable feeling that theatres were coming under official threat both then and now, there is little to relate his play to us, or indeed Webster's supposed drama to his own. The production is heavy, the actors mostly speaking with a worried reasonableness that gets individual lines over, but obscures whatever larger pattern may lie behind. Only Adrian Vaux's timbered set for the Fortune Theatre (theirs, not ours) hits the right note of gorgeous simplicity, and makes incidentally a fine double with his work, at the opposite extreme of expenditure, on My Fair Lady.

Spoon River (Soho Poly) is an adaptation of a long poetic sequence by Edgar Lee Masters depicting life in an Illinois small town between the American Civil and First World Wars. The inhabitants rise from the churchyard and tell us their mostly macabre stories. This is Thornton Wilder without the cloying uplift, Our Town with the Grover's Corners knocked off. Spoon River seems to have contained an abnormal proportion of girls who went wrong in some picturesque way or other; Beth Porter, playing most of them, distinguishes them juicily and resourcefully, though she and her three adroit colleagues seem at unnecessary pains to disguise the fact that what they are speaking is verse.

With which I retreat for a month to the other side (to appear in an Ogden Nash revue at the King's Head, to be precise), bequeathing a four weeks' supply of bouquets and brickbats to Steve Grant.
