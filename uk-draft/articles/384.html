From Papp to Pope

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'The Pirates,' 'The Jeweller's Shop' and 'Berenice.'

UNLIKE The Pirates of Penzance, who stymied themselves by practising fair play and kindness to orphans, Joseph Papp knows how to make money. Mr Papp runs the New York Shakespeare Festival in Central Park and the multiple Public Theatre in Greenwich Village. These operations have largely been financed by the profits from 'Hair' and 'A Chorus Line,' which originated at the Public, and the revival of 'The Pirates' was explicitly planned to carry on the good work. It started in the Park, where all who saw it seem agreed it was at its best, then moved to Broadway's looming Uris Theatre, where it still remains. It has now reproduced itself at Drury Lane; meanwhile the film-version waits.

'The Pirates' has claims to be considered one of the first American musicals; for copyright reasons, Gilbert, Sullivan and Richard D'Oyly Carte put it on in New York before bringing it to London. In lavishing on it the techniques of contemporary Broadway, Mr Papp's director, Wilford Leach, is merely returning a compliment. Actually, the compliment is still double-edged: G&S do as much for Broadway as Broadway does for them. It is a relief to see showmanship applied to a decently humorous text and score. For once all that leaping about seems justified. Well, most of it. The production has enough energy, invention and goodwill to pleasure any audience. Bear that in mind while I dissect it.

The music has been spicily orchestrated for, among other things, the merriest synthesizer you ever heard: all to the good. The performance style was set in New York by Kevin Kline, who, as the Pirate King, combined the looks, bravado and open-shirt sexiness of Flynn and Fairbanks with the cheeky self-mocking agility of the young Burt Lancaster (remember 'The Crimson Pirate'?) His pirate-technics infected the entire company, especially the choruses. There are, you may recall, three of these: Buccaneers, Bobbies and Beautiful Maidens.

In London the pirates retain most of their zest, dressed to the eight-and-a-halfs and carrying on to match. The police have lost a bit: conceived as wind-up Keystone Kops, they now fly through the air at less extraordinary angles than they did. General Stanley's daughters are presented as hysterical virgins, achieving by hard work effects that D'Oyly Carte ladies would have lumbered into by accident.

When Frederic, the virtuous pirate apprentice, sings and gyrates a pop aria at them, they practically wet themselves. Unfortunately the plot dictates that they reject him. The plot is of course nonsensical, but the players ought to believe in it. It is mock melodrama; and this show too often spoofs the spoof. It also treads on some perfectly good jokes.

The production is at its best when its extravagance accords with Gilbert's own: when the action suddenly stops dead for a full-stage Handelian ode to poetry, and above all when the pirates enter 'with cat-like tread,' which means with stamping feet and percussive accompaniment, singing at the tops of their voices, and about 27 abreast. This leads to a riotous chase-sequence.

Three performances outstrip their originals. George Cole, dressed for a tour of the pyramids, is a splendidly pointed Major-General. (Incidentally you don't have to be a besotted Gilbertian to suspect that the inept couplet, 'But still at getting off my daughters, eight or nine or 10 in all, I've shown myself the model of a modern major-general' was inserted into the finale by another hand, presumably piratical.) Annie Ross's Ruth is a bespectacled Scots gentlewoman, which does very well. Above all, Michael Praed sings Frederic into his rightful place at the centre of the opera; some gaucherie in his acting actually works to this priggish hero's advantage.

As Mabel, his beloved, Pamela Stephenson has inherited a mould--of a music-box shaped like a china doll--fashioned for the non-acting talents of Linda Ronstadt, but she makes it as funny as she can. Tim Curry's Pirate King is very acceptable if you've seen Mr Kline, and probably wonderful if you haven't.

From Papp to Pope. Karol Wojtyla's The Jeweller's Shop (Westminster) arrived in London three days before its author, giving theatregoers the chance of actually being a papal audience. There is a humane mind at work in this piece, but little instinct for drama; the author is probably better off in his other job. (He wrote this in 1960 when he was Bishop of Cracow.) What happens is that three couples parade outside a symbolic store; their wedding rings are only worth as much as the state of their marriage. Since we always seem to arrive after hours, this has to be explained to us, like much else, by a raisonneur called Adam. From the audience's point of view the main trouble with the relationships is that the participants never talk to each other. They only talk to Adam and--at length--to themselves.

Adam's line seems to be that they are in danger of substituting earthly love for divine love and so devaluing both; but he does not quite go uncontested. There is a brief and effective boost for human feeling, delivered by John Carson, but you wait a long time for it. Of the other players, Gwen Watford injects some feeling into a strictly externalised text. Robin Phillips's production, with pretty Perspex sets and wistful music, is reminiscent of 'The Umbrellas of Cherbourg,' which may not be so inappropriate at that.

A more respectable minimal action play is at the Lyric Studio: Racine's Berenice in fact, in which an Eastern queen, kept waiting for years by a prospective Roman emperor, realises that once in power, he will never marry her. Meanwhile a rival Oriental loves her, but decides he is not going to take what another has left. So we have a drama of triple abnegation, sadistically interlocked. The problem, crudely put, is how to pump some passion into it. Let us assume that French actors are helped by the text; they got rhythm. John Cairncross's translation is written in bloodless iambics. Like most English versions it is kinder to confidants who merely advise than to principals, who have to suffer.

The modern setting of Christopher Fettes's production is less offensively facile than the police-state ambience of his Britannicus. Indeed Kandis Cook's set, suggesting an antechamber to a Sartrean Hell, is excellent in its ominous politesse. But the suits and dresses are no help. Mr Fettes is very good at imposing concentration on both his actors and his audience, though the latter seemed to be responding with devotion rather than enjoyment.

Only at the end is there real illumination; Sheila Gish as Berenice, released from the shackles of hope, finds freedom in accepting despair; freedom expressed, as it should be in French tragedy, through restraint. The tension, in all senses, is tremendous.

Pam Gems's Queen Christina (Tricycle) is a superb play, though acted less rigorously than it demands. I hope to return to it next week.
