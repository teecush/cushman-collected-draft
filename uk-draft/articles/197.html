Old pals act

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

ONE OF the postulates of Ben Travers's Plunder (Lyttelton) is that our two heroes, Freddy Malone and D'Arcy Tuck, were at school together.

The memory inspires them both; it prompts D'Arcy to help Freddy in his house-breaking, jewel robbing schemes (the house is actually Freddy's own, but the rocks certainly aren't) and it prevents Freddy from bashing D'Arcy's silly head in. John Standing now plays Malone to Dinsdale Landen's Tuck, and as the two of them loll against a table, breathing insouciant defiance of all who have not had their advantages, their common background is easy to credit.

Two years ago, when Frank Finlay played Freddy as a shark with apparent South American connections, this vital element was missing. But Mr Finlay had a pace, bite and attack which were extremely valuable; and which, on his opening night at least, were not supplied by his successor. Mr Standing let the side down (no other phrase will do) and there is no actor of whom I would have expected it less.

Mr Landen, however, has refined his performance to the point of ineffability. I never really knew what this was before, but it seems to involve a centre parting, much brilliantine, more good will and a perfect innocent balance between love and avarice. He has sacrificed a few first act laughs in the interests of clear narrative and characterisation, while gaining many in his Scotland Yard interrogation, which is now the pearl it should be (His Majesty's police help to polish it) and is triumphant as ever in the actual commission of crime. Michael Blakemore's direction of both these scenes is masterful.

Derek Newark, Polly Adams (a cut-glass heroine) and Trevor Ray have also survived to advantage, and Peggy Mount has arrived as a harridan worth defying and a decided gain to boot.

The Open Space's American season continues with a visiting show, a genuine piece of rubbish called The Ball Game, which takes farcical satire to be the art of dismembering dummies. It has Deborah Norton characterising hard and strong, and Maureen Lipman, who is so appealing that any man must yearn to rush out into the night (apparently a dangerous proceeding in Pittsburgh, where the action is laid) and find a better play for her.

You get a higher class of Americana at Madisons Supper Room, a much needed new resort at Camden Lock, where Gotham, a trio offering skilled close harmony singing and more questionable close harmony jokes, have given place to Peter Allen, who smiles and cavorts at the piano like some inspired combination of Liberace and Jerry Lee Lewis while giving enthusiastic voice to his own songs, which are usually rhythmic and sometimes touching. Gotham mostly offered old songs; Mr Allen offers new ones which sound like old, and much better than such things have a right to be.
