Masked tragedy

Robert Cushman on Peter Hall's production of The Oresteia

The National Theatre is advertising The Oresteia (Olivier) as the world's earliest dramatic masterpiece. Appropriately, performances begin at five in the afternoon.

Even before that, the first actor is in place, peering at us through a chink high in the set. He seems to have a beak, but of course, like everyone else in the production, he is merely wearing a mask. When we embark on Agamemnon, the first play of Aeschylus's trilogy, he proves to be the Watchman sent by Clytemnestra to look out for her husband's return from Troy. He has a North-country accent, and is amusingly disgruntled. There is no patronising or desperate attempt to prove that Aeschylus can be fun; but we feel, for once, let in to a Greek tragedy. There are telling, and agreeable, see-saw rhythms:

Wish it were over, this waiting, this watching,
twelve weary months, night in and night out.

Score one up to Tony Harrison's translation.

The good impression persists during the long first chorus, the tale of Troy and the fatal sacrifice of Iphigenia delivered here by a dozen elders. (The choruses of the subsequent two plays are the same size). Voices are still mainly northern, and the alliteration now reveals a sharp dramatic edge: 'Batter, batter the doom-drum, but believe there'll be better' certainly makes more sense and better sound than 'sing sorrow, sorrow but good win out in the end' in the approved modern version, Richmond Lattimore's.

There are danger-signs in this chorus, though, and they intensify when Clytemnestra comes on. I wouldn't blame the actor for this, even were I allowed to tell you who he is. (Sixteen players in this all-male cast divide choral and individual speaking roles between them, with no specific credits provided. This apparently makes the actors feel good. It also furnishes critics and other regulars with a harmless guessing-game). Both watchman and chorus have displayed a taste for hard-edged and newly-minted compound nouns. The chorus now fling a few of them at their queen.

It's right that we honour the wife of our clanchief
when the manlord himself's
not here on the thronestone

A feud in Mr Harrison's diction is a 'bloodgrudge,' a divine command a 'godgoad,' a human destiny a 'lifelot,' and brace yourselves, death by drowning will land you in a 'wavegrave.' That last cuteness, though, is atypical. Once again Mr Harrison has done a virtuoso translation job: lucid, resourceful, a pleasure to read. Only it doesn't work; or, if you prefer, it doesn't play.

It hems the drama in, hammers it to death with monosyllables. (Even in combination, that's how they strike the ear.) It exudes a gnarled and forbidding primitivism which excludes the audience. Peter Hall's production reminds us that heroic Argos (or indeed Aeschylean Athens) was by our standards less a thriving city-state than a small tribal encampment. I half-liked Jocelyn Herbert's set: that is I approved of the wall done in strips of reinforced tinfoil, but not of the steps approaching it, which look as if they are filling in until the real ones arrive. I expected, I suppose, something more spectacular; the Olivier, we are told, derives from Epidaurus, where the views are magnificent. With grandeur gone, homeliness might at least involve us with the characters, but the masks prevent that. Mr Harrison's literary reference-point is Anglo-Saxon, but his six-foot line, however syncopated, reminded me of something more recent, encountered on this very stage: the verses of Mendoza, alias the Devil, in Man and Superman:

O wert thou, Louisa, the wife of Mendoza:
Mendoza's Louisa, Louisa Mendoza

They sent Jack Tanner to sleep.

The Oresteia can be a lulling experience, too, stretching a single moment for pages, then jack-knifing major events and heartlessly banishing vital characters who have served their immediate purpose. Something has to anchor us to it: if not aural or visual splendour, then psychological realism. (All drama aspires towards realism unless it has already got there, in which case it needs to be nudged back towards ritual.) I tried hard to feel moved or horrified when wife killed husband or son mother, but nothing came. There was some quickening in the second play Choephori, when Orestes and Electra were reunited and plotted murder, but not enough. The production had created no framework to which I could relate these events. Some vexing problems of choral speaking had been intelligently tackled, but only by the extreme (and self-defeating) method of virtually banishing unison delivery.

Then a surprise. The last play, The Eumenides, had been in my experience the most boring. (Oh yes, pace last week's Sunday Times, the plays have been done before in London in the past 2,000 years. In 1961 at the Old Vic I saw something that certainly said it was The Oresteia and which I have no reason to think was lying.) The case of Orestes's matricide is referred to the first-ever Athenian jury, who find for him and against the avenging Furies, thereby founding Athenian justice and Athenian Democracy.

As the actors drop their pebbles into the urns (or votepots), they seem for almost the first time to be playing real actions. We are caught up, and at the end respond gladly to the gentle command, 'stand while the Kind Ones pass,' as the now beneficent Furies exit through the auditorium. It is a stirring moment; but I cannot say that it truly places the drama in the audience's laps or that it gathers up all that has gone before. Many of the production's techniques, though, pay off here. Harrison Birtwistle's music crowns the most helpful and sophisticated score ever composed for the tongs and the bones. The masks have been good throughout, especially the pop-eyed ones for the foreigners, Aegisthus and Cassandra (whose caged entrance is an event), and the white creation, oriental and feminine, for Orestes (an unusually graceful performance, incidentally). Those for the Furies make very convincing emblems of female malevolence as, crowning the evening's drag-show aspect, do the actors behind them.

Does that brand me as a he-critic? Much as I longed throughout for an actor to enter barefaced, or to say 'king' instead of 'clanchief,' I yearned more to hear 'god' or 'goddess' instead of the 'he-god' and 'she-god' imprinted on our skulls to keep us aware of the play's roots in a patriarchal society; hence, though I can't follow the argument, the all-male cast. Certainly there was a gulp in the house when Athena pronounced the father the important parent, the mother a mere seed-bed; certainly Clytemnestra resents the unfaithful husband who also happens to have killed her daughter. But these things do not seem to have been felt by Aeschylus as issues, and Hall is too scrupulous a director to force them. So they merely, and awkwardly, hang around. The production's great failure is that in five hours it seldom lets us know why we are there.

What other attractions lie here in my playtray? Steafel Solo (at the King's Head through Christmas) is a richly-written and performed one-woman show; Sheila Steafel even succeeds in satirising other comics while being funny herself. A production of The Dresser, at least as good as the London one, has been going the regional rounds, coming this week in Nottingham; Joss Ackland is a touching Sir and Kenneth Haigh an unlikely but inspired choice for his acolyte. At the Beck Theatre, Hayes (this week in Croydon), I saw Mike Harding's Fur Coat and No Knickers, but forbear to speak of it. Stand, while this Kind One passes.
