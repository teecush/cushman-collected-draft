Panache from Poland

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'official' and fringe theatre.

THE Polish painter-director Tadeusz Kantor has been back in Edinburgh, this time at the Moray House Gymnasium.

The approach to the audience is appropriately strenuous. There are no intervals, and a notice in the foyer proclaims that no late-comers will be admitted. This might be admirable if it were being done for the sake of the spectators. Actually the people being considered are the performers, so the edict counts as self-indulgence. More specifically it is an attempt to intimidate us into feeling privileged to be there.

Kantor's Wielopole Wielopole does in fact come off as something special. It is presented to us an evocation of the director's own childhood; and he himself is on stage throughout--the very picture of the Central European genius in shiny black suit and open-neck white shirt--to keep his memories in order. Mostly they revolve around the army and the church. Kantor's uncle is recalled as the priest of the village that gives the play its title, his father as one of a group of conscripts who for a long time merely stand and crouch ominously in a corner. They might be papier-mache: when they spring into life they are deeply alarming--and remain so for the rest of the action.

The priest seems to be blessing and encouraging the soldiers as they go to fight. When, later on, they turn on him and--all seven of them--bayonet him, this is something of a judgment and rather more of a moral and political truth. (They that live by the sword, etc.) We see uncle and father marching happily side by side, one with his weapons, the other with his Cross. The Cross, which is huge, later becomes a more oppressive burden. The priest staggers under it, like Christ en route to Calvary. But round and round with it he goes and it comes to dominate the play.

Crucifixion is certainly an apt enough image for twentieth-century Poland. All the same, the Catholic iconography had me confused, for I had always imagined Kantor to be Jewish: and confusion was compounded when one of the players (Kantor's wife in fact) appeared as a rabbi.

Had I understood the dialogue (one forgets that Kantor uses words, but he does), I might have been able to fit the pieces together. As it is I have to report a spectacle of undoubted panache and authority, but repetitive. The same pictures keep revolving through the evening at increasing speed. I don't know whether this is intended to illustrate the frenzied workings of memory or simply to pulverise the audience. Very loud music suggests the latter.

Of course if you really want to direct your actors while they are actually at it, you become a puppeteer. At the National Arts Centre in Ottawa they apparently specialise in marionette versions of nineteenth-century classics. The obvious candidate would be A Doll's House, but they have brought us Buchner's Woyzeck and Strindberg's A Dream Play.

The treatment of Woyzeck is elaborately structured to emphasise the elements of social and military exploitation. Soldiers enter and are barked at in German. When they have been thoroughly cowed, they are issued with puppets whom they have to control. Sitting above are a quartet of nobs (3m, 1f) who control them, announcing the scenes and reading the dialogue. Meanwhile the puppets oppress one another, with Woyzeck himself, a piteous sight with his bald lantern head and skeletal features, at the bottom of the heap.

Only the doll playing Marie seems miscast, making her a Hollywood vamp, and the execution is immaculate: the hero being a barber, there is plenty of opportunity for fancy handwork. But a drama which deals with the hounding of a man till he becomes a thing loses some of its impetus if he is a thing to start with.

In Le Songe (the Strindberg is done in French) all is sedate and beautifully modulated, and the piece loses its only interest--its phantasmagoria of changing scenes. It is also slow. But dreams are fast: ask Mr Kantor. The text is read by a series of actors, each in turn imitating the god Indra, whose daughter, on the main stage below, descends to earth to find that man is unhappy and has to do the washing-up. That, mercilessly reiterated, is Strindberg's message: one that can still find takers. Le Songe is ended, but la maladie lingers on.

After all this I looked forward to the flesh, bone and overflow of Henry IV, Parts 1 and 2 (Daniel Stewart's and Melville College). This RSC touring production, directed by Bill Alexander, captures a good measure of them. Alfred Marks's Falstaff gains in assurance all the way. The more complicated verbal architecture defeats him, but he is a wizard with one-liners, with impatient eye-rollings, with audience contact, and with comic situations: the ensemble scenes in Eastcheap build firmly outwards from him.

He has two striking female team-mates, though their styles hardly blend. Barbara Jefford's Quickly is an elaborate, textually observant assemblage of optimistically genteel vowels: Patti Love's Doll is jaggedly at odds with the rhythm, but her first drunken entrance is magnificent. Among the straights, Stuart Wilson's Hotspur (apart from looking and sounding like Richard III) has the same kind of problems as Mr Marks: Hotspur can hardly stop talking, and it sometimes seems that Mr Wilson can hardly start. But he develops great charm, especially in the scenes with Juliet Stevenson as his wife (this must have been how the marriage in The Taming of the Shrew turned out), and the baiting of Glendower can scarcely have been better done.

Mr Alexander seems, for an RSC cadet, uncommonly alive to comic nuance, and he lets the plays' two worlds (epitomised in a set composed of banners and barrels) unobtrusively illuminate each other. The thieves "make the commonwealth their boots" and so do the rebels, stomping all over a map of England. The Welsh foxiness of Bernard Lloyd's Henry recalls Emrys James's five years ago, but is less showy and allows the king nobility as well: David Rintoul's Hal is resonant but hollow. In general, company experience tells: as in John Burgess's politic Northumberland doubled with a properly actorish Pistol, and in David Shaw-Parker's string of roles, culminating in a Justice Silence who looks, in his modified balaclava, like a pantomime mole and blinks like one too.

One-man shows better be funny. Three on the fringe are: (1) Three Men in a Boat (at Buster Brown's). Jeremy Nicholas beautifully times the infallible jokes and also manages to place and accommodate the passages of fatuous moralising without patronising them. (2) The Third Nam (Heriot-Watt Theatre). In this play, credited to Bill Stair, Tony Robinson is a prodigy of naturalness as a company manager left to carry the show alone, and also conveys numerous misgivings about the spare-part section of humanity that he embodies. (3) Here's a Funny Thing (Viewforth Centre). John Bardon impersonates Max Miller to, apart from a slight lack of expansiveness, the life. The patter works and Mr Bardon stills the house with unmarked poignancy in the biographical section.

The Traverse, with money scarce, offers fewer plays than formerly and, as usual, quality slips with quantity. John Anderson's Snapshots is Forties-Fifties Americana, growing-up-in-New-York division, the hero too insubstantial to give it point. Song and dance erupt too late. There are some entertaining scenes and matching performances from Tucker McGuire and Ramsay Williams as crabbed age, Robin Pappas as youth, Janene Possell somewhere in between and Lou Hirsch all over.

Wielopole Wielopole can be seen at the Riverside Studios, Hammersmith, from 3-14 September.
