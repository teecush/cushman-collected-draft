Under false pretences

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a new play at the Royal Court

LONDON is suddenly full of impersonators pretending to be something else.

I'll try that again. Obviously all impersonators pretend to be something, or somebody, else. These are pretending that they are not pretending to be somebody else; or at least that they are not pretending to be the somebody else they seem to be pretending to be. Robyn Archer, in A Star is Torn (which is about to move into the West End), reproduces the vocal styles of Billie Holiday and Judy Garland, but says she isn't an impressionist. She could have fooled me. (And she does. Only she doesn't want to. Apparently. Is this clear?)

Now, at the Royal Court, there is a play called Insignificance by Terry Johnson. The scene is a New York hotel-room. I would like you to meet the cast.

First on is Ian McDiarmid as the Professor: small, impish, gentle, with pattering feet and a white moustache. He turns out to have been a refugee from Nazi Germany and to have discovered that e=mc2. If he doesn't look like Einstein, then he looks like the Einstein playing-card from Cluedo. To him enter William Hootkins as a Senator, fat, redneck, Southern, an emissary from HUAC. No dead ringer he, but since every word he utters reeks of McCarthyism, audiences can hardly be blamed for making the identification.

The one lady in the cast is Judy Davis, star of My Brilliant Career, playing another star who is blonde and very beautiful, has a soft dumb-sounding voice but reads Dostoyevsky, and is making a film in which the wind from a man-hole blows her skirt about her legs. (The Seven Year Itch was released in 1955. Mr Johnson says his play takes place in 1953. Maybe he's trying to hang loose. And then Marilyn Monroe was a notoriously unreliable performer whose movies probably took years to shoot.)

Finally there is Larry Lamb, playing a baseball star who claims that his picture has figured in 13 series of bubblegum cards. Since I didn't collect any of them, I have to rely on his being married to Marilyn to know that he is Joe DiMaggio.

Plot. Joe McC summons Albert to a committee hearing, promising roughness if Albert does not co-operate. Albert refuses. Marilyn, tired from a long night's skirt-blowing, takes refuge in Albert's room, and proves to him with the aid of flashlights and toy-trains that she understands the Specific (not the General) Theory of Relativity. She then invites Albert to bed with her. But Joe diM breaks in, angry, as he apparently usually is. Theirs is not a happy marriage, what with his boorishness and her instability. Next morning Joe McC returns and blackmails Albert into handing over his current researches on the shape of Space-Time.

That's all really. You will guess that, apart from an astral touch of the Snoo Wilsons, the play resembles two Tom Stoppards: Jumpers (the professor and the actress) and Travesties (comedy of famous persons). Mr Johnson says, "There are no impersonations in this play. The life of each character is based on a handful of facts . . . those facts are the only resemblance." Come, come now. Actually Mr Stoppard's frankly-named Joyce, Lenin and Tzara are more original creations than Mr Johnson's Professor, Senator, Actress and Ballplayer. Of course the story is fanciful, but its interest derives from the fame or notoriety of the people jumping through its hoops.

So I thought when reading it, after seeing it. But seeing it I had thought different. In performance the people do, by the end, cut loose from their prototypes. Les Waters's sharp production helps and the acting is excellent. Mr McDiarmid, no longer vulpine, has never been better and Miss Davis is very tasty. Besides, there is a theme. The Senator learns a new word every day; in Act One it's "solifluxion" (look it up); in Act Two "solipsism," the belief that only the believer exists. Only the Senator subscribes to this, but they all carry on as if they do, right down to the end, where Albert has a vision--approximately--of the end of the world, while Marilyn keeps right on learning her lines.

This happens to be Mr Stoppard's central theme (that's what I believe, though nobody, including Mr Stoppard, seems to agree with me), but I doubt if he would have been content with only two keywords; he would have used, and illustrated, dozens. Mr Johnson's wit is neither as sharp, as fecund, as integral nor as funny dammit, but it will serve.

For a play concerning disarmament (also incidentally the price of fame, but that's no big deal) Insignificance is appropriately disarming. All its characters are allowed a case, and three of them come up likeable; that may not be the sole true end of dramaturgy, but charm isn't exactly rampaging through our theatres at the moment, is it? Mr Johnson is very promising, and if we survive to the end of the year I shall probably vote for him.

I probably shan't vote for Alan Symons, whose first play, Maybe This Time (New End), is a real old-fashioned terrible: a cosy confection set in a marriage-bureau run by two youngish ladies, one divorced, the other with a husband who disapproves of her work and says so, at length and in bed. (Comedy of this order should never have been taught the facts of life; it was far happier as a virgin.) "You promise the world and you don't even deliver Iceland" is about as far as the wit and the indignation go, en route to a tantalising mention of "our friendly neighbourhood bank manager, dear Donald Ingram," which is no way to conduct a conversation or a play; I mean, if Donald is so fascinating, let's meet him. (And ask him to bring Marcia Taylor.)

Mr Symons also fails to write convincingly one end of a telephone conversation but then whoever has. Proof of the unfairness of life is that his play is better acted--at least by four-fifths of its cast--than the presumably more deserving works of Racine, Kleist and Ostrovsky available elsewhere. Judy Carne, the English girl who had everything socked to her on the Rowan and Martin Laugh-In, is very perky.

I recently attended the first international festival of Jewish theatre, held in Tel Aviv. I and an Israeli critic spoke on the Anglo-Jewish theatrical experience, undaunted by the fact that no such thing exists. Other seminars were concerned with ritual and tradition; there was little consideration of the work of current Israeli playwrights. They apparently don't have to be Jewish; at least, unlike those in the Diaspora, they are not expected to prove it.

International representation was patchy, partly in protest at the Lebanon war, though since the festival was organised by dissidents (headed by Danny Horovitz, one of the aforementioned playwrights) it seemed an odd target. Britain mainly sent bad fringe, though I was glad to catch up with Seamus Finnegan's James Joyce and the Israelites, a garrulous but enjoyable talk-piece (with Joyce as raisonneur getting rather lost) about the Jews and Irish as lost tribes.

Five years ago I nominated Susan Littler as best new actress for her stolidly distraught young wife in Bedroom Farce. She was a marvellously clear and versatile player, and her death is heartbreaking.
