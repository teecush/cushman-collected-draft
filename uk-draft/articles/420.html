Half-cock Heartbreak

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Shaw at the Haymarket.

IT would be nice if the classical programme at the Haymarket had managed to include one play that had not previously been done by the National Theatre. The management might have done themselves a service as well as us; unfortunate comparisons were bound to be made, never more than in the new production of Heartbreak House, which, with Captain Shotover at its helm and his nautical house as its setting, seems fair to describe as the flagship of the season.

John Schlesinger directed this play, to great admiration, in the National's last days at the Old Vic; and I feel, after all this time, like apologising to him. I stated then that, given a good enough cast (and this play always attracts stars), no director could go wrong with Heartbreak House; the luminaries, all having their moments, would automatically arrange themselves into an ensemble governed by the English sense of fair play, and Shaw's text, self-orchestrating, would do the rest. John Dexter's production is here to confute me. It is cast to the nines and it is no disaster, but it falls continually short.

His actors rarely connect with one another, and the play disperses into a series of random conversations, which themselves sometimes become collections of random remarks. I now see that for the whole thing to come together, and to make patterns in the mind, some kind of confidence trick has to be worked.

Shaw gives the hint by insisting on a thick and rambling setting, and by beginning romantic comedy; Jocelyn Herbert's ascetic design kills its atmosphere almost at first glance, besides denying the actors effective entrances. It improves in the second act when curtains are drawn, and even more in the third when the action moves outside, but by then the damage has been done.

It is compounded by Rex Harrison's performance as Shotover. He has some perfectly timed deflationary throwaways, and he rises to the prophetic moments, but in the middle ground of the role he offers only mildly eccentric geniality. There is no iron in him; and we disbelieve not only the captain's seafaring past, but his despairing present. Shotover talks of "the horror of an old man drinking," but we feel no horror here. It should represent Shaw's terror of his own decay; if that is absent, the play becomes very complacent.

Of course it is an attack on the governing and intellectual classes of the First World War, but that assault is less comprehensive than is often supposed, and unless the author is personally involved, it is of little interest. Anyone can satirise other people.

On the first night Mr Harrison was uncertain of his lines (he destroyed Shotover's first good joke and his last great cry) and slow on his cues; and the second fault was even more alarming than the first. It defused the tension of the air-raid, otherwise the best of the production, with Doris Hare's nurse turned to a harpy exulting over the dead.

En route there are other lightweight performances: note Rosemary Harris's Ariadne, professional but subdued, and Frank Middlemass's capitalist Mangan, pleasant enough, but too unassuming to be worth bringing down. Paul Curran, another actor afflicted with uncertainty, nevertheless brings great quizzical force to the gentle Mazzini; Simon Ward is a public schoolboy spoiled for life as Randall the rotter; Mel Martin, though too old for the virginal role, goes a fine disillusioned progress as Ellie.

The flamboyant Hector is done by Paxton Whitehead, an actor to whom extravagance comes natural; only his Act Two invocation, "fall and cease," is underdone. On which note, it is ingenious to have the personnel singing the blues for the incantation that ends the previous act, but the effect is to make the words sound as much like doggerel as they look.

There remains, thankfully, Diana Rigg's Hesione, which combines gypsyish beauty, moon-faced comic attack and an honest, unembarrassed audience rapport. Miss Rigg is a rarity - an apparently unselfish personality performer. But Shaw needs - and used almost automatically to get - star performances thoroughly fused. The recent National Major Barbara gave us the fusion without the stars; this offers the reverse. Neither is enough.

La Fianza Satisfecha is the play, conjecturally by Lope de Vega, which John Osborne once adapted as A Bond Honoured. As The Outrageous Saint, it now arrives at the Grove, Hammersmith, unadapted but not quite unashamed. In some ways the Stage Fright company's approach is more perverse than Mr Osborne's. He found a play he could not stomach and rewrote it, chiefly by denying repentance to the central character.

This is Leonido, a Sicilian blade who rapes his sister, blinds his father and is probably not above ringing the church bell when everybody is trying to sleep. Like Satan, Cain, Don Juan and Faust (there is, incidentally, a play called Don Juan and Faust by the German romantic Grabbe, and I want to see it), he gets a divine comeuppance, and ends crucified and repentant.

Mr Osborne kept the crucifixion, but not the development, which was odd; Gub Neal's production keeps both, but signals its disbelief in them, which is odder, especially since the play plainly has the courage of its convictions and communicates them powerfully. I see the problem, but I cannot officially approve of the solution, nor in any capacity of the musical interludes, even if it is fun to hear a Moorish maiden sing country-and-western.

However, staging and performance are far above the level we addicts usually endure when seeking neglected texts in neglected venues. John Lyne Pirkis acts Leonido as a baffled biker; the others, including several women playing men and playing them well, portray his opponents as deserving everything they get. His conventionally conventional servant is changed to a sceptical harlequin (or columbine), but what else would you expect? Some day - not necessarily tomorrow, since there are other Spanish plays to be attended to - I want to see this work done straight.

The Soho Poly last week harboured two plays advertised as about radio, and full of static. The first, Nature of Communication, was sketchy; the second, World Service, protracted. They're off now anyway.
