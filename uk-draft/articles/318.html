Dreaming of Hollywood

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Leicester.

PETER PRINCE'S Television Times (Warehouse) are hard times for all concerned. The malaise of TV drama is defined as the wish of all concerned that they were making movies instead.

Accordingly we watch a long-established team -- author, director, producer, actor and make-up girl -- at work on the series they hope, wanly, will prove at least to be the prelude to Hollywood: a weekly canter through English history, the kind of project in which, as someone says, the characters inevitably end up telling each other that this place must be Runnymede.

The playwright is the nearest thing on hand to a protagonist, going through marital and professional crises simultaneously. He is given a nervily attractive performance by Karl Johnson, but he is moved into the centre of the piece too late, and out of it too early. Mr Prince seems to be creating problems for him as a means of keeping the show going. Interest is more firmly held by group-portraiture: Richard O'Callaghan as the producer and Ron Cook as the director are a pair of fey, insecure opportunists much given to elaborate parades of petulant despair.

An opening sequence of general paranoia in the control-room is fun; so is a closing lampoon of the BAFTA awards. What goes between is pettiness that Mr Prince observes well enough, but about which he seems neither especially angry nor especially amused. Neither, in consequence, do we. If the aim was to present one medium to another, the result I think will be to increase mutual mistrust.

Bernard Kops's More Out Than In (Bush) is another play that presents a milieu, and tells us in effect to get on with it. Mr Kops's characters are mental patients, out of hospital but still leading a terrified institutionalised life in a Margate boarding-house. The pity becomes a parade of fixed smiles, and it takes a tough playwright -- a Chekhov or Ben Jonson -- to make that formula work. Mr Kops is far too kind to deal harshly with the deranged; he farms that duty out to his Dickensian landlady.

The production ladles out additional syrup, and the pierhead set usurps space without evoking it. Bill Stewart, with gap teeth and questing eyes, does as much for the principal sufferer as any actor could, but there is some narrow, monotonous acting going on around him.

Gigi (Haymarket, Leicester) is the stage version of the film musical of the dramatisation of Colette's novel. The first two stages are perhaps beyond recall, but the transition from film to theatre is always threatening. Stage performances, however good, can be replaced; but we know that Leslie Caron, Louis Jourdan, Hermione Gingold, Maurice Chevalier are still there on celluloid, and almost impossible to dislodge. Only one scene here imposes itself; Betty Marsden and Hugh Paddick sing I Remember It Well (as, with all their radio years together, they presumably do) with leisured, affectionate grace.

Everywhere else the production hurries, as if trying to catch some unattainable bus. There is style in Terry Parsons's settings but nowhere else; and on stage this American tourist's view of Paris -- so elegant, so vivacious, so naughty -- is cloying. (The Belle Epoque that Feydeau caricatured had the right idea.) The film got by uplifting views of fountains and statues; having David Firth walk round a post is no real substitute. There is one interesting addition to the score: a song of surrender for Gigi (Janet Shaw), the reluctant courtesan, that may be the most adventurous number Alan Jay Lerner and Frederick Loewe have written.

On the bank of London's river
Stands a theatre built of concrete,
Built of louring, greying concrete;
Where, to greet the festive season,
Actors who appeared but lately
Dressed as Celts, undressed as Druids
Now have changed their pigmentation,
Now assumed another warpaint.
(Body makeup by Max Factor?
Hard to tell; there is no credit.
Could this be the Hidden Factor?)
On they run in crouching posture,
Bending double, at the double;
(Which is quite a trick to master,
Guaranteed to put your back out);
Tell the tale of Hiawatha,
From the pen of Henry Wadsworth,
He the Fellow, he the Long One;
Though no longer than his poem,
Written in trochaic metre
With no rhyme but lots of rhythm,
As is known to every schoolboy
(Though it seems -- I blush to say it --
Not to every theatre critic);
And no longer than this sentence.
(Can you hear me, Bernard Levin?)
Three the stages in this complex,
In this highly complex complex;
And the play of Hiawatha,
Every afternoon and morning,
Currently adorns the largest:
The Olivier they named it,
Named it for our greatest actor,
He the baron, late of Brighton,
He the Moor and Jew of Venice,
Star of stage and magic lantern
Where he plays sadistic dentist
Or -- I cannot wait to see it --
Ailing father of Al Jolson
In remake of early talkie.
Very big and grand this actor;
Very big and grand his theatre;
And -- despite a smallish cast-list
And, I'd guess, a smallish cost-list --
This production big and grand too.
The adaptor and director --
First name Michael, last Bogdanov --
Specialises in such ventures:
Mystic poems, active poems,
Told for children but not childish.
Some reviewers brought their offspring;
Some brought other people's offspring.
Your reporter, Scrooge incarnate,
Sat without a young companion;
Noted, surly but approving,
How impeccable the silence,
How they watched and did not chatter,
How they listened without coughing.
Plot was not the main attraction;
For the tale was episodic,
Leaving you to muse and wonder
Why his tribe loved Hiawatha,
Just what made him so important.
Spectacle it was that held them,
From the farandole of kerchiefs --
Blue and green and white and yellow --
Through the fingers of the West Wind
(He was Hiawatha's papa)
To the figure of the white prophet,
Posed against a fiery circle,
Gun in hand and set to use it.
(There's a thought to send you home with.)
So to Marty Flood, designer.
Commendations for the teepee
That enveloped all the action,
Gave a focus and a meaning.
Hail the Great Bear, Mishe Mokwa,
Black and huge and slain in combat
(Though if we were meant to triumph
In the death of one so furry,
They don't know the British public:)
There was joking, there was dancing,
And a pretty Minnehaha,
She the interest romantic;
Plus the odd interpolation --
Nothing though to vex the spirit.
'Hiya, Minnie.' 'Hiya, Watha.'
This variety of crassness
Was conspicuous by absence.
I'm surprised you raised the question.
Let me end with something joyful.
Merry Christmas. No, that's next week.
Hang this up outside the theatre:
I enjoyed it. Honest Injun.
