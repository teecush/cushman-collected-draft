The Glisten of Humour

ROBERT CUSHMAN writes an appreciation of American songwriter and singer Johnny Mercer, who died on Friday, aged 66.

I FIRST became conscious of Johnny Mercer as a singer: a man who did duets with Bing Crosby.

When I started listening to the radio in the late forties a record which they must have cut a decade earlier was still current (it hasn't died yet). It was a reworking of the old vaudeville cross-talk number "Mr Gallagher and Mrs Shean," with the lyrics altered to a dictionary of the jargon of swing. Mercer did the rewriting of course and there are many of his peculiar qualities captured on that disc. The rhythm, the fascination with jazz and with language, the poker-faced wit. Harold Arlen, the composer with whom much of his best work was done, said that he only once saw Mercer crack a smile.

Both as writer and performer, his work glistens with humour, but it is so slyly delivered in a tart voice that it can almost slip by the listener. Singing with Crosby, and later with Bobby Darin, he loved to assume the role of a slightly bemused peasant, counterpointing the hip behaviour of the professional singer he was teamed with. In fact, he could outswing them all. Recently he made a couple of albums of his own songs, and one of the saddest things about his death is that there are so many hundreds more of which he had still to make the definitive recording.

He wrote lyrics with everybody: with Kern, Carmichael, Ellington, Schwartz down to Henry Mancini and André Previn, with whom he collaborated on the recent musical of Priestley's "The Good Companions." That was not his best work; he could not adapt to an English idiom, or indeed to a theatrical one.

He wrote individual songs, not revelations of situation or character: the greatest he ever created -- Jubilation T Cornpone -- was the subject of the song, not its singer. And of course he was an American subject: Mercer was the lyricist of Americana, in two modes rural-bucolic (as in the score for "Seven Brides for Seven Brothers," or his host of ornithological songs -- "Skylark" and the others) and urban-hip. "One for my Baby" is the archetypal saloon ballad, suggesting the smoke of cigarettes as much as of lovers' torches. And it is less a moaning than a shrugging song; like all the best lyricists, he knew how to be wry about heartbreak.

Others of his songs are filled with a kind of crazy, irreverent euphoria; they sound fun to write and fun to sing. An obscure piece (in fact I have only just discovered it) typically entitled "Hang on to your Lids, Kids," concludes:--

"Ben Franklin once said it all
Divided we've got to fall
United we'll have a ball
Here we go again."

He seems hardly ever to have stopped writing, and he turned out a great many routine numbers. But few lyricists (or composers, for he furnished Fred Astaire with the music as well as the words of the magnificent "Something's Got to Give") can have turned out so many great ones.
