Brook's 'Mesure pour Mesure'

Theatre

by Robert Cushman

TALKING or writing about the theatre over the past few years, Peter Brook has always come back to Measure for Measure. Now at his Paris theatre, the Bouffes-du-Nord, he has come back to it in another, literal sense.

In 1950 he directed a production of this play at Stratford which those who saw it still recall with rapture. In his book The Empty Space Mr Brook writes of 'a base world . . . the disgusting, stinking world of medieval Vienna . . . we must animate all this stretch of the play as the roughest comedy we can make . . . the rough is in prose, the rest in verse . . . if we iron Shakespeare into any one typography of theatre we lose the real meaning of the play.' The italics are mine; I believe that sentence to be of vital importance in the consideration of almost any Shakespeare play.

But it does not altogether apply to the 'Mesure pour Mesure' Mr Brook has staged in Paris, since Jean-Claude Carriere's French adaptation ignores the 'clear signal' (Mr Brook's phrase) that Shakespeare gives when the play is about to jump tracks: it is all in prose, and clipped declarative prose at that. It speeds the action along and Mr Brook's direction moves at a matching clip. No longer a confusion, the play remains a puzzle. There are no glaring contradictions in it--Angelo and Mistress Overdone belong to the same hard, clear world--but there is no overriding coherence either. Everyone is himself, firmly drawn, without apology or explanation.

This even applies to the one character for whom most directors feel compelled to invent a rationale. Shakespeare gave the Duke little personality but it is customary to make him the vehicle of an interpretation, agent of Providence or greasy manipulator. Francois Marthouret, a dazzling actor, sticks to the basics of charm and authority.

Mr Brook may have had in mind a legend of justice--the most powerful tableau of the evening consists of the three judges--Duke, Angelo and Escalus standing side by side--but his production says surprisingly little about it, or about the twin subject of mercy. Isabella pauses long before agreeing to beg for Angelo's life, but her hesitation lacks the explosive effect it apparently had in 1950. There can be no doubt that Clementine Amouroux's crew-cut Isabella will do the decent thing; youthfully idealistic (and, for the role, ideally young), she lacks rigidity or spiritual pride.

The most complex performance is Bruce Myers's Angelo, which moves easily from self-satisfaction to self-hatred by (and this is something of a revelation) a comic route; the clean thrust of the translation puts a hard edge on the revolving paradox of his situation.

But then the whole company is, as ever, commandingly unfussed, notably Maurice Benichou's sleek, chic Lucio and Andreas Katsulas's huge, and hugely ingratiating, Pompey. There is some viciousness in M. Benichou, but M. Katsulas seems harmless. Theirs is not the reeking world of which Mr Brook has written, but then in this neat 'Mesure' his ideal 'Measure' has gone by default. On this occasion he has stretched the mind, not blown it.

The Catalan company La Claca, who have brought their Mori el Merma to the Riverside Studios, are like nothing we have seen since the Bread and Puppet Theatre who were the best group to come out of the American explosion of the sixties. La Claca are as charming and more powerful. They communicate enormous gaiety and they make with it a powerful statement on the nature of dictatorship which widens into an image of any and every power-structure. They are, in every sense, essential theatre.

Which is strange since their method here is very specialised. Actors in this production are the slaves of the designer. This is contrary to most theories of theatrical well-being, though the fact that the designer is Joan Miro might earn them an indulgence even were the work less successful than it is.

Miro has constructed a series of giant grotesque machines; the actors inhabit and propel them. Thus is created for example a sort of Michelin man whose bulk has descended to his buttocks and who sports a blue hawk-head with a red fluffy beak. He is followed on stage by a companion monster, bigger yet, with enormously long black pneumatic arms. Ruling them all is something elephantine: something with a trunk anyway. They are all initially endearing (like the more sinister of the Muppets) and it is an achievement of the show that by the end it has made these floppy funsters seem irredeemably horrible. They are attended and fawned over by grey-suited flunkeys with black ties and false noses, and they tyrannise over a race of beaked sea-green figures, unprepossessing but inoffensive.

A sequence in which one of these is dismembered while the monarch's consort, hugely girlish, disports herself with a skipping-rope recalls the torture-scene in Edward Bond's Lear, and is far less questionable. Nor is it easy to forget the sight of the lady rocking her boring spouse to sleep. Those children's books about Babar, king of the jungle, will never seem so innocent again. The actors occasionally break out from behind their masks; one, for example, is allowed a final gesture of contempt for the old regime which is understandable but artistically regrettable; the point was already made.

The Comings and Goings of Mike Stott's new play at Hampstead are part of a halting mating-dance. Most of the characters are concerned to leave their partners, and the process turns out to be easy but unprofitable; something, or someone equally unsatisfactory will usually transpire instead. Either way it doesn't matter much. The arbitrariness of the pattern infects the play itself, at least where plotting is concerned; the action takes place in a single flat to which a surprising number of people have access. Mr Stott is meticulous in telling us what their jobs are--they tend geriatrics, sell biscuits, plan cities, research Norse myths--and in no case does it matter; he places us all, whatever our social or sexual orientation, in the same boat.

The play's energy resides in its language, which is full of fine twisting flourishes. John Normington is rather too restrained an actor to take full advantage of his choric role as the Viking expert, but Lindsay Duncan as a hearty county girl trying earnestly to untie her tongue and describe her first orgasm is tremendously funny (and no caricature incidentally), and so is Paul Jesson as her awakener who could not be more surprised.

Snoo Wilson's A Greenish Man (Bush) is a goodish play; like Mr Stott's piece it is both concerned with and afflicted by half-heartedness. Mr Wilson's is the more considerable talent; so his play is the more frustrating. It concerns various would-be adherents of the IRA domiciled in Kilburn, including one who is plainly an overgrown leprechaun. The only untainted character is a little girl; she is called Deirdre and dances on tables, and I think she represents Ireland. The play keeps promising to become really enjoyable; the production, which always seems a beat behind, doesn't help much.
