Moral gymnastics

THEATRE

Robert Cushman on a Stoppard revival and a new play by Storey

FOUR years after leaping into prominence at the Old Vic, Tom Stoppard's Jumpers has arrived at the Lyttelton. Jumpers is the play about the professor of moral philosophy, possessed of a hare called Thumper, a tortoise called Pat and a wife called Dotty; he accidentally impales the first, treads on the second and, horror-struck, calls out for help from the third.

She has already spent much of the play making a similar request of him, and for a not totally dissimilar reason. She too is in receipt of a corpse and she has a suspicion that she may, at a party the previous evening, have murdered a logician and a gymnast, they being the same person. How are we doing?

The moral philosopher, a George Moore (as opposed to the George Moore), has ignored his wife's cries in the interest of what he considers a greater good, that good being goodness itself. He is preparing a debating speech to prove the existence of God, under two heads: as creator and as moral source. The first gives him comparatively little trouble (his argument seems watertight, though no more or less so than A. J. Ayer's rebuttal of it printed in the programme), but the second causes him real frustration.

He can dispose of semantic obstacles, such as the confusing use of the word 'good' in both ethical and aesthetic judgments, but this is just cape-work: immensely adroit and amusing cape-work, Mr Stoppard being the writer he is and George the beneficiary of the fact. But to bring off the kill, George has, in strictly moral terms, to answer his own question, 'what is so good about good?' and he can never quite find the right words, though he convinces us that they must somewhere exist. He does, however, find the right cry, even if it costs him a hare and a tortoise.

Michael Hordern, repeating his original and definitive performance, finds the passion as well as the oddity in George; his attempts to articulate his faith are rather moving. Elsewhere he prepares his speech in a series of beaming spurts, taking brief self-hugging trips around the stage whenever inspiration strikes, rounding off his sentences with a triumphant nasal swoop--vocal italics--whenever he wishes, by pointing a contemporary metaphor to rout the forces of trendiness on their own ground.

Leading these forces is Archie, vice-chancellor of George's university, for whom attempts to arrive at the roots of morality have little significance. Incidentally a man of means, he is primarily a man of ends, and ruthless in attaining them. His interests are wide; to please him, philosophers are athletes.

George, of course, is out of step on both counts. George is a duffer, Archie is omnipotent, and the play foresees a government of Archies. (Though the spirit of the work is joyous and funny, its import is black.) Mr Stoppard seems happier, though, with failure than success; I have seen two good actors play Archie (Graham Crowden last time, Julian Glover this) and they both came out wooden.

Between the two men is Dotty, an ex-musical comedy star who broke down when man reached the moon, thereby outdating most of her repertoire and other human values besides. This conceit has always seemed the least plausible thing in the play; the kind of violence and selfishness adduced long antedate space travel and Dotty's collapse seems arbitrary.

Julie Covington gets more conviction into it than I had hoped; she is disturbed where Diana Rigg was dizzy, with no loss of wit and with a great gain in singing. (Dotty's kind of star, by the way, has hardly existed in Britain since the 1940s.) One sensitive piece of rewriting brings her a fraction nearer to George (wishing him good luck with his speech) than before; the play seems more hopeful for the addition.

There are many other convolutions. If the form of the play is eccentric--a stretched monologue balancing a compressed plot--it is still effective, and the jokes, which bind the structure, are irresistible. The author, having discovered philosophy himself, makes us an exuberant present of it and the pleasure is contagious.

Peter Wood's production has expanded, literally and metaphorically, to fill the Lyttelton stage to the best advantage yet; the final scene incorporates a song and dance around Miss Covington which looks like a parody of the closing number in A Chorus Line--or perhaps like the object of which that number is itself a parody. The tune is 'Sentimental Journey.' Mr Stoppard stands out among contemporary playwrights for his generosity and acumen in giving us something to hum on our way home.

Two other comedies this week incorporate familiar songs in the gaps between scenes. In David Storey's Mother's Day (Royal Court) they are meant for whimsical comments on the action, though the connection becomes increasingly tangential as the evening proceeds, a symptom of growing desperation all round. The typical menage of Mr Storey's plays--embittered mothers, embattled fathers, and children in all varieties of peculiarity--have long hovered on the brink of farce, and it was sensible enough to push them over.

Now, too, Mr Storey can gaily expend all the narrative resources he has been withholding over the years; the play is never actually boring. But it is very effortful; as if the author had heard about farce and had set out to do right by it, but lacked the spirit. He employs Ortonesque shock tactics but turns them into running gags; and the two devices defeat each other.

Mother has bred a daughter whom she always addresses as 'liar' and keeps in a cupboard, and a son who elaborately rapes every woman he meets, and two other siblings who marry each other. Greed is heavily featured and there is probably an implicit moral about all families being like this. Betty Marsden manages one brilliant double-take, Jane Carr does her usual plump and proper schoolgirl dissolving in pleasure, Alun Armstrong has a dainty lour, and David Rydall (once of Jumpers) adds a touch of smouldering class. There is some awkwardness in Robert Kidd's production, but nothing really inexcusable.

The holes in Same Time Next Year (Prince of Wales) are filled by music typical of the period 1951-76, over which span a couple spend annual adulterous weekends. We witness six and see a strong if specialised kind of love developing.

The first half I found coy and irritating, stuffed with wisecracks that belonged not to the characters but to the author, Bernard Slade, and more particularly to his Broadway audience. Thereafter he is less indulgent, more observant; the fads and causes of the last decade pass before us, and if they are not very deeply considered that is probably true to the part they played in most people's lives.

You do not really have to believe in the relationship; it has been contrived as a theatrical means of keeping tabs on the participants, and in the woman's case at least the evidence mounts interestingly up; a shy starter, she develops in a rush and formidably. The curve is beautifully plotted by Frances Cuka, plumply convincing at every age. Michael Crawford takes on a slim distinction at about 40; but until then he gives a piping, laboured performance. Since they always meet in the same hotel room (which, they are careful to point out, never changes) and since no one else appears, the outgoings on this show must be minimal. The incomings are expected to be colossal.
