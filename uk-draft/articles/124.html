Delicious tripe

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN ranges from the Fosdykes to Strindberg.

The subject of The Fosdyke Saga (Bush) is tripe. That, you may say, hardly distinguishes it from most of the entertainment currently available in London. But tripe here is not a metaphor; it is the thing itself. The Fosdykes, abstracted from Bill Tidy's Daily Mirror cartoon by their creator and Alan Plater, manufacture the stuff and have grown great upon it.

The game is Fosdykes v. the Rest of the World. Roger Sloman, Jane Wood, Malcolm Ranson, Kevin Elyot and Penelope Nice are the Fosdykes. Philip Jackson is the Rest of the World which, being so outnumbered, not unnaturally loses, though Mr Jackson puts up a very good fight. The family resources are impressive; Dad Fosdyke is mean and rowdy, Mum is comfortable and a punch-bag, daughter Vicky is nubile, son Albert, a slimmer version of his old man, unexpectedly turns into an air ace come the First World War, and other son Tom, though he deprecates any kind of violence, does surprisingly well out of his pacific principles.

Recruited into the same war as a non-combatant linguist Tom discovers that the very first German prisoner he is required to interrogate is something very big in sausages. A mutually profitable merger is immediately planned. Thus the Fosdykes become a multi-national corporation and can sing--indeed do sing, and expect us to join in--as follows: "Tripe it is grand/Tripe it is noble/In every land/Tripe it is global."

The Fosdyke Saga is instructive as well as poetic. It shows us, and I quote, "the working-classes-of Europe exploited by the massed forces of capitalism in an ongoing counter-productive war situation." You may find that claim a bit inflated, but then you may also not have sat through the shows that use similar language in all seriousness, and on which the present uproarious entertainment is gleefully bent on avenging itself. Mike Bradwell is the director and the production is a boisterous counterpart to the filigree elegance of his work with Hull Truck. Occasionally he indulges his actors, and rather more often they indulge themselves; the show was into its second week when I saw it; I suspect that on the first night it was a little more sober and less aggressive in its goodwill.

Any gentleman sitting in the front row may get a cuddle from Miss Nice, who certainly is, but may also be belaboured about the head by Miss Wood when she impersonates a particularly militant suffragette. Patrons of either sex are at risk from the free tripe-samples energetically dispensed by Mr Sloman, or from this saliva with which he is no less generous. But from a greater hazard--that of not being amused--they should be totally safe.

The Fosdyke clan is first seen, at the dawn of the century, in direst poverty. Dad is a miner, until he gets persecuted for scabbing ("they broke both the windows of the hovel"), so the family emigrates to Manchester ("industrial Lancashire, I'd know it anywhere"), where the first man they meet is old Ben Ditchley, the reigning tripe-king, who offers a job in his factory ("report to work at three in the morning--seeing it's your first day"). Father prospers, partly through his own abilities, partly through Vicky's; she attracts both Ditchley and his son--naturally, since they are the same actor--and the resultant friction ends with the first dead, the second disinherited, and the Fosdykes in command.

From here on there are both thrills and tears (tripe and onions) as young Ditchley (reverting to tripe) attempts to regain control while the Fosdykes prove themselves more homely and lovable and regional and downright nasty than he is. The performances all get the strip-cartoon note of wide-eyed cynicism, and as the evening spreads itself into all kinds of pastiche--with Miss Nice executing another kind of strip and Mr Jackson imitating Hitler ("that is Herr Schickelgruber, he is painting my house") and soldiers unable to write home from the trenches without mouth-organ accompaniment and Mr Sloman going into a wild Cossack routine at the mere mention of Russia--there seems no reason for it ever to end. Just as you've reached this conclusion it does end, thereby--on top of all its other virtues--sparing you the pain of disillusion.

The Pip Simmons group, rendering Dracula (Royal Court), are hell-bent. They have a powerful talent for grotesquerie--and the music composed and performed by Chris Jordan is the most sinister I know--but hardly any for narrative, and not much sympathy for period. So apart from making the story incomprehensible, they butcher it in two ways: they play every moment and every character sinister, and when this fails they fall back on showing what a giggle the Victorians, clean-living heroes and pure heroines and all that, must have been.

Every scientist is mad, as menacing as Dracula himself; and he looks from his first appearance like something, underwear and hand-mike included, out of The Rocky Horror Show--of which one is enough. His first victim, an estate agent, looks, perhaps because of Mr Simmons's house-style in actors, every bit as frightening. The company still bring off some surprising feats; the fly-eating Renfield sings a song while hanging upside down, bound hand and foot. (He might, I thought, have completed the Houdini effect by escaping at the same time.) The opening could hardly be more nerve-jangling, but you know what they say about starting high.

Counting the Ways (Olivier) is what you would expect of a 60-minute duologue written by Edward Albee, and performed by two characters called He and She: a smoothly uninvolved catalogue of understated marital discontents, sometimes witty (there is a very funny line about a buffet), often arch, and leaving you at the end as unacquainted with the characters as at the beginning. There is still a senior faculty tone about Mr Albee's pair, the man shedding an unaccustomed tear at the decline of civilisation.

Either the actors in Bill Bryden's production are making intermittent stabs at an American accent or the rhythms of the text are asserting themselves at odd moments. Their delivery is short on both worry and crackle. Michael Gough, who used to be a specialist in neurosis, is getting much too gracious; Beryl Reid comes into her own when the words "Identify Yourselves" go up in neon at the back and she steps out of character and tells a cunningly-timed story about herself.

There are better short plays in existence. At the Roundhouse Downstairs, a new company last week dug up Strindberg's The Pelican, in which embittered juniors, embittered as only Strindberg characters can be, turn on their widowed mother to give the lie to her stories of all she has done for them, and of what she had to suffer from their father. It sounds like (and knowing Strindberg's feelings about Ibsen probably was) a riposte to Ghosts. I often find his supposedly major works trying: he was not a stayer. But into a miniature like this he could pack a prodigious amount and even his recurrent tics. The production and performances, like the play, were a strange mixture of clumsiness and accomplishment.
