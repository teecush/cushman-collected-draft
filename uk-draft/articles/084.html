Brave and bracing

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a Harley Granville Barker revival.

ONE of the more disturbing features of the theatre this year has been the extent to which our two most highly subsidised theatres have been encouraged to play safe.

The National Theatre can hardly have been expected to fail with Ibsen, Shaw, Beckett and Pinter--standard authors, infallibly cast. But a national theatre should not only protect, but extend the national repertory, and though we may be within our rights to pronounce the attempted resurrection of Gilbert's 'Engaged' a failure, it is chilling to hear it declared that the attempt should never have been made.

To their credit, the National proceeded undeterred to honour another of the obscurer dead, a foreigner too. After a week of brooding over their 'Phaedra Britannica,' my admiration for the technical skill of translator, director and performers remains immense, though in altering Racine's mythology they have sacrificed more logic than they have gained.

I cannot begrudge the Aldwych their safe period earlier in the year, if only because the revival of Tom Stoppard's 'Travesties,' with John Wood picking his way unerringly through a minefield of jokes and Lenin (Harry Towb) accorded his proper status like the other characters among history's clowns, possesses twice the sparkle of the first attempt and three times the logic. ('If only Tom Stoppard had got it together,' I pontificated last Christmas, 'it would have been the play of the year.' I see now that he had, and it was.) But I respect the RSC the more for their new kamikaze tactics. True there are limits (David Mercer's 'Duck Song' last year was beyond them, even as it was beyond most of us), but Charles Wood's 'Jingo' was a thoroughly honourable flop, whatever it cost. Suppose we agree to stop using the economic situation as a critical sanction.

From all of which it follows that I wish to hear no puling objections to the latest offering at the Aldwych, where on Thursday we assisted at the second performance (not production, performance) of Granville Barker's The Marrying of Ann Leete, the first having taken place on a Sunday night in 1902. How pleasant to be in virtually at the birth of a major dramatist.

The play was actually written in 1899, and is set a hundred years previously; it is one dying era's tribute to another. No, tribute is not the word; and Barker and his characters are very careful of their words, very jealous of how much they may be permitted to convey. He was 21 when he composed it, and the play has the harshness of youth, though not (compared with some of our infant prodigies) either its hysteria or its priggishness. It is also extraordinarily brave.

Ann Leete is a politician's daughter. Her elder sister has been married off for her father's advantage, and she is about to be sent the same way. At first she acquiesces, somewhat frightened but resigned. Only she overhears too much of the family cynicism, the family scheming and--particularly in reference to her sister's marriage--the bitterness of the family failure. The same day as her match is made she breaks it off, and offers herself as the wife of the gardener, the new self-reliant man, named--with heavy but not unpleasant symbolism--Abud. At the play's end, Ann, removed from spacious gardens to her husband's narrow cottage, contemplates an unromantic future. 'I'll do my part . . . something will come of it.'

The play presages change, not only historical (and its characters live in submerged awareness of revolution across the Channel) but theatrical. To us it may recall Chekhov--but the play it most resembles, 'The Cherry Orchard,' it actually antedates. The dialogue can be cryptic and allusive beyond anything in Pinter. It requires attention (and, if possible, a preliminary squint at the text), but magnificently rewards it. Sometimes the words are used, in Jamesian fashion, to polish thought (and to convey a period style with no recourse to archaism), sometimes to conceal it; the characters play devious games. Barker's plays are oddly angled; they tell their stories with a teasing disregard for the obvious.

This one begins with a kissing-game, before dawn, and the images of kissing, of play, of light and darkness reverberate through it, in action as well as dialogue. The Leetes' intrigues are harshly, classically illuminated; the final scene, candle-lit, implies both completion and progression. David Jones's production seems to me a rarer achievement than his Gorki plays (and will, hopefully, inaugurate a similar Barker cycle); Gorki's are juicy plays; obvious gifts for actors. This needs to be played with passion, but within a strict, witty convention. Here, as in 'Phaedra Britannica,' those demands are met. Mr Jones finds the rhythms of the play, and shapes them into personal encounters.

The casting of Mia Farrow as Ann proves an inspiration. She is opposed to a collection of hard stylish actors; Paul Rogers, Richard Pasco, Estelle Kohler at her brittlest and best; only Mike Gwilym, as a brother half out of the family, makes some humane concessions. Among them all Miss Farrow, fiercely vulnerable, has to work out her own stance. Hers is, in no derogatory sense, a very painstaking performance; she learns, and grows, we watch and grow with her. 'I must study politics,' she says with her head on one side (her voice, too, it seems) and the impact on her consciousness is precisely charted. Not all of Barker's ideas come off the page; a Hogarthian rout of wedding-guests remains an intention rather than a fact. But the evening is fresh, vital, bracing, and all things pertaining to iced water.

Steven Berkoff has in the past directed the London Theatre Group, acted with it, adapted Kafka for it. He now puts it, himself included, through its paces in his first original work. Unfortunately East (King's Head) seems to have been written for little reason beyond that of furnishing the group with a text of Mr Berkoff's own; it is aimless, with a tyro's idea of shape. That is, it ends with the same lines as it has begun, in the apparent belief that there is something profound about a loop. (I would have said a circle, but 'East' is not so well rounded.)

The subject matter, loosely assembled, is life in the East End; vignettes have been selected in order to allow scope for maximum aggression which, when it slackens, necessarily entails maximum sentimentality. Much of the dialogue is pastiche blank verse, delivered in the clipped Shakespearean tones made familiar by Peter Sellers, Benny Hill and Laurence Olivier. Some of the prose bits, though, are good; notably a hard-pressed mum's dreamy monologue of escape, delivered in drag by Robert Longden.

Barry Stanton has some huffy, rosy-faced moments as Dad; his off-beat timing suggests that they may be improvised. And as a work-out for its actors the show, fluently staged, is reasonably enjoyable; enabling them to shine as motor-bikes, dodgem-cars (Mr Berkoff's arrival tending to the mechanistic) and even, sometimes, as people.
