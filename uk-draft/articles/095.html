Going off the rails

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

The flaws in John Osborne's Watch It Come Down (the National Theatre's last production at the Old Vic) are manifest and manifold. You could, if you felt traditional, drive a coach-and-four through them. Mr Osborne himself has done something of the kind, only his chosen vehicle is a train. Though referred to as the weekly goods, it crosses the stage twice, within a span of two days. This is typical.

The train passes by what was once a station, but has now been converted into a house. It belongs to Ben Prosser, a film-director of uncertain distinction who 'must have been barmy to buy this place'. His wife Sally tells us that in the very first line; she goes on to fill us in on the details of the menage. There is Ben's old mother, forever offstage and generally forgotten. There is Sally's sister Shirley, a painter, whose presence Ben tolerates because, says Sally, rather clumsily, 'he still has a daft mystical itch that she might be the enigmatic tally to me.' She does appear, though she says virtually nothing. 'She looms quite impressively, though.'

There is another girl called Jo, described in Mr Osborne's usual clotted jargon as 'the sort of randy tongue-in-the-lip' romantic Ben thinks he missed out in his search for the Holy Groin.' There is Glen, an old, dying homosexual writer whom Jo is nursing. And there is a younger pouf (the favoured word in the Osborne old-theatrical milieu) named Raymond who is Sally's help and confidant. He listens to Sally giving out all this information in the opening scene, and shows remarkable patience considering that he must know all of it already.

This, even for a writer who has always been conspicuously bad at narrative, is exposition of staggering baldness. But if one half of the mind condemns the ineptitude, another succumbs to its charm: enough to make one wish that Mr Osborne would stop trying to provide a naturalistic frame for his diatribes and return to the patterned simplicity of A Sense of Detachment.

Watch It Come Down offers both plot and pattern. Neither is fully developed and the chances are that they throttle each other. Virtually nothing is made of the railway setting; one assumes that it represents a desperate nostalgia which Sally ascribes to Ben, but which occasionally gathers over the play like a cloud without much reference to character. Outside the station lies a hostile world; the Prossers are hated (and, we presume, envied) by their neighbours, who employ 'yobboes' first to kill Ben's dog, then to attack the place.

Mr Osborne has no charity for the present (the twentieth century is his major villain. England both its victim and its partner-in-crime), but he has plenty of bile to spare for the sawn-off segment of the past living on the right side of the tracks. There is generalised approval for wise, doomed, honourable Glen and for all-loving Jo, but more specific attitudes are vitriolic. The main focus is the marriage of Ben and Sally, a running sore. Their battles are the play's most immediate source of life. It seems that they are faking a separation, to see what effect it will have on the rest of the menage, but this idea is not pursued. Glen duly expires, and Jo throws herself under the precipitate goods, though whether in despair at the death of love or the persistence of hate is unclear.

Michael Gough plays Glen propped up in bed dispensing prickly benevolence with infirm authority; and Susan Fleetwood's Jo is the image of affection, like a large, warm and beautiful sheepdog. Their scenes read sticky, but act touching: even a lesbian passage of arms, which Miss Fleetwood shares with Jill Bennett's Sally, and which relates to nothing which precedes or follows it. For the rest Miss Bennett plays the usual pinched bitch she and Mr Osborne have evolved between them with more variety than usual; Frank Finlay builds a careful characterisation as Ben, haggard and slightly epicene. Their scenes together are the most nasal on the London stage.

It used to be claimed for Mr Osborne that he did not mince words; but this in fact is precisely what he does. The language, straining for virility, collapses into spiteful heaps. 'Your unlistening, constant rant,' (Ben to Sally) is both an example and an apt description. Ben's first wife (Rowena Cooper), coming in from outside, makes a brief refreshing contrast, tweedily forthright; otherwise everyone, whether blessing or cursing, sounds alike. But if the tone hardly alters, the tempo does, and Bill Bryden's production rings all the changes. Even his inability to make us believe in the offstage characters is turned to account. Mr Osborne's precious group are all we have and for the duration we accept them--not in spite but because of the limitations and the tricks. Though the play corresponds to nothing that I know or credit, though its reverberations are extremely shaky, it somehow manages to impose itself as truth.

At least it shines by comparison with other new work. Roger Milner's You'll Never Be Michel Angelo (Hampstead) is an aimless series of jokes, with few ideas and those above its station. Robin Bailey, still among our most polished comedians, walks tall throughout. This piece was first seen at the Edinburgh Traverse, which now houses Hector Macmillan's The Gay Gorbals, peopled by stereotypes slightly closer to life than Mr Milner's whimsies, but even more wearing.

The Glasgow Citizens' Theatre, which is really in the Gorbals, has in the past few years gained a bright and lurid reputation. Its publicity symbol--a drag Shakespeare--suggests the ambience; so does the splendid cheek which last year offered Seneca's Thyestes in repertory with Sailor Beware. I caught a bold pair of classics last weekend.

Middleton's The Changeling suggested that the Citizens' style was reaching the point of self-parody. The designer, Philip Prowse, was also the director and he offered an image more suited to a generalised idea of Jacobean tragedy, steamy and decadent, than to this particular play. Middleton depicts evil encroaching on normality, but nobody setting foot on Mr Prowse's latticed stage could sanely have expected to get off alive. Individual Citizens' actors (James Aubrey, Mike Gwilym, David Yelland) have shone of late in other companies, but a concentration of slim, saturnine young men can be confusing; I could not tell the minor characters apart. Some things were striking: the central scene between the careless Beatrice and De Flores, the scorned agent who becomes her ravisher (here played as a stolid white-suited slug), is infallible, and was here succeeded by a ritualised rape; Beatrice (a mettlesome performance by Johanna Kirby) surrendered, mysterious Spaniards danced what I took to be the last tango in Valencia. A beggar crawled around muttering Flores para los muertos, reminding us at once of the hero's name, the play's location and the director's existence, all things I would be happy to take for granted.

Goldoni's Mirandolina was another kind of camp, but more fun: a neat comedy of female superiority with Suzanne Bertish as glowingly attractive, if not quite as practical, as a heroine should be; a radiantly decaying Venetian set by Mr Prowse; much witty allusion imported into the text by the director, Robert David Macdonald; and an outstanding performance by Jonathan Hyde, dusty and raddled as an impoverished sponging marquis, gleaming with wit and underpinned, as such acting always must be, by ineffable dignity.
