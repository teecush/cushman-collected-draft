A sunny outlook

ROBERT CUSHMAN talks to Charles Strouse, whose new musical opens this week

CHARLES STROUSE, who was 51 last Thursday, claims to have "a very funny outlook," and I believe him. He talks cheerfully and temperately about both his failures and his successes as a Broadway composer. The successes include Bye Bye Birdie, Applause and the record-breaker Annie.

Right now he has a new show opening at the Queen's, not yet seen in New York, though already tried out in Canada: in Edmonton to be precise, at a theatre run--in the face of much chauvinistic discontent--by the British director Peter Coe. Coe also directs the musical, called Flowers for Algernon, in London where its early arrival is due largely to the availability and the enthusiasm of its star, Michael Crawford.

Flowers for Algernon is not the most obvious title for a musical (everyone immediately assumes it must be a version of The Importance of Being Earnest) but it apparently has considerable commercial appeal in America where the novel (by David Keyes) has long been a cult. When they filmed it in 1969 the name was changed to Charly but the show goes back to the original. It deals with a retarded man who undergoes brain surgery and becomes--temporarily--a genius.

It is, he admits, not merely a strange title but a strange idea for a musical. "But I've always liked the story; and anyway who can tell which the best ideas are going to be? I originally thought Annie might just about make a TV special. And I once wrote the music for what I thought was going to be a perfectly ordinary cops-and-robbers movie called Bonnie and Clyde."

Strouse's involvement in Flowers is due to a friend, David Rogers, whom he has known for 30 years ("I was at summer-camp with him") but with whom he has never worked before. "I saw the play on his shelf, read it and liked it." So the decision was taken to turn it into a musical, and necessarily a heavier one than Strouse has usually attempted.

And here we come to a paradox in him. His optimism seems to have its roots in a consciousness of pain. "My father was a very sick man, and I grew up hating and fearing illness desperately."

It has enabled him to write genuinely bright scores. "Bye Bye Birdie, his first major show and his first major success, must be one of the best escapist musicals ever written. Birdie is the kind of show that makes a composer not only rich but popular; everyone likes you because you're the smart kid at the party." Strouse has two favourites among his own shows, and neither of them are among his greatest hits. One, It's a Bird, It's a Plane, It's Superman, we have never seen over here, though to judge from the record it's a work of considerable charm and poker-faced wit. It may be his ultimate achievement as smart kid at the party. The other, Golden Boy, was staged here with Sammy Davis, as in New York, playing the title role: an updated black version of Clifford Odets's boxer-hero of the Depression.

Golden Boy was also one of the few musicals of the sixties (Cy Coleman's Sweet Charity was another) to make positive use of the pop-jazz idiom that was all around us at the time.

Strouse is aware that pretentiousness can kill a musical, if the talent involved is less than the aspiration. He often, to his bemusement, is asked to give college lectures on the form, causing him to wonder if the whole thing isn't getting out of hand. And yet, he says, "serious music--serial and electronic music--has totally alienated the middle-class concert audience, and intentionally so. So people turn instead to the musical theatre. We spent a good half-hour discussing the infuriating slippery boundary between musical and opera, and failed to define it. But he did say 'the musical differs from opera in acknowledging the third collaborator--the audience. I like people liking me. I like audiences.'

He has written a couple of operas himself, and he had a full musical training, studying with Aaron Copland and in Paris with Nadia Boulanger. "Boulanger was remarkably supple and supportive. She learns about you as a person through your music. She asked me to play her everything I'd written--I was 19 or 20 at the time--so I went through all the serious stuff. She said was there anything else? Well, when I was 12 there was a cute girl I'd wanted to impress and I wrote her this song full of local references called Moon Over 83rd Street." So I played this and Boulanger said very seriously--and she was the first person to say it to me--"you have a great talent for light music."

Trying to exercise this talent back in America, he met a journalist named Lee Adams with whom he collaborated first on songs for revues, then on Bye Bye Birdie and most of his subsequent shows down to A Broadway Musical, a likeable mess which flopped spectacularly last year and which I, as one of the few who saw it, would like to turn into a cult.

Annie and Flowers are among the few shows he has written with other lyricists. Adams, he says, likes to rest and enjoy life occasionally. "He has a healthier attitude than I do. I love the hustle and working all the time. Working with Lee is like a marriage; sometimes you like to have an affair." For the benefit of his wife, somewhere on the high seas en route to Thursday's first night: "Barbara, that is just a metaphor."
