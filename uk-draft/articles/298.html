Pal Joey's back in town

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a Rodgers and Hart revival.

LAST YEAR, in their old quarters at Aldgate East, the Half Moon produced Guys and Dolls as if it were Pal Joey. They have now, logically and delightfully, presented Pal Joey on an approximation of its own terms.

The company are now in residence down the Mile End Road, and their standards in the mounting -- not to say the casting -- of musicals have risen meteorically. For a fringe company with a Germanic bias, Pal Joey may be the ideal American musical. The penultimate work of Rodgers and Hart, first staged in 1940, its originality for its time lies in its unremitting seediness.

It is set for the most part in a Chicago nightclub, some way down the minor league, and its hero is a song-and-dance man of similar status. He talks himself into a job as the club's m.c., and subsequently into the beds of most of the contiguous ladies. His biggest catch by far is Vera, nymphomaniac wife of a presumably purblind local millionaire who has made his bread out of bread. She sets him up in his own showspot (Chez Joey), but withdraws both her cash and her person when a blackmailer appears. Joey, if not quite down, is certainly out.

John O'Hara wrote the script from his own volume of short stories, which are Runyon-and-vinegar; he ran into trouble with his plot (his gangster is a compound of stock gags and stock menace), but he kept the show remarkably free from any trace of sentimentality. That includes the negative variety to be found in the current Chicago, slicker but bludgeoning show. Cynicism was nothing new in Thirties musicals, but it had generally been airy and flippant; O'Hara kept it tied to specific characters and events. Leaving the story in Chicago rather than moving it to Manhattan was in its way an act of integrity.

All this was new territory for Rodgers and Hart, who had previously written warm, high-spirited or melancholy songs with no particular regard for context. Those qualities are rather flattened out for Pal Joey; the score, despite its reputation, is far from their best work. But it is still enjoyable; and Rodgers, in particular, seized on the chance to write music that was smoky, brash or metallic.

Hart's contribution is more problematic. He was a specialist in bawdy (by the end of the Thirties he and Cole Porter had become competitors in innuendo); and in the jewel of the score, Bewitched -- particularly in its sardonic reprise -- he refined this vein to produce an erotic classic, sharply exultant. (The fact that we have grown up on a bowdlerised version of this song makes the original words all the more piquant. I wonder if the correspondent who recently quoted it to me as one of Hart's few touching ballads has ever heard them).

A lesser-known song, You Mustn't Kick It Around, does equal justice to Joey's cockily insensitive sexuality. The other standard, I Could Write a Book, is an example of what goes wrong in the show. Rodgers's tune is, for him, ordinary; and the lyric -- for two uncultivated people who have just met and are only superficially attracted -- becomes, with its dominating literary conceit, an expression of sophisticated romanticism: literal head-over-heels and Hart-over-head.

It is the lyricist himself talking -- which is the constant fascination of his work, but an intrusion here. O'Hara's dialogue set-up is, as is generally the case, clumsy.

And yet... the techniques of Pal Joey have been surpassed and finally superseded. Its style is one-of-a-kind. Robert Walker's mini-production, which cramped Guys and Dolls, releases all that is best in this show. Sian Phillips, in the maneating tradition of the late Hy Hazell, dominates as a Vera basking in financial and sexual security; all she gives to Joey, she knows she can get right back, herself most of all. (She's vocally secure, too: a pleasure to report.) Denis Lawson, appealing but not likeable, wisecracking on the edge of an abyss he senses but won't confront, has Joey sized up; his blank-faced clockwork hoofing is a treat.

So is most of Stuart Hopps's choreography, which picks off a series of tired floor-show routines with venomous enthusiasm. All his seven dancing girls can act, or maybe it's the other way round; anyway they're individuals, especially Susan Kyd's smart cookie and Jane Gurnett's dumb ox. Dodging in and out between episodes, they contribute much to the atmosphere -- mostly hard-boiled but sometimes a bit runny -- as do Jean Hart's lady journalist and Christopher Muncke's club-owner who shrugs through the show with the air of a man who could outperform the lot of them if only he had a number.

The textual history of Pal Joey is of Shakespearean complexity. Mr Walker has collated Good and Bad Quartos (plus his own interpolations), sensibly, barring some solecisms in the lyric-readings. (Details on request.) There is a six-piece band and good glitzy costumes.
