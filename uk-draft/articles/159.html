Tartan blues

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

THERE was a good time to be had at the Edinburgh Festival. There was Carmen and the film festival and good company and Ian McKellen's Shakespeare recital. All of these except the last count for this column as hookey and the charming informality of Mr McKellen's performance really precludes criticism. It included by the way an image-by-image analysis of Mafia Macbeth's "Tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow" which is absolutely invaluable.

The main official drama in the festival's second week came from the Nottingham Playhouse company whose director, Richard Eyre, is about to depart for television. He will be missed; as the best director of new work to come along since Peter Gill. His two festival offerings, though, were unfortunate.

First was Stephen Lowe's Touched, about kindness and cruelty as demonstrated in working-class Nottingham and the world at large between VE and VJ Days - or from the discovery of Belsen to the blasting of Hiroshima. One moment the world is revolted by barbarism, and the next it is adding to the score. That is more or less Mr Lowe's pattern, epitomised by a young lady who develops a hysterical pregnancy when things look hopeful which subsides when they fade back to black. She believes the father to be an Italian PoW; a belief which brings her into great disfavour with her two sisters and almost to blows with her mother.

The most charitable explanation offered for her behaviour is that she is "touched," as in slightly mad. We in the audience, hearing her take off into gentle monologues about her hopes for a gentle future, are meant to feel touched, as in moved, but are likely to feel touched, as in cheated. She is a playwright's convenience, slow to emerge as the centre of the play, but irritatingly immovable once there. It is impossible to believe that, at the end of a six-year war, people chirped so self-consciously about a "new world." They must have been much too tired.

The rest of the play is routine back-fence naturalism, and I am not sure that Mr Eyre does it any service by poeticising it - in plain terms, slowing it down. If he were working with better material his production might well be extremely fine; in the circumstances it is indulgent. The same must go, really, for Marjorie Yates's performance in the leading role; she has quality - a reeling, obstinate fragility - but I wanted to kick her.

This was followed by Adrian Mitchell's White Suit Blues, which does for Mark Twain what Mr Mitchell's Tyger did for Blake i.e. it lets him spend most of his time shaking his fist, literally or figuratively; only when allowed to deliver a monologue, cheerfully demolishing the literary pretentions of Fenimore Cooper, does he come to life; here, of course, the words are Twain's own. The whole is framed in a vast humourless conceit about Twain going to heaven, finding it composed mainly of upper-class Englishmen, and gladly descending to hell. Religion, especially Christianity, is blamed for all its literally--cripples are "God's experiments."

The final scene seems to mean that if we were all jolly atheists, floating down the Mississippi surrounded by our loved ones (everyone, including Huck and Jim, on for the finale) pain would disappear. Mike Westbrook has provided a rousing antispiritual for the occasion and he does good service throughout; it's refreshing to encounter a score on the British stage that really sounds like music. But for a musical, indeed for anything meant actually to entertain an audience or at least keep it awake, Mr Eyre's production is uncommonly louring; it begins with the grey of a funeral and is black-avised hereafter. "Tyger" was at least jolly.

The Traverse has this year returned to its former resident playwright C. P. Taylor, who has provided two pieces collectively known as Walter, he being a Scots Jewish Socialist comedian, living in retirement that would be comfortable if only he wasn't ill. The comfort is afforded by a late success in a TV soap-opera, a series which, he says, achieved the impossible by uniting the Scottish press; they all hated it.

The play is constructed as a deathbed flashback with the various cultural errands of Walter's past alternating in his mind; one would like to say entwining, or even interacting, but in fact there is a peculiar recurring stasis about the play which, despite rich moments in the dialogue, exhausted me. I left half-way through Part Two, which at least enables me to declare the show a play and a half. Certainly Grant Hicks has designed a set and a half - a living-room so complete that there can be no excuse for demolishing it at the end of the run; it should be given to the homeless. Mr Hicks does more for the texture of the evening than the author or any of the actors, though Peter Kelly is resourceful in the lead, and Peter Lincoln engaging as an emblem of respectability.

It is possible that, among the hundreds of shows on the fringe, I missed a couple of masterpieces; what I saw was unhappily typified by The Player King, an attempt to graft we're-all-actors-really implications onto a commonplace and desperately protracted triangle story. If Writer's Cramp, reviewed last week, shows the virtues of the Edinburgh fringe; this exemplifies its vices: arch attempts at experiment for the titillation of audiences who, except at Festival time, don't get out much. It is almost saved by Neil Cunningham, an actor with the aplomb and precision of an emergent Alec McCowen, plus a touch of madness.

Back to London for Dean (Casino), which I tried to approach by forgetting the publicity and concentrating on the title: hoping that it might yet turn out to be a one-man show by Frank Sinatra's sleepy friend, or perhaps a saucily-christened musicalisation of one of Trollope's ecclesiastical novels. But no, it's James Dean all right, or rather all wrong. Dean may have been important for his talent or for his social influence; neither is explored here. It is merely a movieland success story with an unsurprising surprise ending. Probably they now wish they'd done Elvis Presley instead. He at least would have provided cues for song; there is no reason on earth or in Hollywood for the numbers here.
