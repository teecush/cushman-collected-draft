Oh No You Don't!

Robert Cushman · The Observer · Review · 1976-01-25

The new policy at the Phoenix--short runs, movie stars and season tickets--is inaugurated with I DO! I DO!, a two-handed musical about marriage that should do a lot for divorce.

At curtain-rise Rock Hudson takes Juliet Prowse. Over the rest of the evening they age 50 years. (The audience come out feeling pretty old, too.) They mature at different rates. Mr Hudson, sensing perhaps that grey hair becomes him, makes straight for middle age and stays there a good long time. Miss Prowse, on the other hand, remains pert and youthful until the very last moment: a moment at which both performers advance to the footlights, sit down, get out their paint-boxes, and mercilessly line their faces. Having thus earned their pensions, they totter (all old people totter; it's a rule) out of the house which they have inhabited for the duration.

But where do they go? This is the first time that we have been led to believe there is any world outside. When we think about it, we realise there must be, and we also realise that in 50 years it must have changed somewhat. We are never even told which 50 years are in question; the only clues are that Mr Hudson wears a funny old nightcap on his wedding night (until Miss Prowse giggles him out of it) and that about 10 years later, or so I would guess, they can apparently manage to keep a houseful of domestics (unseen of course) on what Mr Hudson makes from successful novels that sell 3,000 copies.

But, confusingly enough, the dialogue throughout reeks of the fifties--or at least of that decade's omnipresent TV comedies of domesticity. Stolid Mr Hudson, dizzy Miss Prowse: the eternal couple. And nothing happens to them, even inside the house. Once or twice, there are hints that they may be falling out of love, but a single song is enough to restore them to bliss. The tagline is something about marriage being difficult but worthwhile. It doesn't square with the facts of the show, which are that marriage is too easy to be worth the attention of mature people. If you're going in for it, say the authors, be like Miss Prowse: don't grow up.

Little good that his big number is followed almost immediately by Miss Prowse's one opportunity to stretch her own nimble legs, providing the only moments that approach excitement. Her voice, though, is a squawk. The score dates from 1966, and if I had heard no worse since I would count myself lucky. I found the first half of the show supportable, the second (where inertia really runs riot) not.

Neither, I am afraid, is there more joy in Cakewalk (Hampstead), an even smaller musical though with a larger cast. There are five performers, all pleasing; Dinah Stabb projects a schoolteacherish primness which makes her outbursts of song agreeably surprising, Gaye Brown, on the other hand, is a red-hot lady for whom song is a natural condition; she might be an English Elaine Stritch, and her Theatre Workshop experience has made her adept at distancing herself from ramshackle surroundings.

This is a valuable gift for a performer in Cakewalk since it has virtually no structure. It does have a setting and some kind of ambience; we are on a Hollywood island inhabited by hard drinkers and tempestuous ladies. It suggests Rain remade as a vehicle for Humphrey Bogart and Carmen Miranda. It was suggested to me afterwards that the idea was to parody nostalgia, but that is something that cannot be done. You indulge nostalgia or you let it alone. This show is an indulger. The music is variable but even the good numbers are sapped of effect by the script, which is limp to nonexistent. A show composed simply of good-humoured songs ought, in theory, to work; but in practice it is always dejecting. We need to be given some reason for being there.

Funny Peculiar (Mermaid) by Mike Stott is the newest of the sex-manual comedies. Like The Bed Before Yesterday and such, it not only preaches liberation but practises it. A good laugh is always liberating, and this piece has more of them than any other play in London. To be sure, they are mostly concentrated in the second act; the journey to the interval is tedious, redeemed only by a few bright lines. Mr Stott, a charitable dramatist, expends too much time on a couple of characters whom neither he nor his actors can make interesting. But his hero, Trevor (Richard Beckinsale), shows signs of life from the start; he is a small-town grocer who is understandably perturbed when the local vicar, suspected of homosexuality, hangs himself from his own bell-rope, but who cannot quite communicate the promptings of his permissive conscience to his wife. He wants to experiment, and she cannot bring herself to like the idea.

The play becomes enjoyably farcical when Trevor is seduced by a voluptuous customer and has to hop naked out of the window to escape her husband; touching when his wife (Julie Walters) finds out and tries painfully to define the limits of her own tolerance; and downright uproarious when Trevor relieves his frustrations by a prolonged bun-fight with a proud confectioner (Peter Postlethwaite). This scene is brilliantly directed by Alan Dossor.

Mr Stott has not quite worked out to what extent Trevor is to be taken as the comic victim of his own principles, or indeed whether he is fooling himself about their integrity. Having met with a slight accident, he spends the last scene encased in plaster, which is not a good position from which to communicate moral niceties; and the final resolution is, both ethically and theatrically, a bit glib. However this closing scene is also the occasion of a hilarious device for whose invention Mr Stott may be forgiven almost anything.

Henry V (Aldwych) lacks the invigorating back-to-the-wall quality which distinguished its first night at Stratford: an occasion on which the RSC fought free against odds as heavy as those at Agincourt. Farrah's sets now seem nothing special; they depended a lot on space, with which the Aldwych is far less generous. But individual performances are firmer, and the play interestingly emerges as having little to do with war. That aspect is covered at second-hand, through the excellent offices of Emrys James's friendly Chorus. But the main burden lies on the tension in Alan Howard's Henry, not between man and warrior, but simply between man and king. He yearns, not quite wholeheartedly, to be the one; performs--arduously but not without satisfaction--the duties of the other. This lends a peculiar excitement both to the ceremony speech and the encounters with Dan Maden's Williams, who is again unaffected. The other actors move through Terry Hands's production with a confidence that does both parties credit.
