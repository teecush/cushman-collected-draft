East of Broadway

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a New York original.

DESPITE all pressures the New York commercial theatre remains more interesting than its counterpart in London. The pressures are certainly still there, and mounting; Broadway claims to have been enjoying a boom in the past couple of years, but however well the successful shows are doing, an uncomfortable number of theatres remain empty.

Nor would I assert that much of what goes on is very good; hardly ever, though, is it as unambitiously bad as much of what now occupies the West End. I have yet to discover the American equivalent of 'Murder at the Vicarage' or 'No Sex Please, We're British.' Most crucially, Broadway benefits from New York's lack of institutionalised theatre on the scale of the National Theatre or the RSC. (I am not claiming that this is a desirable situation or even, for very much longer, an economically feasible one.) For the moment, the best theatrical talent is still on Broadway, since it has nowhere else to go.

Occasionally, therefore, Broadway mounts an original work of a size and quality the West End has long forgotten--not that it always gets much thanks for it. The latest Harold Prince-Stephen Sondheim musical, Pacific Overtures, is about to close after a six-month run, never having recovered from its tepid reception at the hands of the local reviewers. It is unlikely to be seen in London, which is doubly sad, as all the English people I know who have seen the show or heard the score have loved it. I saw it twice within a week and thought it staggeringly good. Its setting is Japan, its subject the opening-up of that country in the mid-nineteenth century to trade with the West, America having taken the initiative in the matter by standing four battleships off the Nipponese coast. The commander of this expedition described his duties as the making of 'pacific overtures,' a euphemism which escalates the title of the show into, by my count, a five-way pun. Wit permeates the entire show. All the characters, Westerners included, are played by Oriental actors (many of them admittedly born in America) and it makes free and eclectic use of Japanese--mainly Kabuki--stage conventions: a narrator with accompanying musicians, an entry-ramp, men playing women. Screens are the basis of Boris Aronson's sets: maybe the finest that even he has designed. They make the show a bountiful gift of air and space.

The wedding of Kabuki techniques to Broadway skills produces of course a bastard form. But this is perfectly appropriate, since the subject of the show is bastardisation. Its upshot is the assumption by the Japanese of Western manners and--in a final jump to the present--their phenomenal victory at the West's own commercial game. A closing number balances the account: for most of the evening Eastern rhythms have been adapted by an American director, choreographer and composer; finally we watch American rhythms sung and danced by an Oriental cast--an ending which vindicates the American theatre's right to meddle with this subject and these styles.

As it is told, this is the most moving of the Prince-Sondheim shows. (I don't know whether Mr Prince considers himself a Brechtian, but I take alienation to mean not that emotion should be denied, but that it should be rigorously inspected.) The central character--a minor samurai employed as an intermediary with the invaders--is played by an actor called Isao Sato, who has a most beguiling quality: wry, tender, and quizzically hopeful. One song, 'A Bowler Hat,' which details his capitulation over the years to Western ideas and Western dress, he performs with surpassing delicacy. Indeed, not the least of the skills this show proves the Japanese to have acquired is that of acting in musical comedy; the level of performance is very high.

A couple of Mr Sondheim's lyrics unconvincingly credit some Japanese ladies with a fluent Manhattan wit (a multiple pastiche number for predatory European ambassadors is another, and a hilarious, matter), but his music--full of sweet dissonances, and haunting, angular melodies, has never sounded more individual or more dramatically apt. One song entitled 'Someone in a Tree' concerns, if you please, the impossibility of knowing the truth about any historical event, and the fading of an old man's memory--all set to a surging, questing rhythm that had me on the edge of my seat with excitement. And there is one song of farewell called 'There is No Other Way,' accompanied by a dance of floating silk, which--in sight, sound and sense--is as beautiful as anything I remember in the musical theatre. It can be heard in London--though diminished without the visual element--in the Sondheim compilation at the Mermaid, as can another song--a sailor's trio--actually done better here than in New York.

By way of consolation, Pacific Overtures has just been filmed for television, Japanese television, of course.
