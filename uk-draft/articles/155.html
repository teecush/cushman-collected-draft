The ship-builder crippled by guilt

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on two RSC productions

Did you ever hear the one about Ibsen and his wife? They were about to leave Norway for their long self-imposed exile in Germany, and they were rushing for the boat. They were late, and Ibsen warned Mrs Ibsen not to look back, but she did and she turned into a pillar of the community.

Ibsen never got over it; he blamed himself. And that story - which may not, I admit, be the literal truth - reminds us of the deep seam of guilt that runs throughout his work. From Brand to When We Dead Awaken his heroes fulfil themselves at the expense of their women, and the knowledge cripples them. Dramatically the women have their revenge. The protagonist of The Pillars of the Community (Aldwych) is Consul Bernick, the kingpin of a small and profoundly insular Norwegian town, grown rich as a ship-builder and now hoping to make millions in railway speculation. But he is off-stage for the most resonant scene in the play. Lona Hessel, whom Bernick once loved and whom he abandoned for a more advantageous match, has returned from America to make him face the lies on which his success has been founded. In John Barton's production Judi Dench plays Lona; she makes a flamboyant entrance (she has been caught up in a circus parade) and no actress could more efficiently dispense the fresh air Lona declares to be her new gift to her home town.

For most of the evening the burden passes to Bernick's sister Martha, whom Patricia Brown plays with exquisite tact. She had loved Johan, the man who took the rap for Bernick's misdeeds (seduction and embezzlement) and to whom Lona has subsequently devoted her life. The two women quietly, smilingly, recognise their kinship, and the texture of the play is poignantly enriched.

Add to them the neglected Mrs Bernick (Eliza Ward) and the orphaned girl (Marjorie Bland) patronised by everyone in sight and you have a quartet (the respectable ladies of the town serving as chorus) whose griefs and grievances - never proclaimed and never whined for - raise the play above the problematics of Bernick's own conscience.

Not that this melodrama fails to excite; though Ibsen would come to write it, it still counts as an early play. It belongs to the period of his second start, and precedes the oppressively perfect masterpieces. Max Beerbohm, vetoing The Lady from the Sea, said that though Ibsen's technique still dazzled, his ideas were sadly dated. For us the situation has changed; the ideas, more complex and self-critical than Beerbohm imagined, still live, but the technique prevents us from getting at them. One winces at the prospect of going to Ghosts, Hedda Gabler or Rosmersholm, seeing the jigsaws fitted together all over again. This play takes a deal of exposition to be swallowed, but thereafter the story - as opposed to the theme - moves in an exhilarating straight line. Its ending, to be sure, might seem abrupt. The mere mention of yet another site being allocated for a shopping centre - particularly, of course, when it happens to be in the middle of some delightful locality - is almost always greeted with feelings of real dread.

Bernick, who has been willing to send a ship to the bottom in order to rid himself of the visitors from the past who threaten his future, is redeemed by the near-loss of his young son and makes a public confession of his own guilt. It is possible, however, to argue that he makes a better thing financially out of repentance than he could have done out of continued sinning. This is the line the RSC takes, and it is probably wise; the facts allow it and the straight ending would seem unbearably flatulent. We are nudged rather heavily, though.

Ian McKellen leaves it an open question whether Bernick at the end is a machiavel or a self-deceiver. He is perhaps too flighty and nervous for so stolid a community to have adopted him as a figurehead. They would have wanted a Doric pillar, and Mr McKellen with his fluting is Corinthian. But on these lines he plays magnificently, especially when under stress. It is great to see an Ibsen hero who loses his temper instead of staying buttoned-up.

Michael Annals's setting brings us yet further from the Ibsen mausoleum; out go the horsehair sofas and in come the cane-bottomed chairs with which the RSC has been wont to furnish its Gorky dachas. There are big airy windows (we are after all beside the seaside) and the whole fits with amazing but appropriate snugness into the Shakespearian timbers of the season's permanent set.

Those Aldwych actors excluded from the community are to be found at the Warehouse, offering naught for our comfort. C. P. Taylor's Bandits is marginally bleaker than Howard Barker's That Good Between Us the previous week, if only because its characters speak and behave as they would, rather than as their author would like them to.

I offer this reassurance, and the promise of an overpowering final scene, to help get you through a hard evening. For his study of urban blight, Newcastle brand, Mr Taylor has turned, as the movies often do, to the police records. The bandits are the one-armed kind (a selection mount guard at the back) and their avatars, two of whom are run in for the murder of a third. But the culprit, according to Mr Taylor, was a mineworker and ex-soldier acting out of jealousy, sexual and financial, and also perhaps out of the ashes of his own largely discarded communism.

This last bit comes over as an author's mannerism; however motivation is not everything, whatever they may have told you in drama school, and Bob Peck makes the killer's gangling animosity a sufficient dramatic fact in itself. Paul Moriarty's all-over trembles as his victim make up unequivocally the best image of physical terror I have ever seen on a stage. Behind them the cops, investigating the murder, uncover a world - that of the bystanders as well as the crooks - fit for anyone to reject: a world of which they themselves are of course a part. If the common-coin expletives of the dialogue sound grotesque on the lips of the superintendent (Hubert Rees), that is the point; he is the arch-matter and the characters' philosophy (by "Z-Cars" out of Samuel Beckett) is ludicrously inapposite to his job.

Steven Berkoff's London Theatre Group was briefly at the Cottesloe in its version of Kafka's Metamorphosis; Terry McGinty won all hearts as the mutant whose dung-beetlehood could hardly be less horrible than life with his family. This point was heavily underscored by turning them into grotesques with voices like slowed-down gramophone records. This distanced and allowed for cunning gradations between theatre and mime. It made for something like dehumanised theatre, but felt, on its own terms, faultless.

The Elizabethan stage at the St George's turns up trumps for The Merry Wives of Windsor; it looks pleasant in itself and is architecturally helpful, even realistic (the case for its appropriateness in plays not set in Elizabethan households is still unproven). The social detail in the dialogue comes off best, particularly from David Horovitch's Slender, with his wistful talk of bear-baiting. Otherwise the production, though full of bustle, is dour; Falstaff is subdued and though the wives seem to live next door to each other, one country and the other county.
