That old sex war

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Caryl Churchill's 'Cloud Nine' and Bob Hope.

IT IS quite some time since the advanced wing of our theatre treated us to a mocking panorama of Victorian society with special emphasis on the hang-ups; and to that extent the first half of Joint Stock's new show, Cloud Nine (Royal Court), is pleasantly nostalgic.

The scene is Africa, the characters mostly British, and a lot of heavy irony about Empire is being energetically shouldered. "Come one and all for help," chants the cast in the course of a rhymed prologue, setting an airy and unimpassioned tone for the whole of the proceedings. However, this tone is difficult to sustain and, though Caryl Churchill's subsequent prose is very often witty, the play falters whenever the jokes run out.

The points being made are familiar, and no doubt truthful; they concern the warping of Victorian sexuality by the traditions of Victorian colonialism. Or it could be the other way about; author and company do not seem to have made up their minds. The trouble is that there is now an agreed code of laughter for dealing with such characters, a taboo in its way as pernicious as any of the Victorians' own, and one which this production does nothing to breach.

To the house of Clive, paterfamilias and famous explorer, Harry Bagley, and immediately the dovecote is a-flutter. The ladies of the household mostly fancy Harry; he himself is for the boys, whatever their complexion. He makes a particular impression on Clive's young son Edward, who is growing up confused: urged towards manly sports, while he would prefer to play with his sister.

A black servant, sniggering up a neighbouring lady's capacious skirts, is appalled when he learns of Harry's comradeship ("There's something dark in women that their men know nothing about"); so Harry must, for his own sake and the family's peace of mind, be married off to the family governess. We get the message--we in fact, within seconds, get all the messages about repression. They make occasional spectral reappearances in Part Two, which is set here and now, but since they were never really alive in the first place, it is difficult to attach much importance to their ghosts. They spoil what is otherwise a far more credible account of sex wars in this second half, which is almost the best thing to arrive on the London theatre this young and dismal year. Dealing with people they know and understand, the show's creators contrive to do them justice, to do themselves justice.

The play is still fragmentary. Act One has skits; Act Two has fragments. But the focus has shifted, and the second half's emotional meaning is as unmistakable as the first half's is over-emphatic. Here sexual role-playing is no longer an emblematic cartoon but something which changes as a personality expands.

The dialogue continues to ring and sparkle; it also, from time to time, probes. All that happens is that two young mothers shack up together and draw into their orbit a male homosexual whose former relationship has broken up. "Do you still," asks his ex-partner, "think of yourself as a woman?" "I'm not sure," is the reply, "what I think a woman's like." This bafflement is the limit of what most of us are prepared to admit in the way of affirmations about sex: one reason why the play's second half is so much more engaging than its patronising first.

Actually, if you did add the two bits together the result would not, I think, be much to the author's or performers' liking. The logic would seem to be that our great-grandfathers, trying to suppress or deny homosexuality, actually encouraged it and thanks to them here we all are in Sodom and Gomorrah and unhappy about it.

The acting is variable, but at best very fine. Julie Covington not only extends but refines her range as an elderly daughter of Empire confused among the vagaries of Britain today. She also plays the sailor-suited Edward in Part One, again with more than skill; but the sexual cross-casting, though maybe intended as its own political gesture, inevitably plays tricks with audience response. It matters more in Part Two, where Antony Sher gives a riotously accurate performance as a little girl; the laughs he gets, though, are bound to be at least those elicited by the performer as well as the character.

Bob Hope was back at the Palladium last week and more relaxed than ever; the highlight of his act came when he reclined on a chaise longue, slid his feet on to the floor and into a dance while the rest of him remained horizontal, and sang with feeling "Lazy." Jokes about his age blended quite naturally into the texture of his act; his pose has always been one of aggressive self-deprecation. Occasionally on the first night danger threatened; not all his gags worked, but he always managed to get to the next one in time. Occasionally he faltered at the start of a new joke, but he never mistimed the punch line; he may never have done so in his life. His is not the highest form of stand-up comedy (and I prefer him in films, where his persona expands into a character; a sneakily likeable one), but it is a good one. Unlike his late confrere Bing Crosby (to whom he paid the best kind of tribute in continuing to make jokes about him), Mr Hope did not come on at the Palladium until the second half. Hope deferred maketh the heart sick: the ears, too, since we listened in the interim to Leslie Uggams, a loud lady singer who brought with her something I have certainly never heard before, to wit a four-man rhythm section composed entirely of drummers.

Another soloist is at the Roundhouse Downstairs: Maxim Mazumdar, an Indian-born Canadian actor, whom I saw in Dance of Gods, a piece I hope is unrepresentative. He appeared before us as an ancient Greek actor-dancer, chatting mainly about his very good friend Euripides. Having just received the script of The Bacchae, he said right out, quoting without acknowledgment from the introduction to the standard modern translation, "It's that unmistakable thing, a masterpiece."

I object not to the plagiarism but to the assumption that expository prose can be made to work as dialogue. And I object beyond that to the whole idea of watching an actor spend an evening conducting imaginary conversations. We go (more or less reluctantly, most of us) to a one-man show in the hope that for once a performer will drop the humiliating pretence that we in the audience do not exist. If he still cannot bring himself to talk to us, then let him by all means talk to himself--but not, please, to invisible interlocutors in whom neither he nor we can believe.
