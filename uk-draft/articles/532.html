Crime and Showbiz

THE HIT Broadway musical Chicago, now given its British premiere at the Sheffield Crucible, begins with a joke pilfered from the opening of "Irma la Douce." We are informed that we are about to be told a story of adultery, murder, "all the things we hold dear." Derivative and patronisingly cynical, that gets the evening off to an appropriate start.

The show's ultimate source is a Twenties melodrama, also called "Chicago" and filmed in the Thirties under the title "Roxie Hart." Roxie is an unsuccessful chorus-girl who gets famous by shooting her lover. The present version concentrates on the similarities between crime and showbiz: just two aspects of the great American spectacle. The same point was made in passing in "Little Me" (c.1962) whose heroine also shot an admirer and was thereupon succinctly advised in a Cy Coleman-Carolyn Leigh number wittier than anything in the present entertainment, to "Be a Performer."

Fair's fair; much of the plagiarism in "Chicago" is self-inflicted. Lyricist Fred Ebb and composer John Kander wrote the score for "Cabaret," whose film version was directed by Bob Fosse; he is now listed only as co-librettist of "Chicago" but he was its presiding genius in New York and his influence can still be seen in Sheffield.

"Cabaret," which came ready-made to Mr Fosse, seems to have released in him a reservoir of self-hatred; after a brilliant career as a director-choreographer he apparently came to despise his own skills but was unable to relinquish them. "Cabaret" used song-and-dance to reflect the rise of Nazism, but it never actually suggested that the two were synonymous. In "Chicago," however, as in his previous show, "Pippin," Mr Fosse pelts us with tinsel, reviles us for applauding it, and then throws us more.

One thing the show does not lack is talent; Mr Fosse is perhaps the most stylish and inventive of Broadway choreographers and he has tirelessly elaborated the central conceit; his Sheffield stand-in Gillian Gregory retains his key ideas (a duet for lawyer and murderess as ventriloquist and dummy, an execution staged as a rope-trick) and works energetically around them. The director, Peter James, meantime works energetically and efficiently round her; I doubt if the Crucible's open stage has been better used. Messrs Kander and Ebb have gone off since "Cabaret," having perhaps written too many slam-bang numbers for Liza Minnelli, but they are still competent, and two of the tunes ("Class" and "Nowadays," both late entries) have distinction; the rest are exercises in particular styles, brought off with bloodless accuracy.

There are two leading ladies, since Roxie is set against a less successful and extremely envious man-killer named Velma. The roles were created by Gwen Verdon and Chita Rivera, which enabled tourists like myself to collect two stars for the price of one but left each with less than enough to do; the story, being split between them, was exhausted by the interval. Besides, one's instinct to applaud was frustrated by the whole tone of the show; Velma starts Act Two with a cry of "Hello, suckers" which is enough to make any audience guarded.

One feels less uncomfortable about resisting Antonia Ellis and Jenny Logan, though the latter is accomplished and the former rather more. The leading man, Ben Cross, is colourless, which dulls the effect of his big shyster-lawyer's number "Razzle Dazzle," for which Miss Gregory is also off form.

Meanwhile the Crucible's second new-play festival (in the studio) has come up with a major work. Howard Barker's Love of a Good Man concerns death-worship, as practised in the wake of the First World War. A rich woman is anxious to recover and honour the body of her son, killed at Passchendaele; one of the undertakers contracted to dig up and re-bury the dead becomes besotted with her and intrigues prodigiously to secure her treasure, the ultimate accolade of being interred as the Unknown Soldier. The two are obviously made for each other, though in the end (since a class war theme is being simultaneously sounded) she ditches him for the Prince of Wales.

Natasha Parry makes a stiff lady and Roger Sloman an over-clownish gent, but Toby Salaman gives a most acute account of the prince, painfully well-meaning and totally reined in by his position. The writing of this role, an ambiguous cartoon, is one of the finest things Mr Barker has done; and the play as a whole, sprouting subsidiary actions with perfectly congruent inventiveness, his most impressive to date. As in his previous good play, "Claw," the third act is the best with a raw mournfulness reinforcing the mockery.
