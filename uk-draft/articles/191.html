Driven quite mad by peace

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on David Hare's new play and a Japanese company.

DAVID HARE'S Plenty (Lyttelton) is described in a programme note as a history play. The history comes in two varieties: Social and Case.

We skim the years between 1943 and 1962. At the first extremity Susan, aged 17, is seen working as a courier to the resistance in France. She then pursues a varied, but generally disastrous, peacetime career; much of it spent married to a junior diplomat, apparently fated to remain junior for ever. There are occasional references to ration-cards and such; but as far as the actual style of the dialogue is concerned, time stands still, strongly suggesting the disenchanted 1970s--particularly the version of them familiar on our best-regarded stages.

Two outside events are mentioned: the Festival of Britain, a symbol of manufactured national reassurance, and Suez, standing for disaster. The latter is in fact the first act-curtain, and it is a flashpoint for Susan. But for us, and for the play, it seems too obvious, too isolated; we know, by now, what to think about Suez.

Susan takes it at the time as a welcome portent. "Everything is up for grabs, we will see some changes." She is reiterating, in opposite terms, an optimism she also expresses on a peaceful French day at the end of the war: "There will be days and days and days like this." There won't and there aren't. If you think things look good, they change; if change itself looks good, it doesn't happen.

The problem with using these dramatic poles is that it hardly matters what you put between them. Almost any evidence will support you. So Susan's own story comes over as arbitrary, both in itself and its relation to external events. Suez gives her a chance to lash out at the Foreign Office; when her husband's career seems blocked she has another go. We can all agree that the Foreign Office represents Britain at its most British, and for a few moments it seems clear what the play is meant to be about. Then the picture clouds again.

The trouble with Susan as a mouthpiece is that you never know where, intellectually, her attacks are coming from; she becomes a front for generalised disaffection, and what cannot be unloaded on her is transferred to her scarcely individualised sidekick Alice. They generate between them a few laughs and a good many more sardonic chuckles. They belong in spirit with the heroes and heroines of John Osborne.

Mr Hare is of course a far more controlled writer than Mr Osborne. He remembers to attack his protagonists; he does so, in fact, with icy ferocity. Susan's wartime record, the implicit justification for her intransigence, also gets blamed for it; you didn't talk to people much in the resistance and sex was an encounter of strangers. The pressures of peace drive her mad, literally, and marriage hardly helps. She ruins her husband's career, but even this matters less than it should, since we never know what his job meant to him anyway. Late on he denounces her for her selfishness and delivers, from the audience's point of view, the most satisfying tirade of the night.

The good will earned by his delivery of this speech brought Stephen Moore (though he is excellent throughout) the warmest of responses at the curtain-call. It feels like the last word on Susan; but since the play proceeds for another two scenes it obviously isn't. Like the singer in Mr Hare's Teeth 'n' Smiles, she is indulged, castigated, then indulged again. There is a fine line between irony and equivocation, and the play ends up on the wrong side of it. Susan has witty moments, even moving moments, but she never comes through whole and alive.

She does work, though, as a whopping part for Kate Nelligan, who gives a performance of shattering composure which when itself shattered--as when suddenly clinging, feet off the ground, to a fellow-agent, or in the frantic measured declaration "I am in temporary need of this room"--genuinely disturbing. Julie Covington pounces voraciously on the scraps Miss Nelligan leaves over and shows a welcome ability to transcend not just her own period but her own age-group; she gets visibly solider as the evening progresses.

There is much good miniature work as well: Basil Henson and Frederick Treves as soft and hard FO chiefs, David Schofield and Lindsay Duncan as the losing youth of two decades. There is no lack of accomplishment in the playing, in Hayden Griffin's sets, or, apart from an occasional stateliness (possibly endemic to the Lyttelton), in Mr Hare's direction. No lack of accomplishment, come to that, in his writing. It is not a wasted or unexciting evening; merely a frustrating one.

The Japanese company Tenjosajiki has come to Riverside Studios with Directions to Servants, title from Swift, substance a sequence of stunning images of sadomasochism; something in which we can all take an interest, though not necessarily when served neat for two hours.

I have a recurring problem with the Theatre of Stunning Images tout court; I am mightily impressed for 10 minutes, I then go on being impressed, but am bored as well. (As with Tadeusz Kantor's more quiescent The Dead Class at the same address a year ago.) If the human condition is really being stripped bare, then the human condition has been overrated. That the director Shuji Terayama is a master (that seems the right word) and that he commands an intrepid group of automata is undeniable. This is dead-end theatre; but you should see it once, and now is probably the time.

Royce Ryton's comedy The Unvarnished Truth (Phoenix) has an incredible plot. It becomes clear that this is its point (sounds of critics striking their heads on the floor as rugs are pulled from under them), but I don't see why the characters have to be incredible as well. Under such circumstances laughter, on any continuing basis, becomes difficult; when it does come, it induces self-hatred.

The leading players, Tim Brooke-Taylor and Graeme Garden, have slight stage personalities, though Mr Garden purveys an endearing brand of casual panic. Jonathan Lynn's production, logistically good, is disfigured by some of the worst supporting acting in London. I am forbidden to tell the story, but, what the hell, it involves death and a completely new brand of sexual discrimination. There.

At Salisbury's newish and attractive Playhouse, I caught, not having been inculcated, a new kiddie-rock epic called Gilgamesh, he being an extremely primeval Sumerian hero--or hero as the opening couplet has it. Since there is no dialogue, that kind of aural torture goes on all night. The music includes occasional relieving bits of pastiche. There are cute lighting effects; there is cute everything.
