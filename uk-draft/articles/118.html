National disappointment

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Goldoni at the Olivier Theatre.

THE Venetian Carlo Goldoni, Italy's most celebrated comic playwright, is high on the list of neglected writers whom critics like myself are constantly nagging the National Theatre to resurrect. This week, at the Olivier, they obliged us, in theory at least; in practice they annoyed us considerably. Il Campiello ('The Public Square') is as faint an evening's entertainment as this company can ever have offered.

Goldoni's career began in the mid-eighteenth century when Italian theatre was dominated by the stereotypes of the commedia dell'arte; his first success came in persuading its performers to act his texts instead of their own improvisations. Later he broke further out by discarding stock characters and plots and writing about his own society. Even by our own standards Il Campiello is unconventional, jettisoning narrative and character in favour of community and locale.

Dramatic interest is equally split among nine people who live on three sides of the square. (Who or what lies behind the fourth wall we never discover.) Three old widows and four youngsters play games, quarrel, rampage, enjoy themselves; at the end one boy and girl are married and another couple betrothed. A more sedate young lady and her bearish guardian make up the number; she considers herself a few notches above her neighbours, and he cannot stand any of them. He comes from Naples, and this seems to involve a class as well as a territorial difference, reinforced by the arrival of a Neapolitan count who seems remarkably well pleased by just about everyone.

Bill Bryden's production attempts a miniature re-enactment of Goldoni's own progress. There are puppet-shows in the foyer and a carnival procession through the stalls as the lights go down, corresponding to the commedia inheritance that Goldoni never thoroughly abandoned. Then flats are erected round the stage, representing the houses in the square and the play, more or less realistic, begins. The trouble is that both styles seem half-hearted; the cavorting in the audience is more noisy than vital, and there is more volume than energy about the main action as well. Everything is insubstantial; Hayden Griffin's set is probably true enough to the scenic modesty of the original staging, but we need more; we need to be given a solid awareness of a setting now so far removed from us, particularly if the setting is essentially the play.

Goldoni wrote more than 200 pieces, so it can hardly have mattered to his original audience if one of them was sketchy; it would be sustained by their experience of the others and--when he was in his realistic vein--by the life going on around. Of the two Goldoni plays that have taken some root in English, The Servant of Two Masters offers vaudeville pyrotechnics, and Mirandolina a captivating heroine. But in Il Campiello atmosphere is everything and this production muffs it.

The kisses and scuffles are routine; it is difficult to keep track of which daughter is jealous of whose son. The actors do little more than say the lines; the translation, which runs to such smart ripostes as 'the same to you with knobs on,' may sell the original text short, but not, I suspect, by much. Peggy Mount, flourishing a broom like a pantomime dame, puts in some ripe bitching with Beryl Reid, but it leads nowhere. Jeananne Crowley, fresh and baby-faced, adds to the excellent impression she has made in several small roles at the National. Patti Love, by contrast, always looks best in other companies; as the bride here she gives a clenched, raucous performance, leaping up and down to express proletarian joie-de-vivre.

She is unlucky in being burdened with what this production assumes to be the message of the play, defiantly asserting her celebratory rights against the noise abatement man from Naples, the square's square. The Neapolitans speak standard English, the Venetians mostly Scots; some are Irish and Miss Mount of course Cockney, so the pattern never quite establishes itself. It does enable Morag Hood to give a moderately delightful account of a young lady from primmest Edinburgh; though as the Count--the outsider and so the role that might focus the play--Michael Gough restricts himself to bland neutrality. The text at least hints that he might be an arrant fool or a fortune-hunter; but Goldoni is too carelessly sunny to pursue these possibilities and Mr Gough declines to take the initiative. That indeed is the note of the evening. Acoustically too it is unsettling, for ever perched on the edge of inaudibility.

An earlier National production, Peter Shaffer's Equus, seems set fair for infinity at the Albery: a cunning mixture of suspense, psychology and religion which is limited without being meretricious. Michael Jayston now plays the psychiatrist; conscientious and good-natured, he falls short of Alec McCowen's tortured pedantry and Colin Blakely's wild frustration, but far surpasses Richard Burton, who on Broadway offered boredom topped off with rhetoric. After so much exposure I was surprised to find the play still holding me; surprised, too, to find John Dexter's production now less remarkable for spectacle than for human detail.

Jane Wenham and Glyn Owen have discovered much in the bewildered parents though their social context is still baffling; why has their son never had any friends? David Dixon's performance, though the most accomplished I have seen, is too knowing to stifle doubts. The play shares the weakness of Howard Brenton's Weapons of Happiness: its elements (more particularly its characters) have not been welded together, craftily though they have been assembled. (Metaphors are dropped like clues in a thriller.) All the same, Mr Shaffer deserves defending on one point at least; he does not claim that the blinding of horses is a necessary part of Alan's vaunted 'worship' (it is a disastrous mutation that would not be repeated), nor does he suggest that what the boy needs is a nice lay. But Alan's equine passion is finally too specialised to be meaningful; the symbol rides off with the play.

A style of humour flowered at the beginning of the sixties, in the plays of Harold Pinter and the revue-sketches of Peter Cook: deadpan, London-accented, bleakly absurd and largely dependent on the blockish enunciation of unglamorous place-names. It has nourished the theatrical fringe for years: Henry Woolf, one of its best exponents as an actor, puts it to good use as a playwright in Underarm Bowling (New End).

Among his cast Robert Walker handles it particularly well, especially when deploring the ascendancy of his violent impulses over the pacific ideals that have led him to a Buddhist monastery. It is monastic politics with which the play is more seriously, but less happily, concerned; though Mr Woolf's wit is still sharp enough to evoke a rich American devotee, crazily game for anything and a terror when she finally knows she has been had, played to the life by Ellen Sheean.

Hull Truck is at the Bush this week with Bridget's House, which was intermittently good at the Young Vic and should be better now.
