Mine eyes dazzle

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Webster and Priestley

ADRIAN NOBLE is an assistant with the RSC who has been let out to do a production of The Duchess of Malfi at the Royal Exchange, Manchester.

He seems to have a complete understanding of play and space--both of them tricky--and has used each to bring out the strengths of the other. The arena stage is left uncluttered; a thick strip of carpet along its diameter is enough to suggest the poisoned courts of Webster's action.

At the beginning the honest steward Antonio describes, for his even more honest chum, Delio, the dispositions of the three siblings--Duke, Duchess, Cardinal--with whom his own life is about to become fatally entwined. For a moment the story stops, with each participant bathed in his own pool of illumination; and an effect which might seem ludicrously studied on a proscenium, or even a platform, stage here seems completely right.

Again, there is the episode in which crazy Duke Ferdinand visits his imprisoned sister and leaves her with, as she thinks, the severed hand of her husband, Antonio. We are near enough to the action for it to be played in complete darkness without losing us, as both credibility and atmosphere demand.

As with light, so with sound: the Exchange has its own aural equivalent of Sensurround, an enveloping acoustic. Moments of defiance or determination--charged single lines--are pitched to wake the obliging Exchange echo. For some more sustained passages--the elegy spoken by Bosola, the prince's hit-man, in the disguise of a sexton, and his earlier briefing by Ferdinand--the actors have, I assume, been equipped with throat-mikes; certainly the effect is unearthly, and yet totally persuasive.

The rediscovery of the Jacobeans--and of the means to do them justice--has been the most encouraging development in the British theatre in the last couple of years, and it is to the poetry that this production is finally keyed.

This has nothing to do with golden voices, everything with a considered imaginative approach to Webster's pervasive wit. He was the nearest of all the playwrights to the metaphysical poets; and The Duchess's swooning of Antonio is carried out as a series of geometrical conceits. 'All discord, without this circumference, is only to be pitied,' says Helen Mirren's Duchess, spreading her arms. Hers is not a great performance, but it is captivating and ultimately moving.

Mike Gwilym delays Ferdinand's madness until the rebuke to Bosola, 'Why didst thou not pity her?' which, though crazy in context, is natural and even gentle in delivery. This becomes the keynote of his performance. Sane, he is a routine neurotic villain; mad, he develops a highly disturbing charm.

Julian Curry makes a suave and secular Cardinal, and Peter Postlethwaite establishes the worth, the bewilderment, and the timidity of the declassed Antonio. Bob Hoskins admirably performs the basic duty of any Bosola--that of keeping us with him through the moral twists that corkscrew through Webster's universe; but his subdued Cockney lacks the vocal flexibility to make the journey exhilarating. Bosola is an escaped galley-slave, but he is also a decayed gentleman. It could be one of the play's ironies that he is actually better-born than the Antonio he half admires, half despises.

There is a revival of comparable merit at Greenwich where Alan Strachan shows J. B. Priestley's Time and the Conways to be a play three-quarters superb. This is the one which jumps from 1919 to 1937 and back again. In Act One a northern middle-class family--widowed mother, four daughters, two sons--enjoys and celebrates itself; in Act Two it confronts financial ruin and the failure of all social and marital hopes; in Act Three it returns to happiness while the audience smugly ponders how little the characters know and spots the seeds of the approaching debacle.

This makes for some admittedly crude dramaturgy (the nadir comes when the youngest daughter, whom we know will soon die, cries out that she is going to live) but it is saved by the efficiency and penetration of Mr Priestley's writing. The second act is as tough and chilling a sequence as any British playwright has brought off this century. Beyond praise is the interweaving of the personal and political so that Madge, the spinster schoolmistress who put her trust in the League of Nations, stands for a whole generation of disappointed liberalism; while her mother--outmoded even in her own time--clings to her wastrel soldier son who is 'the same sort' and the most reactionary of them all.

Meanwhile the despised new industrialist--an Antonio who refused to be intimidated--turns out the most successful and unpleasant of all, exerting a nameless tyranny over the once-proud Conway daughter who was unwary enough to marry him.

It is a pity that Mr Priestley felt obliged to wrap up a quite legitimate use of flash-back-and-forward in spurious mysticism about time, with a soothing bit of Blake to make everything cosy. Reduced to common sense the moral is that yesterday's pleasure is no less real than today's pain, and that tomorrow they will be equally part of the past. But the theatre, of its nature, insists that the present is all-important, and indeed Mr Priestley's specific practice here contradicts his theory.

The effect of Act Three depends on our already knowing--from Act Two--what is really going to happen and regarding it as all-important. However, the theorising only takes a couple of minutes and what remains in Mr Strachan's production is excellently modulated, placed and--despite some self-conscious throbbing as the actors approach the significant and/or juicy bits--played.

Some of John Dankworth's tunes for Colette (Comedy) are very attractive; but his lyrics run to such couplets as 'You'll see Puccini and Zola/Toulouse Lautrec in his bowler.' This in a number called, I'm afraid, 'Paree.' Even this might conceivably be saved by flashy production but Wendy Toye's idea of excitement is to send sequins of light winking round the stage. Even a goodish lyrical idea--as in 'I'm about to make the same mistake ... more than 17 times'--is run coyly into the ground, and then reprised.

Nor can the songs find any support from the book; Mr Dankworth merely supplies a series of limp uncharacterised sketches taking Colette from pigtails to arthritis, via three husbands (all played, together with a lesbian girl-friend, by Kenneth Nelson) and a trip on the Normandie apparently undertaken only so that Glen Laine, looking forward to visiting New York cabarets, can do a Dietrich impersonation. (For this she left home?)

Miss Laine has an agreeable stage presence and her singing, warm-voiced as ever, is less flighty than usual; only once does she resort to bluesy melismatics. They are misplaced but one can understand the desire to bring in some excitement somehow. The narrator, very tactful, is John Moffatt, in a red smoking-jacket, to go with the heroine's little red room. Occasionally the lighting goes the same colour and Tim Goodchild's elegant set takes on the complexion of a tandoori chicken. Or, perhaps more appropriately, a tandoori turkey.

The Passion has returned to the Cottesloe. Part One (Creation to Nativity) must rank as the most inventive of Bill Bryden's promenade productions, and the most engaging in its involvement of the audience; it is far tighter, not to say more audible, at home than at the Assembly Hall in Edinburgh. If the text seems sometimes thin, blame the Middle Ages.
