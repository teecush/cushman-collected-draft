A famous Thane

I AM sorry to go on about the Royal Shakespeare Company's public relations but their latest display advertisement combines a puff for the famous Royal Shakespeare Theatre (gosh, not the Royal Shakespeare Theatre?) with the announcement for the Aldwych of Wedekind's celebrated comedy The Marquis of Keith. Just where these celebrations have been carried on I am unsure, but I doubt if you were invited.

Anyway, this week at Stratford they have given us William Shakespeare's famous tragedy of Macbeth, with the celebrated Scottish actor Nicol Williamson in the title role. Ever since he began astonishing us a decade ago we have awaited Mr Williamson's Macbeth for many reasons, not all of them ethical. Still, it was shrewd of Trevor Nunn, the director, to capitalise on his star's origins and present us with a Scotland fit for nationalists to speak in. All the gentlemen have a bash at the accent, the only southerner audibly present is Helen Mirren's Lady Macbeth, a sexy predator who has crossed the border to drain her husband of his virtue and allegiance as if they were North Sea oil.

Visually there is nothing particularly Caledonian about Mr Nunn's production, which appears to be going on partly in its hero's head and partly in church. As we enter the theatre we are faced by what appears to be an altar-table; doors at the back of the stage are able to blow open for processional entrances, as if the characters were coming at us down the nave of a cathedral.

At the beginning of the play we watch the coronation of Duncan, a paradigm of order; a pair of white curtains close across the scene, and, in shadow-play, we watch destruction sicken. Some kind of horned being appears to be breaking up the ceremony with a gigantic inverted crucifix; silhouettes can be peculiarly horrific. It looks as though three figures are levitating from the debris, and sure enough, when the curtains part, there are the witches, perched cosily on a giant chandelier. The conflict of good and evil established, we begin the play.

The theological emphasis enables Mr Williamson to give full value to passages that are generally skimped: 'the eternal jewel given to the common enemy of man' is a realisation that eats into his consciousness, and into ours as well. There is no more immediate actor on our stage and his performance develops through a series of breath-taking insights. At the beginning he is the most jovial of successful generals and at the close the most hopeless of forsaken tyrants.

Other actors have shown me desperation, but I do not believe I have ever had a clearer glimpse of despair (not, in fact, a condition encountered in many roles) than in Mr Williamson's last scenes, and in particular in his speaking--shorn of false, or any other, nobility--of 'tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow.' (Mr Nunn's production, which can be surprisingly literal in a symbolic sort of way, gives him an actual brief candle to blow out, just as earlier he has had a real poisoned chalice to put to his own lips.) It is time, by the way, to dispose of the assumption that this actor cannot speak verse. A Victorian critic, paying tribute to Irving's 'staccato, raven croak,' held that elocution was primarily the art of making yourself heard and understood. By that standard Mr Williamson, like Irving, is a superb elocutionist.

But he can be heavy-handed with it: literally. Climax after climax in this performance is accompanied by the steady pounding of fists on furniture. There is a heart-stopping moment when Macbeth first catches sight of Banquo's ghost, but the relentless percussion which follows can only cheapen it. Some of the power of his concluding scenes derives from their stillness, with Macbeth perched high and lonely on a devil's throne, itself perched on what appears to be several layers of table. By this time the subjective aspect of the production has become paramount, scenes in which Macbeth does not appear are cut to the quick (which does not mean played very fast), while minor characters are allowed as little time on stage as possible.

Still, an exclusive focus on the tragic hero is, at this stage of theatrical history, no bad direction in which to err. It chimes, too, with other RSC productions; Richard II to an extent, certainly Dr Faustus, even Tom Stoppard's Travesties, have all led us into their protagonists' minds; and have occasioned an unprecedented quantity of major acting. As far as the classics are concerned I am ready to subscribe to the cult of the individual. It may mean that the RSC, who have crested with the histories and never lacked for success in comedy, will finally be able to conquer the tragedies.

Robert Patrick is a hugely prolific off and off-off Broadway playwright; and if that intelligence makes you yawn you are hardly likely to be revived by the information that Kennedy's Children consists entirely of interconnected monologues delivered by five human casualties in a downtown New York bar, time the present. However, wake up and make straight for the King's Head. The play is fantastic. Its subject is the sixties, a decade which, according to one of the five, began with the death of Marilyn Monroe. More central is the Kennedy assassination; the first one, as another character carefully, ghoulishly, puts it.

One of the quintet is a JFK freak, she worshipped 'the Kennedy legend and time stopped for her on the day of the murder.' Her obsession could be ludicrous and Mr Patrick does get some laughs out of it, but his writing and the acting of Vivian Pickles, with her careful delivery and nervous eyes, are so finely balanced that ambivalence is triumphantly preserved. Don Parker plays a gay actor who traces for us the rise and fall of underground theatre. His evocations of Greenwich Village, icily hilarious, do a great deal to draw us into the play; his later speeches slightly outlive their interest. Jan Waters paints a bitter, not quite defeated picture of a glamour girl born after glamour had died.

Then there is a GI, finding cynicism in Vietnam. His is the weakest part in the piece. He takes us over unhappily familiar ground and his presence in the bar seems to be symbolic rather than actual; a play should abide by its own rules, however tenuous. But Deborah Norton throttles all doubts as a veteran of every protest, every movement of the decade, suffering a little from her own disillusion and a lot from that of her man; to be in the same small room with Mr Williamson's, and Miss Norton's, anguish has been a week I shall not forget.

At first I distrusted the play; then it began to tap my English nostalgia for New York (gratitude here to Clive Donner's direction and John Scully's set); by the time it had conducted its unsparing, unsneering progress through the heroes of the decade I grew up in (Kennedy only the figurehead and the title unfathomably ironic), it had grabbed me as very few plays ever have.

This has been a week for heroes; John Whiting was one, fashioning a wry, elegant victory from defeat. He wrote of them as well: I find his life, though, more appealing than his art. Marching Song, revived at Greenwich, proves to be no masterpiece, though it contains some bracing scenes and is worth checking out.
