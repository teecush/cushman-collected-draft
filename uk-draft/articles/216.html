Finite variety

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Peter Brook's new production of Antony and Cleopatra.

PETER BROOK's production of Antony and Cleopatra (Stratford-on-Avon) is an excellent evening's entertainment; don't let anyone tell you different. All the same, Tuesday's audience was definitely underwhelmed.

Mr Brook is now generally felt to have a historic function at Stratford: about once a decade, he arrives and changes everything. This time it seems at first glance that the company has been ahead of him; his production fits as neatly as is possible on that stage, into the intimate "chamber" mould that the RSC has pioneered in the Other Place.

But I forget: Mr Brook has already practised this half-way-house staging at the Bouffes-du-Nord in Paris, where his French Timon of Athens was a beautiful piece of storytelling. Using the native text of Antony, he is more constricted, though actually the production's greatest merit is its unremitting concentration on the text - a virtue not uniquely Brookish, but as much to be praised in him as in John Barton or Trevor Nunn within the RSC or Peter Gill (who may well be Mr Brook's heir) without it.

Sally Jacobs's set is bounded on the sides by movable trestles and at the back by glass screens. It is a set more practical than pretty (and at that it is more attractive than the costumes, which include a generous assortment of striped nightwear and turn at least one attendant Egyptian into a Desert Song Bedouin), but it proves itself in the notoriously difficult monument scenes; with no split-level pretences the dying Antony can simply be hauled horizontally to Cleopatra wrapped in her and her women's scarves.

Earlier, the battle scenes are suggested by the hurling of blood upon the screens - the only stand-out shock effect in the production, unless you count the carousal on Pompey's galley which works, like some of the best scenes in Evita, by going on just the vital bit longer and more, intensely than you would think possible. (Richard Peaslee's music, here and throughout, has unusual distinction.)

Otherwise the startling moments are all in the acting; they begin when Antony and Enobarbus break into helpless joyful laughter at the news that Antony's wife is dead. The episode brands Patrick Stewart's Enobarbus not (as is usual) as the keeper of his general's conscience, but as his junior partner in dissipation. "Now he'll outstare the lightning" is spoken in admiration, even though followed by his realistic plan for desertion. The conflict eventually and graphically kills him. He prefaces his anthology description of Cleopatra with a proprietorial "I will tell you," as who should say, you ain't heard nothing yet.

Even more complex is Jonathan Pryce's Octavius. Maecenas talks of Caesar seeing himself in Antony's spacious mirror. This is the relationship played by Mr Pryce: at first dazzled by his senior partner, tempted by him into surprising levity; then sobering up in horror, regarding him increasingly as an example to be studied, dreaded and destroyed. "He calls me boy," he cries exultantly on receiving Antony's craziest message (and using it as a paper kite): as who should say, he's really blown it now.

David Suchet, completing a fine season, finds more in Pompey than could have been suspected; he, unlike Caesar, gives way to his own generosity and dies of it. Then there are Paul Brooke's complacently agreeable Lepidus, Paul Moriarty's Menas, Paola Dionisotti and Juliet Stevenson as Charmian and Iras, Richard Griffiths unrecognisably straight as Cleopatra's messenger and himself again as the Clown (the only actor I have known to take Shakespeare at his word and make the asp-man red-nosed funny), Raymond Westwell's old soldier drawing, or so I guess, on his Churchill at the Other Place, Marjorie Bland's Octavia is colder than necessary, but really only two performances disappoint.

You guessed. It would be presumptuous to dictate terms for the playing of Antony and Cleopatra. But there are clues; both are defined by other characters' attitudes towards them. (That is why I have dwelt on the supporting playing; its superb quality is not luxury but a necessity. All of these actors have at some time to carry the baton.)

The story dictates that almost everyone in the play shall be in thrall to Antony and he to Cleopatra. Here it isn't believable. Alan Howard gets Antony's madness (suicidal in his determination to fight by sea, by sea, which words he burns up), but not his child- or god-like magnanimity. He is a loner: still Coriolanus. Glenda Jackson meanwhile is still Elizabeth I, capable of a superb death-scene (she is transformed, as she should be, from the moment she enters the monument) and of a breathtaking thunder-and-lightning transformation when the messenger tells her of Antony's marriage. (When he flees, she throws a knife after him.) But Cleopatra's sexuality is left in abeyance, where in fact Shakespeare himself left it. He, using the code of the boy-player, could afford to: we cannot.

Accepting that, we can feel with and applaud her - can appreciate, as we cannot with the lovers up the road, how a bitch can inspire devotion. The cheers that were stifled at Antony rang out next night for Pam Gems's Piaf (Other Place). Miss Gems is an imperfect playwright, but man, I mean woman, I mean person, can she ever write parts for actresses. To Alison Fiske's Fish and Sheila Allen's Queen Christina must now be added Jane Lapotaire's Edith - "Ede" as her friends on the Parisian streets call her. This little sparrow is a sparrer.

The play follows her from cheerful, foul-mouthed whoredom to loneliness-at-the-top; drugs and desperation and car accidents, caused by her inability to keep her hands off the men at the wheel. Sometimes she lashes out at the middle class (whatever that may be) and occasionally Miss Gems seems inclined to humour her, but in general what makes the play is the author's realisation that - with whatever provocation and whatever excuse - it was Piaf who destroyed Piaf.

Actually our warmth to her gets mixed in with our warmth towards Miss Lapotaire, whose success here is the most involving since Angela Lansbury's in Gypsy. Even her early chirpy vulgarity (in partnership with an irresistible Zoe Wanamaker) is unsentimental and the ageing, done without change of costume or make-up, is almost consummate - though she cannot quite manage the gnarled imperiousness that you hear in Piaf's records.

Her numbers are the only gash in an otherwise seamless assumption; though she sings well, gentility invades her voice. But her shakes as she approaches the mike in later days are relentlessly truthful. There are good sideline performances by Ian Charleson and Allan Hendrick; others in Howard Davies's baldly staged production confirm that Mr Brook has scooped Stratford's acting pool.

Another fabled lady: Celestina, the classic raddled bawd of the Spanish fifteenth-century classic, bravely revived at the Sheffield Crucible. Bravely because Fernando de Rojas's piece, in a jewelled Elizabethan translation, is not a play but a series of dialogues. Only in the last act, after hours have passed, does Charles Lewsen's production begin to stand on its own feet, aided by a heroine (Pamela Stephenson) who passes from virgin to voluptuary in record time. Bill Stewart's bravo is also a help; Antonia Pemberton's Celestina, though serviceable, comes far short of legend.

Baby business

MARY and Joe are a young married couple who are trying to conceive a baby; their next-door neighbours, a lesbian couple named Georgina and Sandy, want a child as well. Georgina resorts to AID (Artificial Insemination by Donor) after a failed pregnancy by more natural means; while Mary decides that she doesn't really want a baby after all.

I can't help wondering what Gloria Graham and her mum, two of the first-night audience at Michelene Wandor's AID Thy Neighbour (Theatre at New End), made of it all. Ms Wandor's play for Women's Aid sets out (1) to show us how nice lesbians really are (indeed they're just like those two nice young women next door) and (2) to argue for lesbian motherhood.

Ms Wandor does all right with her first premise, but falls down rather in her second, where she bases her argument, in a way Mary Whitehouse wouldn't dream of doing, on the wickedness of the other side. This is incarnated in the person of a woman journalist attempting to write a series (code term: Operation Dyke) criticising AID for lesbians.

The journalist arrives for supper at their house equipped with a hidden tape recorder. Georgina and Sandy, apprised of her mission, have armed themselves in turn with a camera, and they use the photographs of the drunken Geraldine ("just call me Gerald") to blackmail her out of writing her series. Although the supper scene - with each party hiding her clandestine instrument and the couple adopting exaggeratedly masculine and feminine roles - reaches a classic moment of farce, the blackmailing is a rather unhappy argument for Ms Wandor's cause. Sara Bowes makes a sympathetically confused lesbian-baiter.
