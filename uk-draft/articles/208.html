Law student, singer, dissident

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Chichester

NOBODY would describe Paul Robeson (Her Majesty's) as a play: nobody, that is, except the author, Phillip Hayes Dean. Or maybe it was just the programme's fault.

Anyway what Mr Dean has to offer is a monologue in which James Earl Jones takes us from Robeson's days as a student at Rutgers almost up to his death. No other actors appear and Mr Dean perforce makes much use of the imaginary conversation; it is a relief when Mr Jones is allowed recourse to simple reminiscence or straightforward oratory.

With him on stage is an accompanist, the superbly dapper Burt Wallace. When Mr Jones's Robeson walks offstage after refusing to sing for a segregated audience in Kansas City, Mr Wallace stalks after him with magnificent hauteur. He looks demonic, so it is perhaps appropriate that when a second voice is at last admitted he should provide it in the collective role of Robeson's adversaries at the House Un-American Activities Committee. This is the second time that this hearing has been re-enacted on the London stage; the first was last year's HUAC documentary 'Are You Now or Have You Ever Been?'

The instructiveness of the evening overrides its weaknesses. Most of us will have two or three isolated images of Robeson in our minds -- the law student, the singer of 'Ol' Man River,' the dissident banned from foreign travel -- surrounded by large chronological blanks. This show enables us to fit them together, and to watch the development of a man's awareness.

At Rutgers he defends himself against racial prejudice with all the strength of his body, his voice and his wits. He becomes a football hero; proving to the whites that they cannot do without him. Later, having discovered his vocation as a performer almost by accident, he becomes a culture hero on a similar basis. In New York, at the time of the abortive 'Harlem Renaissance,' he begins to relate his own struggles to those of all black Americans. He comes to London with 'Show Boat,' remains there for most of the thirties, and his political horizons begin to widen further. Messrs Jones and Dean are excellent at balancing innocence and experience.

Ideological confidence usually entails selective blindness; though he had enormous provocation, Robeson was guilty of doublethink over the Soviet invasion of Hungary. The format of this show prevents it from examining this point; more seriously it denies us much of an insight into Robeson's private life. At one point he accuses his wife of not understanding him; we never, of course, get to hear her reply.

In some ways the brief glimpse we got of him in 'Are You Now' was more revealing than this full-length performance, perhaps because he was played there by a singer, Thomas Baptiste, whose delivery convinced us that, however considerable his other achievements, the source of Robeson's charisma was his voice: not just its power but its dignity. It made him impossible to ignore or, I would guess, to argue with.

Mr Jones gets the right startling note within seconds of opening his mouth, but he cannot keep it up all night; he and Mr Wallace do a charming relaxed duet of 'Nobody Knows You When You're Down and Out,' but for the most part Robeson's singing has to be taken for granted. At the preview I saw, Mr Jones was still feeling his way and the show's rhythms often went awry; but he is clearly a masterly actor, alert to every nuance of humour, and with regal authority. He, like Robeson, could withstand a more searching examination than Mr Dean's and still emerge intact.

Some of my most cherished Feydeau memories do not concern slamming doors or bouncing beds. They are sudden cynical moments of truth: the confession of the Lothario in 'A Flea in Her Ear,' that "I know what's expected of me, but I don't know how to make it new"; or the lament in 'Hotel Paradiso' of the would-be adulterer suddenly stricken by indigestion: "This would happen when my wife's not here to help."

There are no such cris du coeur in Look After Lulu (Chichester); more importantly, there is no solid ground from which they could spring. The keynote of Noel Coward's version of 'Occupe-toi d'Amelie' is struck at the end of Act Two. What, somebody asks, is going on here? Geraldine McEwan as Lulu responds with a brief and accurate resume of the story so far. "This," says her interlocutor, "is becoming farcical." Curtain. In other words we get a scornful commentary on the idea of French farce; and who in echt Feydeau would be able to come up with a synopsis?

Lulu is a cocotte, temporarily abandoned by her lover to the care of a friend. When her old protector finds her in bed with her new one he takes signal revenge; he persuades them into a mock-marriage which turns out to be genuine. The marriage scene itself, performed by an authentic magistrate whom everyone believes to be a fraud, is a set-piece of considerable merit; this scene of orchestrated disbelief is the only credible thing in the evening.

Of course with two comic masters at war some sparks fly. The actors are required to play in two styles at once. Some decline, like Fenella Fielding who performs in her own manner, and Kenneth Haigh who hardly performs at all. Geraldine McEwan reconciles them best; ironically enough the virtuous indignation she deployed so marvellously as the bourgeoisie of 'A Flea in Her Ear' also suits Coward's Lulu, for whom sex is just a matter of double-entendres. I doubt if she has much in common with Feydeau's Amelie, who would be businesslike and half the age. Still, Miss McEwan looks beautiful and sounds funny. Clive Francis shines too as the unwilling husband; his performance is a model of economy (i.e., he is in a state of permanent slump). Nigel Stock gets closer than anyone to true Parisian determination, bolstered as it happens, by hilarious Dutch vowels. I wish Chichester had mounted a real translation; it's one thing to revive West End successes, but 'Lulu' (which dates, like 'The Aspern Papers,' from 1959) was a West End flop. 'Lulu' is high comic art compared to Philip Magdalen's Boo Hoo (Open Space). Here are a trio of Miami hookers, each forcing the actress concerned on to the aggressive-defensive. Georgina Hale affects a belligerent squeal, Estelle Kohler a Monroevian pout and Janet Suzman -- the most successful of the three -- a world-weary brashness. She could probably sail through the entire Katharine Hepburn repertoire.

While this trio compare incomes, plot to kill their husbands, disclose unexpected relationships or drop references to the state of the Nation (desperate jabs in the direction of satire), we look at Timian Alsaker's set, which incorporates a Buddha that opens out into a cocktail cabinet and a table on three stockinged legs: the evening's sum total of wit.
