Prince(ss) of Denmark

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN at the new Half Moon.

THE new Half Moon Theatre, in the Mile End Road, promises to be as friendly a showplace as its predecessor - still operative - at Aldgate.

How can a critic feel hostile towards a playhouse which permits him to perch on its set half an hour before curtain rise, tucking into chicken sandwiches and cheesecake from the delicatessen down the road?

I should explain about that set. The opening production is Hamlet and it is mounted in what the National Theatre describes as promenade-fashion. That is, a series of rostra have been erected round the hall and on these the action takes place.

One construction represents the battlements, another the throne-room. The throne itself is a huge and fearsome piece of Meccano sculpture, with a skeletal 'royal' figure surmounting it. Vacant pieces of scenery are all the audience have to sit on: for the most part they mill around. Hamlet may seem rather a long play for this sort of caper, but the director, Robert Walker, has cut it severely; we begin with the second scene, and end with 'the rest is silence.'

Mr Walker perhaps underrates human laziness when he says that his scenic method offers the audience a choice of vantage-point for each scene; our view is as likely to depend as much on chance as choice. But the strangeness and the proximity - for the new Half Moon is a small, square room - make the play fresher than any amount of gimmicking up the text itself could achieve. The fundamental relation between actors and audience can never be altered as long as the latter pay for their tickets, but it is possible to play fruitful tricks with it.

Mr Walker's treatment of the play is actually rather conservative: in the attempts to saddle Hamlet with an Oedipus complex, positively old-fashioned. He concentrates on the corruption of the court (all white-faced, as is now the custom in productions of lesser Jacobean tragedies), and particularly on its propensity for eavesdropping - here shared, of course, by the audience. Polonius, in a clever stylised performance by Robin Hooper, becomes almost the leading figure of the first half, always peeping behind the nearest arras and eventually slain (with strict poetic justice) by an assailant who doesn't bother to look. Claudius is a neurotic, fear-ridden tyrant, and Gertrude a high-heeled, bare-bosomed belle, who can hardly forbear laughing at the idea that her precious son (much given to crawling up her skirts) might have fallen for so ordinary a girl as Ophelia.

The closet-scene accordingly takes on peculiar intensity; the intimacy achieved by Hamlet and Gertrude can only be described as sisterly. Which brings me to Frances de la Tour in the title-role. Mr Walker's decision to make this a walk-around Hamlet has deflected me from considering it as a female Hamlet. It works like that in performance, too; the vigour and intelligence applied to the play as a whole make consideration of the prince a secondary matter.

Much of the time he is a neutral figure, as were the women in the National's all-male As You Like It. But there neutrality was a deliberate convention; here, with all the other male roles played by men, it seems an accident. At one unguarded moment, bemused by both a scene-change and a cut, I found myself thinking of Miss de la Tour as Ophelia; I could never forget she was an actress. But on this occasion frailty's name is Hamlet. The actress has a biting wit, but her greatest successes are in the area of vulnerability: the 'rogue and peasant slave' speech is memorably trapped. But she only proves that there is a feminine principle and that it is far from being the whole of Hamlet.

Vivre la difference, I say, and roll on Miss de la Tour's Hedda Gabler (as opposed to, say, Tom Courtenay's). For, considered purely in terms of the actress's own development, this performance is a breakthrough, achieving a vocal and emotional range beyond anything she has shown us before. The evening makes anyway a rousing beginning for a new theatre; and when the cast applauded us, Continental-style, the gesture for once seemed genuine and even, may I say, merited.

Roger Hall's Middle Age Spread (Lyric) looks at first glance like a foray into Ayckbourn territory. Its main scene is a dinner party, interrupted by flashbacks showing us the previous relationships of the diners. But technical juggling is not the point, and where Mr Ayckbourn's tensions are largely unspoken, Mr Hall's are overt.

The play aligns more with the Peter Nichols school of menopausal comedy. Colin the protagonist, is a schoolmaster recently promoted to Head; finding his marriage intellectually and sexually stultifying, he has his first-ever affair with a rather idealised junior colleague, egged on by his cynically philandering best friend.

The three involved couples swap worried aphorisms (and if the audience applauded some of the more reactionary offerings, I am charitable enough to think that the laugh was on them) while waiting for the situation to explode. When it does, the size of the bang seems disproportionate to the length of the fuse; the play is not terribly shapely. But, moment to moment, it offers far more than most boulevard pieces; both its jokes and its misgivings are truthful.

Some of the performances are, like the piece, victims of a double standard. A man with the sharp-edged physical funniness and impeccable verbal timing of Richard Briers's Colin is wasted as a teacher; he should have been an actor. But in outbursts Mr Briers is quite unassailable, as is Paul Eddington in what amounts to a continuous acrid drunk-scene. There is also a splendidly needled piece of work from Sheila Grant, who deserves (but doesn't get) a flashback of her own.

Julia Kearsley's Wednesday (Bush) also strays into Nichols territory. It is the comedy of a widow with a retarded 15-year-old son, whom she both loves and resents, and a bitterly aggressive teenage daughter whom she mostly just resents. The man who comes to dinner this time is an old friend who once saved the boy's life at the expense of his own son's. Miss Kearsley puts her quartet through its paces with considerable skill - her dialogue has a rare conviction - though she has exhausted her possible permutations half an hour before closing-time. If Mr Nichols's Joe Egg suffered from abandoning its central theme, Miss Kearsley rubs our noses in hers.

Jean Boht's performance of the mother, bald beneath a glamorous wig, is a masterpiece of tact, playing on our feelings without lacerating them. David Bamber brings off the necessary tour de force as her son, Gil Brailey scratches with style, and David Hargreaves is a model of well-meaning Northern bemusement, like Michael Parkinson trapped in a documentary on approved schools. Grant Hicks supplies another of his stunningly believable sets; the kind that suggests somebody has gone homeless to provide it. In our smallest theatres, naturalism lives.
