Plugging the gap

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Pirandello's 'Six Characters'

WE ARE now at the half-way point of the year: a year so far distinguished by the absence of a single new play able to inspire real enthusiasm.

As usual, our theatres have partially concealed the vacuum by mounting a series of raids, often imaginative, on the past. Eliot, Maugham, Molnar and Schnitzler have been the top London playwrights of 1979. And now follows Pirandello, with a Greenwich revival of Six Characters in Search of an Author, a play apparently written to plug just such a gap. The Director, whom we find in rehearsal when we enter the theatre, complains that there is no good new stuff available and that he has had to fall back on reviving Pirandello's The Rules of the Game. (One of the author's little jokes, made even funnier by the fact that The Rules of the Game is a better play than Six Characters.) He is interrupted by the sextet of lost souls conceived and left unfinished by their unseen dramatist and now come to the playhouse in search of final realisation.

But here Greenwich's own director, Phil Young, has fallen foul of a difficult problem. The play was a bombshell at its first production in 1921, and a director naturally wishes to preserve as much of that explosiveness as possible. To that end he puts the play into modern dress and tries to pretend that the interrupted rehearsal is happening now.

But if he is going to get away with this, he needs to take more liberties than either Mr Young or his translator, John Linstrum, have permitted themselves. We now have obviously contemporary performers practising archaic rehearsal methods. They shrink from improvisation, blench at the thought of the heroine removing her dress, and never address one another by name. (The Director gives brusque instructions to stage management; they'd go on strike.)

It is a sad truth that nothing dates faster than experiment. A period production of Six Characters would allow us to pay our respects to it as a pioneering work, while acknowledging that its insights are now commonplace. (It would also ease the task of the actors playing actors, by making their roles characters as well. But would that make them more Pirandellian or less? No, that way madness, etc.)

When the drama closes in, the production improves considerably, though without ever stilling my recurrent doubts about the play. For, in the confrontation between the characters begging to be acted and the actors who try to oblige and immediately falsify everything, Pirandello, ingeniously but far from seamlessly, has jammed together three disparate notions. One is the inevitable gulf between the vision in an author's head and its expression by other minds and bodies on the stage. A second (adumbrated by Keats a century earlier) is the haunting stasis of art; its property of freezing a single word, gesture or moment forever. And linked to this is the despair of the man who finds himself judged for a single atypical action.

This is the plight of the Father, who entered a brothel as the unknowing client of his own Stepdaughter. For her, he will always be an exploiter and a seducer. And the emotional capital Pirandello makes of this has far more to do with their condition as people than as figures in an uncompleted play. The intensest moments, particularly well managed by Philip Stone as the Father despite some odd swooping movements, make the theatrical superstructure seem unnecessary and rather meretricious.

The Stepdaughter, forever striking vengeful or lascivious attitudes, is a tough assignment for a British actress, and Pauline Moran is liable to slide from brilliance to banality within a couple of lines. Hers is certainly a brave performance; more tactful direction might have stopped it seeming foolhardy.

Theatricality again runs amok in The Three Musketeers (Royal Exchange, Manchester), which aims at chaos and misses. The ambience of this theatre, and of Braham Murray's visually splendid production, is far too civilised to permit of the fun and games (actors stepping out of character and characters out of narrative) that Ken Hill used to bring engagingly off in the tattered days of Theatre Workshop.

However, there is one superb set-piece: a quick replay of the story so far, carried out half-way through Act One for the benefit of latecomers. Robert Lindsay is an engaging D'Artagnan; Derek Griffiths is in danger of forfeiting his status as the funniest clown in the land, and has also written some thoroughly undistinguished songs. I wish someone would realise that there is no need to send up Dumas; he was a very witty writer who furnished his musketeers with some of the best cross-talk in standard literature.

Barry Collins's Toads (Nottingham Playhouse) offers some amazing scenes: a couple of man-size copulating frogs, a lizard on a trapeze, some attractive nudes. These are intended to illustrate the life and thoughts of Paul Kammerer, Lamarckian biologist, and it is unfortunate that they are by far the liveliest things in the evening.

The text provides them with no fitting springboard. It consists of a long debate between Kammerer, who seems to believe in the benevolent operations of chance, and a police inspector who by contrast has the gift of pessimistic prophecy and has arrived to stop him committing suicide. They go on endlessly, two mouthpieces uncharacterised. The final blow in Geoffrey Reeves's production is dealt by the unchanging set, which looks like an old-fashioned Shakespearean arrangement of steps and rostra (mostly unexploited) hastily covered by a huge white dust-sheet.

There were two revelations for me in the Amnesty International gala, The Secret Policeman's Ball (Her Majesty's). One was Pete Townshend who, singing and playing solo guitar, made Pinball Wizard sound a better song than ever before; the other was Rowan Atkinson, whom I unwisely scorned at Hampstead last year, but whose bored sadistic schoolmaster was the most polished turn of the evening.

Peter Cook's E. L. Wisty, still the finest of post-war comic creations, wandered on to find his customary bench occupied by John Cleese; and a battle of equals commenced. Mr Cleese is the only comedian who, when baulked of his desires (cheese, in this case), can say to his adversary "I'm going to kill you" and mean it.
