Mabel's back in town

ROBERT CUSHMAN meets a great supper-club singer.

MABEL MERCER, who is to popular singing what Elisabeth Schwarzkopf is to lieder, is in London until the end of the month, singing in a newly opened room at the Playboy Club. I should warn or reassure you though that there is no singer in the world who sounds less a vocalising bunny than Miss Mercer.

She is, to begin with, one of those comfortable people (Fred Astaire is another) who, having been born in 1900, are as old as the century. Then, too, she is richly cosmopolitan; her career gathered momentum in Paris, peaked and consolidated in New York, but she was born and grew up in England. (Her father, though, was black American.) North-country vowels come through in her conversation if not in her singing. They have been reasserting themselves, she says, since she has been back in Britain.

This is the first time she has been here since 1938. Just before that she went to New York for a short engagement and found that the war prevented her return to Europe. So a brief trip was extended almost to infinity. Ironically the lady from the old world became the paragon and the prototype of American supper-club singers.

The supper-club was described by Whitney Balliett, jazz critic of the New Yorker, as "that royal relative of the night-club" and ideally should combine elegance and intimacy; the second is indispensable. It has never taken root here, and even in New York it came close to dying out in the sixties. Recently there has been a small revival, and the New York entertainment listings are themselves again.

Show-songs (generally the unhackneyed ones) are the essence of their repertoire; a singer usually begins by plunging through the catalogues, then as years pass refines the list down to the songs which -- through her choice or that of her public -- have accumulated around her and will not be dislodged. The programme I heard Mabel sing at the Playboy on Tuesday was almost identical to one I heard her offer in a plush New York hotel two years ago -- including Porter, Coward, Rodgers and Hart. Many of the songs she had recorded during a spate of LP activity in the fifties. They still sound fresh; her tone and her bearing acknowledge them to be classics, but no performance is jaded.

She calls them "songs of sentiment," and the sentiments tend to be concern and regret rather than passion -- although she is able to infuse passion into them. Her version of "Send in the Clowns" almost sums up her art; the recurring title-phrase is treated differently each time it comes round; it begins nonchalant and ends up fierce. She also insists, more firmly than any other singer, on this song as a waltz: a rhythm to which she is peculiarly sensitive. She reiterates that she is not a jazz singer; certainly she has none of the trappings, but her sense of time and of beat, though never thundered forth, is infallible.

A sleeve-note once described her as a "one-woman faculty" for American popular singing. She herself is sceptical. "I've often wondered about this. All performers are very aware of other performers; we all learn from each other. I used to sing songs that weren't popular -- unusual songs -- and maybe that was the attraction."

We talked on Monday -- the day of her opening -- with the room still apparently being constructed around her, our conversation punctuated by the arrival of bouquets. The message on one of them read, "Love and welcome back from your old fan of Tony's days -- John Gielgud." Tony's was her first New York booking, and she stayed there for nine years.

Her next engagements were for seven and three years respectively. You knew where to find her, and this permanence she regards as the most important ingredient of her success. "I was a sort of link, generation to generation. I sneaked into everybody's family. It's a warm feeling."

People come and request songs with memories, and "when your voice cracks up on you, it doesn't matter."

The voice has not in fact cracked as much as some reports (including her own) would have it; yes, she talks portions of her songs, but she sings more, and still with power. Uniquely among singers she does not stand at a mike, jump around or even perch on a stool; she sits on a gilt chair. "I got to sitting down all the time when I found it made people more at ease." It also gives her great control; her hands, too, sing.

Her accompanist is a mellifluous gentlemanly pianist named Jimmy Lyon; they have worked together for seven years. (More permanence; one "piano-player" served her for 16 years.) Her earlier fans must have told their grandchildren; her audiences are surprisingly young. Every night someone at the Playboy is sure to call out "welcome back"; I wish to be associated with that sentiment.
