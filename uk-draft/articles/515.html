National's continental drifter

Robert Cushman · Sunday Telegraph · The Arts II · 1990-12-09

AFTER Robert Lepage's show Polygraph, at the Almeida Theatre in 1989, Richard Eyre called it "the most exciting piece of theatre I have seen all year". Now Eyre has managed to lure Lepage to the National with Tectonic Plates (see review), an epic play about continental drift which also contains some very funny scenes set in restaurants.

Still in his early 30s, Lepage is one of two Canadians in the performing arts attracting worldwide attention; the other is the film director Denys Arcand (in whose Jesus of Montreal Lepage was an actor). Both are French-Canadians; both, that is, are part of a culture and society that seems in the process of cutting its links with the rest of Canada.

Quebec theatre attracted attention some years ago with the aggressive plays of Michael Tremblay (Lepage calls it "Tremblay's outburst"), and subsidy began to pour into the province's theatre, most of it going into lavish productions of Broadway-style musicals.

"We thought," says Lepage diplomatically, "that that was wrong." He looked towards other influences: "Dance theatre... visual theatre... it helped Francophone theatre to express itself without language."

In practice Lepage himself has never spurned language, on or off stage. (Some of the more intimate scenes that I saw in the Quebec Tectonic Plates might have been directed by Mike Leigh.) Lepage warily accepts the definition of his work as "performance art" - "though not," he qualifies, "in the Laurie Anderson sense" - but it has few of the more forbidding aspects of that genre. It welcomes the audience in.

Eyre wants Lepage to direct a play - "a European classic" - for the National. On the basis of what I have heard of Lepage's productions of Galileo and A Midsummer Night's Dream and what I have seen myself, it sounds a great idea. This year, working in conjunction with a Shakespeare troupe from Saskatoon, he directed a literally bi-lingual production of Romeo and Juliet set in a town - the Capulets spoke French and the Montagues English - that begged a few questions, but was both poignant and thrilling.

Two months ago he staged Durrenmatt's The Visit, exploiting the play's bizarre imagery to the limit. The two castrati, who are the playthings of the vengeful Old Lady, were discovered trussed up in a giant suitcase; the unemployed citizenry sat around the railway station glued to the kind of tiny TV monitors you see in airports; about as potent an image of depression, spiritual and economic, as could be imagined. The play's glib parable was sharpened but not distorted, its human action moving as well as frightening.

This was Lepage's first production at his new home base, the National Arts Centre in Ottawa, where he heads a Francophone drama unit. It seems a good place for him, since, apart from being Canada's capital, Ottawa is on the cusp of the country's two major cultures. It is a kind of no man's land, and ironically Lepage feels he may be more necessary there than in his native province.

"As Quebec gets more independent culturally, they don't need me." He begins to see himself more as a world citizen. Certainly he is in international demand. He ranks with the hottest: "Interviewers used to compare me with Peter Brook. Now they say I remind them of David Lynch."

He manages to be bold without pretension, playful without facetiousness. The movement of oceans and land masses in Tectonic Plates corresponds to "separation, meeting and drifting of people - or society or a culture."

It is evidence of his charm and authority that he can persuasively make a claim like that within moments of describing himself as "an actor, a group guy, a trouper: more a coach than a director". In fact, he is suspicious of conventionally scripted plays (and hopes gradually to wean his new Ottawa audience away from them) because, he says, they force him into the traditional theatrical hierarchy: below the author, above the cast.

It's all been said before: and nobody really believes that Lepage, even if he lets his actors write most of his experimental pieces themselves, really functions as no more than a catalyst. The Group ethic can be oversold. But whatever it is he does, it has rarely been done so winningly.
