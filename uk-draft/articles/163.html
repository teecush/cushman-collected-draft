After all, it's not Gomorrah

ROBERT CUSHMAN meets the author of the latest Broadway musical to hit London.

THERE is a piece of musical comedy lore which states that the man at the piano surrounded by adoring beauties is the composer; the man biting his finger-nails, neglected in the corner of the room, is the lyricist. Where this leaves the librettist (or "book-writer") I'm not sure. Maybe he never gets invited to any parties.

The book of a musical is traditionally the element of the show that nobody notices. Nobody, that is, except the critics, who can usually be relied on to insult it. Michael Stewart, who wrote the book of I Love My Wife, a Broadway success which opens at the Prince of Wales this week, claims to have a scrapbook full of phrases like "lacks wit" and "despite a weak book by." He looks well enough, since the shows that won him these accolades include Hello Dolly and Bye Bye Birdie; he can afford to.

Recently, anyway, his cuttings-book has brightened; for I Love My Wife he wrote, for the first time, the lyrics as well as the script, and nobody has suggested that they are either weak or witless. Clive Barnes (late of the New York Times) called them "literate," which surprised Mr Stewart; not that he didn't agree, he was just used to Mr Barnes coming out and saying such things.

He spends much of his time in France, and a couple of years ago in Paris he saw there a comedy, by Luis Rego, on the subject of dutifully attempted wife-swapping. He liked the play, though he was less keen on its half-heartedly interpolated songs.

To turn the show into a proper musical, he bought the rights, and refused to relinquish them to a Broadway producer until he was signed to write the words: all the words. For composer he secured Cy Coleman, whose previous scores include Little Me and Sweet Charity and whose individual songs (like Witchcraft and The Best is Yet to Come) are the best recently written in the rhythmic propulsive Gershwin tradition. Coleman was working on another assignment (he still is) and Stewart persuaded him to take on this one by promising that it would only, as in the Paris version, involve half a dozen songs. It grew.

For all that it remains a small show: a cast of four, and a band of the same size, who create a precedent by taking on acting roles as well. This, Stewart cheerfully admits, is the only really experimental thing in the show, though it proved innovative enough to cause a distinct flurry even outside the actors' and musicians' unions. The show has come to London in the time-honoured commercial game, invoking the spirit of Annie. Despite their differing approaches, they are both reassuring shows. The story of I Love My Wife has been moved from France to Trenton, New Jersey, the protagonists rechristened Wally, Monica, Cleo and Alvin.

Alvin it is who states the impulse that moves them all: the desire to "cut loose and have a ball / With orgies relatively small / Because it's Trenton, after all / And not Gomorrah." Though the New York critics liked the show, and despite its immediate success, Stewart found a "guilty feeling" about these lyrics. It had been maybe 17 years, he said, happily, sharing whatever musical honours were to be had with the large-scale Annie.

It is Stewart's own, Seeing Rego's play in Paris he was struck by an "astonishing simple truth" which he could relate to his own homeland. "In the US we're so bombarded by the media with word of the permissive society. Even I have sat home feeling that things must be going on in every apartment in the building except my own."

Like Alvin, he finds two kinds of guilt (puritan and permissive) and they clash. "I think that's the secret of it. Most people are content to fantasise, then come up with some kind of rationalisation for the entire family. There's an innocence about it."

Richard Beckinsale, currently occupied with a similar theme but different tone of Funny Peculiar; Ben Cross, late of the RSC; and Michael Williams and Polly Adams are the London cast. The production is by Gene Saks, the choreography by Onna White.

Stewart thinks that the difficulties of casting a musical in London are overrated. In Paris they are insuperable. But he likes the French theatre. "It's like slipping back in time."

Soft-spoken, sandy haired and 47, Stewart sees the success of I Love My Wife as the end of seven lean years, following on the seven fat ones of Birdie and Dolly. Between Dolly and this one he has had only two Broadway shows, both flops. Now the telephone is ringing again and "the offers are up to one a week again--most of them for impossible shows."

He knows that, whatever the critics say, a musical cannot work without a good script. "Without what the book says--and God knows, it's hardly profound--I Love My Wife would just be a collection of 14 bright numbers; as an adapter I've never betrayed what the original author did it all for."
