Norman's conquests

IF people really laugh like drains, two London theatres must currently resemble sewers. One is the Aldwych, on the occasions when Tom Stoppard's Travesties is playing; the other is Greenwich which, like the Aldwych, is operating a repertoire system. However, since the repertoire consists entirely of Alan Ayckbourn's trilogy The Norman Conquests, you may be assured of a guffaw any night on which you can gain admittance.

I fear that last week I under-emphasised the sheer funniness of Mr Stoppard's play. It is--unless you have a deaf spot for puns and conceits--immense.

Mr Ayckbourn, too, deals in conceits (for how else would you describe the three-fold reiteration of the events of a single weekend). Norman is a man--or at any rate an Assistant Librarian--and his conquests sexual. His attempted conquests, I should say. Though he lays siege during the trilogy to all three of the women in the cast, only one of them actually capitulates, and that his own wife. His avowed intention is to seduce his sister-in-law. He is, however, thwarted by his other sister-in-law, a monitress. It was always a question whether Mr Ayckbourn could convincingly complete his scheme by contriving a plausible encounter between Norman and this last lady. He begged it, until the final play, Round and Round the Garden, and then he blew it.

Though it has always been difficult to fault Tom Courtenay's performance of this Norman, it has never been possible quite to believe in it. In the earlier plays he was a manic presence who promised to improve, and even to become comprehensible, on closer acquaintance.

This last and most schematic of the three drags him firmly but disappointingly into the centre. He propositions the ladies, and is rejected by them, more for the sake of his author's pattern than from internal logic.

Towards the close, Round and Round the Garden takes on the forced formality that for me maimed the finale of Mr Ayckbourn's Absurd Person Singular; while earlier the action is prolonged (and Michael Gambon's delightfully stolid vet distorted) beyond reason by the introduction of a contrived misunderstanding.

The level of incidental merriment remains intelligently high, and many lines still bruise the bone. The play offers pleasing variations on the basic form, without ever breaking faith with it. Though it scrupulously confines itself to the same weekend as its predecessors, it begins a little earlier in the day. We are thus slyly afforded an exposition, wherein Mr Ayckbourn may tell us things we already know and are only too delighted to hear again. And though, since the plot is unalterable, Norman's wife is still condemned to exist offstage until halfway through the evening, on this occasion her activities upon arrival allow the actress, Penelope Wilton, to come within reach of her acerbic own. Eric Thompson's direction is excellent, as it has been throughout; and the whole enterprise will shortly transfer, all three of it, to the West End.

Robert Cushman
