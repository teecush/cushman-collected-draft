In love with the National

ROBERT CUSHMAN talks to Julia McKenzie, who stars in 'Schweyk in the Second World War,' opening at the Olivier this week.

NOBODY doubts that Julia McKenzie is something special. Without her the National Theatre production of 'Guys and Dolls,' in which she plays Miss Adelaide, would be an entirely different proposition.

The director, Richard Eyre, admits that he was at first reluctant to consider her for the role: "Sheer perversity on my part, resisting the idea of the most obvious person being the best." Once she had auditioned--at her own request--there were no more hesitations. Now Eyre directs her again, as Mrs Kopecka in Brecht's 'Schweyk in the Second World War.' He ranks her with Bill Paterson (who plays Schweyk) and Jonathan Pryce: actors who are not satisfied with what could already be a remarkable performance. She says nice things about Eyre too.

"I've only done one Brecht before, so I keep saying, 'What about Brecht's style?'" Richard says, "Forget about the style, just do it." It's a very plain part, not showy like Adelaide: a rather dour, hard-faced innkeeper, who epitomises the Czech people in an occupied situation. The joy's gone out of her, she's dried up, a widow. What gives an extra dimension to her is the music." The play has songs composed by Hanns Eisler.

It's a long musical road from Julia McKenzie's first job, in a touring production of 'Rose Marie' in 1961. She had originally trained for opera, but discovered that it wasn't her voice or her world. She went to the Guildhall School of Music and Drama, though she "had to push them" to let her do the drama.

"'Rose Marie' happened because she was sent to audition for second understudy to the leading lady. At Coventry she had to go on and play the part, with no rehearsal. ('That young, you have the confidence.') The director said afterwards, 'You're going to make a wonderful comedienne'; prophetic, but, at the time, a bit dashing. 'I didn't really wake up till I was 30.'

She did rep, and in the 1960s was in a lot of West End musicals. Some good parts, too: though not many people noticed, since they were usually taken over from other people in the course of the run. She took over from Georgia Brown, for example, in 'Maggie May'; and got to play the blowsy pick-up in 'Promises, Promises,' a small part, but a plum one, and still one of her favourites.

She was also Patricia Routledge's replacement in the first of the Mermaid songwriter revues. That's the way people used to see her: as 'upper-class jaded ladies.' So in 'Cole,' the successor to 'Cowardy Custard,' she got the witty, world-weary Porter songs when she wanted to do the torch-ballads. She puts it down to her tilted nose.

"My face has worked against me. Now I'm growing into it, but I never used to get partners at dances; they all thought I was upper-class. Actually I'm the most suburban person you ever met in your life." (She comes from Enfield in Middlesex.)

She got a chance to play the suburban quality in the cricketing comedy, 'Outside Edge.' She was offered a more glamorous role, but held out for the dedicated player's long-suffering wife, "a cleaning maniac--me, if I hadn't come into this business. Actually it was a great emotional thing in my life to have a cleaner for my own house at all."

I don't know if 'Outside Edge' was a turning-point in Julia McKenzie's career, but it certainly changed my estimate of her. I had decided, after seeing her in Alan Ayckbourn's 'Ten Times Table' (doing what she admits to be 'the Penelope Keith part'), that she wasn't a comedienne. 'Outside Edge' revealed that she was, but that she was an emotional comedienne, one with warmth.

That she could wring your heart had already been proved in her third Mermaid anthology, 'Side by Side by Sondheim.' That was a turning-point; and her slow, dazed, hopeful rendition of 'Broadway Baby' has completely erased the brassy old trouper of that song's original conception. Every girl who auditions for a musical now sings 'Broadway Baby,' and they nearly all sing it like Julia McKenzie.

'Side by Side' took her to Broadway ('I couldn't let myself believe I was singing American material there; getting expenses like Monopoly money'). Between here and there she did the show, for 18 months and was never bored. 'It took me all the time to be equal to the material. Every number was an acting exercise.'

It set her up for her only whacking great big leading part to date, in the glistening spoof operetta 'On the Twentieth Century.' One thing that is not suburban about Julia McKenzie is her voice. It may be the best ever sported by a British musical-comedy lady, and 'Twentieth Century' used all of it. She played a prima donna with the heart of a guttersnipe, and in blending these elements gave the show a warmth to match the style it was granted at birth. You laughed with her.

And so to Adelaide in 'Guys and Dolls,' singing in difficult keys for the proper squawking effect. 'Adelaide's my kind of part. She's suburban; that's why she worries so much what her mother thinks. I know lots of old-fashioned chorus-girls like her.'

It's easy to see why Julia McKenzie and the National love each other. She is the pro who could reassure the actors through a three-day technical ('I told them, "This always happens in a musical"'), while acting with as much honesty and skill as the most dedicated social realist. Eyre says she's a "terribly good touchstone of a production; she never does anything with conviction that she doesn't believe the right thing to do. And because she's so good, people listen to what she says and watch what she's doing."

As for her: "I love it here--I want to do it for the rest of my life. But I think I will have to leave. I get guilty. I think, this isn't acting if you're comfortable. You've got to be uncomfortable."
