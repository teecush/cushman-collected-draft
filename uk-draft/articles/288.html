Snobs of every class

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Chichester and London.

I HAVE to confess to some affection for The Last of Mrs Cheyney, the Frederick Lonsdale comedy from 1925 that has opened the season at Chichester.

It is refreshing to encounter a playwright so completely cynical about all levels of society.

'The snobbishness of the upper classes,' says a butler to a footman, 'is only exceeded by the snobbishness of the lower and middle.' That, like most of Lonsdale's attempted epigrams, has the disadvantage of predictability; having heard the set-up, you can anticipate the pay-off. But it suggests that playwrights who have no idea of the social system giving way can afford to be far more scathing about its constituents than those consciously working to destroy it. Basking in a sense of security, false or otherwise, Lonsdale gives a lot away.

Not all his characters are snobs; some are crooks pretending to be snobs. Mrs Cheyney herself is a jewel-thief; her domestics are actually her accomplices. She invites the rich and titled to her home so that she may be invited back and have an opportunity to despoil them. (The story-line has much in common with Ben Travers's 'Plunder.')

A couple of young peers fall for her; one she despises, the other she fancies. The favoured lord finds her out, and then antagonises her by demanding her favours as the price of his silence: 'Five years in Holloway,' she declares, 'wouldn't be as long as one night with you.' She gives the alarm, and a brief nobility contest develops between them. The last act hinges on the discovery that her other suitor has written her a compromising letter; it suddenly becomes the interest of the assembled guests to buy Mrs Cheyney off rather than to prosecute her. This leads to some ringingly bitchy exchanges across the communal breakfast-table.

There are some remarkable technical gaffs. The fatal letter is never mentioned during the earlier scenes, so the whole last act has the air of a desperate if effective improvisation. The opening dialogue consists almost entirely of long hostile descriptions of those characters who have yet to enter; what grates is not the artificiality of this convention, but its impracticability; there is no way of retaining all this information, so when the nobs troop on we forget about individual characteristics and decide to regard them merely as a set of unpleasant dummies. It's enjoyable to watch Lonsdale playing Shavian games with the identical proclivities of the propertied and criminal classes, and to wonder how long he will be able to fend off the inevitable sentimental resolution. It gets him in the end, but he puts up an encouraging fight.

If the style is sub-Wilde (with the difference that it actually mentions sex) and the philosophy sub-Shaw, the plotting is frequently sub-Pinero. Reconciling these diverse styles would not be impossible but, although Patrick Lau's production is very well staged, his cast lacks gloss, or maybe simply cheek. Joan Collins is a suitably amused adventuress, but she shirks Mrs Cheyney's more melodramatic flourishes. So the play is less nonsensically entertaining than it might be. Simon Williams, though he has an air, ignores the frivolous suggestions of depth that are written into his role. Elspeth March times one line majestically, but the only actor really in control is Benjamin Whitrow, half-Raffles, half-Jeeves.

John Napier's timbered set for The Merry Wives of Windsor is snugger at the Aldwych than it was in Stratford and by that much more effective. The whole of this Trevor Nunn-John Caird production has gained in pleasantness; not that pleasantness is what it lacked in the first place. What is missing is drive.

At Stratford I was inclined to blame this on under-casting; in London, with everyone except the wives (one too young, the other too posh) much nearer to snuff, the fault seems, rather, that too many quietly good comic ideas--more of them verbal than visual--have been allowed to accumulate. This does very well for the opening, which is pure scene-setting and which establishes, for example, Patrick Godfrey's tetchily vivacious Shallow (far more efficient than his namesake in 'Henry IV') against Bob Peck's Page, the personification of solid bourgeois pipemanship.

Into this ambience John Woodvine's Falstaff waddles as another gentleman cracksman. His is a juicier performance than it was; there has been a similar ripening in Ben Kingsley's Ford, whose disguise as Master Brook consists mainly of the vocal quirks of Ian Richardson. But both fall in with the general ambling; even Lila Kaye's Mistress Quickly seems grossly misnamed. Geoffrey Hutchings's French doctor is the best and most economical work; the closing scene in Windsor Park has great charm, and may be regarded as a furtherance of Mr Nunn's claim to be regarded as our best director of musicals--a position he has reached, quite remarkably, without ever having done one.

The final section of Towards a Nuclear Future (ICA) is a staging success in Pip Simmons's best tradition: a vivid picture--achieved mainly by a lowering of lights and a deadly economy of voice and movement--of a desolate world. Whether this was meant to have been caused by the rejection of nuclear energy, or by a disaster in its functioning, I was unclear.

The evening is devoted to an unusually even marshalling of pros and cons. The subject could hardly be more pressing, but vital issues have a way of collapsing in the theatre. Certainly they will not just sustain themselves, a fact of which nobody is usually more aware than Mr Simmons. But his treatment here--some haranguing, some singing, some more haranguing--is strangely conventional.

My generation will trace the form back to Peter Brook's 'US'; others may even think of the living newspaper of the Thirties. There is some challenging of the audience, though by now both sides know this to be merely a ritual, and the actors don't even act aggrieved at getting no response. Why should we join in their games, when we have not practised and they have?

The Devil Himself, which has just completed two weeks at the Lyric Studio, is a Wedekind compendium by Peter Barnes, and a great improvement on his previous attempt at the Theatre Upstairs. The advertised topic is sex, but the evening proved most gripping when the range logically widened to include financial exploitation and social inhibition. These are most powerfully expressed in an interspersed series of hunted stoical monologues.

The main business of the evening is song and here we are at a double distance from the originals, served to us in new musical settings and in a number called 'Capitulation,' fitted out with a sweet-and-sour tune by Carl Davis; otherwise there are too many rhymes on the order of 'miseree' and 'chastitee' to keep me happy in my seat. The cast--Tina Marian, Charles Keating, Dilys Laye and Alan Rickman--was excellent; so was the setting--cabaret with a hint of prison--by Liz da Costa.
