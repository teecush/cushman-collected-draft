Renovated relic

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the RSC's Peter Pan

THE traditional dilapidated productions have left Peter Pan looking like a relic. Now the Barbican takes it over, in a careful and occasionally brilliant production by John Caird and Trevor Nunn, and it still looks like a relic: a fascinating one, but appealing more as a guide to the author's psyche than as a play or adventure story in its own right.

What we get is a voyage around Peter Pan, a tour conducted by Barrie himself in the person (about twice as high as the original) of Stephen Moore in several varieties of Edwardian gentleman's relaxing or travelling dress. Mr Moore, the only actor I know able to convey nervousness and urbanity at the same time, narrates impeccably, his material drawn from Barrie's discursive stage directions and his novelisation of his own play.

The latter takes the story further than we are used to. We glimpse the Lost Boys grown up (into dizzyingly varied professions) and we are told that Wendy continued to do Peter's spring-cleaning on an annual basis (when he remembered to call for her, that is) until her own daughter was of an age to take over, followed by her grand-daughter; "and thus," says Mr Moore, putting on the agony, putting on the style, "it will go on, so long as children are gay and innocent and heartless." Pan will endure "until we wake up."

Is that to be wished? The last line could be the author's savage (and quite justified) comment on his own infantilism, or it could be a desperate prayer for it to continue. Probably it is both. This RSC production, by bothering to act what others have skimped (i.e. nearly everything) makes us more than usually aware of the cloying awfulness of the Darling household (words it is difficult to type with teeth clenched), simultaneously loathed and desired by its creator. Wendy, in the finest of Jane Carr's little madam performances, becomes the central character, trying desperately to seduce Peter even as she meticulously mothers him. As a fearful, fascinated view of womankind it is practically definitive, besides being extremely funny. Sheila Hancock gets more charm and grief out of Mrs Darling than previous actresses can have thought possible.

But the middle episodes are diminished: the fact of the children's escaping to Neverland is more important than what happens to them there. John Napier's revolving design transforms the place, as stipulated, into a closely packed adventure playground, though the components are of unequal merit (pirate ship, yes; lagoon, no). The flying is thrillingly done, up to the full height of the monstrous stage; though if ever a play cried out to be turned into a musical it is this one at the moment of lift-off. All forms of animal life are enthusiastically and skilfully represented, but the humans are disappointing. One lost boy (Anthony O'Donnell's Tootles) and one pirate (George Raistrick's Smee) stand out but the others are anonymous. And the central duel of Pan and Hook turns out not to be central at all.

We have been told for years that having Peter played by a boy instead of a girl would be a revelation. Maybe it would, but Miles Anderson here demonstrates that there is not much in Peter for a grown-up male actor to act. What there is he does well and, at the end, hauntingly. There is something of the Restoration fop in Hook but Joss Ackland, black-curled and almost a Pirate Queen, plays it, excellently, to the exclusion of everything else. Doubled with Mr Darling he is presumably meant to represent the full horror of adult malehood, but here there is no menace in him however much he waves his implement. He is not helped by the direction, which makes the pirate scenes strangely cluttered, or by the writing which in effect castrates him, helpless against all-powerful Pan. Barrie goes on and on about his buccaneers but as a pirate story the play is not much cop.

Something else it signally lacks, compared to other fantasy classics, is laughter. Most children's favourites--Lewis Carroll, Kenneth Grahame, A. A. Milne--satirise the adult world; this one wants to exclude it. Instead of wit Barrie offers whimsy camouflaged by very defensive irony; match the lost boys' imaginary supper against the Mad Hatter's tea-party. Miss Carr, by the way, would make the perfect Alice.

This variorum Peter Pan is a better adult entertainment than most others on offer.

Peter Whelan's Clay, in the Pit (appropriate match), is an exhaustive study of the states of mind of two married couples, former friends reunited, for whom the magic has gone. Mr Whelan cares enough about them to stimulate some excellent acting: Fred Pearson and Janet Dale jauntily or placidly normal before disintegrating, Jim Broadbent effortlessly and Gemma Jones effortfully odd before their suffering humanity comes through, but they don't half go round in circles.

Their muddle is set, in time-honoured fashion, against the painful clarity of old age, represented by Sylvia Coleridge in remarkable form, and John McAndrew, struggling boyfully against his exclusion from most of the action. When he returns it is to remind us of the nuclear threat, potent even in this play's rural setting. This is hopefully (I use the word, of course, advisedly) meant to set the domestic anguish in a wider context. Pull the other one.

More bracingly Steven Davis's A View of Kabul (Bush), whose title refers to a brand of dope, is another nail in the much-studded coffin of 1960s idealism. It is actually set in 1972, getting on for aftermath time, and juxtaposes two fashionable figureheads, supposedly allies: the rock musician and the socialist. The ideals of the former are shown to be bogus and those of the latter unworkable; the latter therefore loses out to the former who steals both of his women. Mr Davis works in a consciously artificial mode--characters walking on when needed rather than when motivated--so knowingly and symmetrically handled as to be a pleasure in itself: likewise his dialogue, which runs to elaborate double-entendre. Talk of the vulnerable heart, following an emotional bust-up, turns out to mean something quite different. The director, Richard Wilson, keeps his author's cool, and the acting is not to be quarrelled with.

Me, Myself and I (Orange Tree) is a musical by Alan Ayckbourn and Paul Todd. A parody of teenage romance, Fifties style. Mr Ayckbourn, who has never written worse, is the slave of his rhymes; as he usually writes short lines, the rhymes come round very often. It might work for light-headed comedy but he is trying to make character points.

Three of the characters are aspects of the same woman, though there are hardly enough characteristics for two of them. Her, herself and she are interviewed, as mother of the year, by two reporters and finally return to their husband. There is little wrong with the performance. Go and see Why Upstream instead.

The New Vic company have been touring with The Night They Raided Minsky's, caught by me at the plushly refurbished Theatre Royal, Bath. The film took us in and around the flavoursome world of 1920s burlesque. The stage version eschews around, and seems hung up on its own skill in evoking the old routines.

The girls have the right shape and the right squeal and there are authentic-sounding songs by Charles Strouse and Lee Adams (whom I credit since the programme neglects to) but it goes on for ever, varied only by endless replays of the same piece of plot, denunciations of everything by a prurient Puritan. In the interval he assails drinking patrons and takes their names. He took mine: What has he done with it?
