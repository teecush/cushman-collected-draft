Travelling nowhere

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London, York and Hornchurch.

IN HIS previous plays David Williamson has come before us as pretty hard-boiled. But of course hard shells are notorious harbingers of soft centres and in Travelling North (Lyric, Hammersmith) Mr Williamson exposes his.

His title is surely meant to possess more resonance than is actually summoned in the course of the play. Its surface meaning is strictly geographical: Frank, a widower in his seventies, and Frances, a widow 20 years younger, leave Melbourne and go to live on the coast of New South Wales. She has daughters who, selfishly, keep calling her back; Frank is afraid to let her go alone (afraid for himself, not her) and the distance, since he has a weak heart and an assortment of other ailments, nearly kills him. He too is selfish and Frances leaves him. But she comes back when, against all his humanist principles, he offers to marry her.

The wedding, or its aftermath, really does for him, and he expires on the front porch while listening to Mozart. Frances honours his last wishes by breaking open a magnum of champagne and, having fended off proposals from the local doctor and the man next door, decides to continue 'travelling north.' This, according to the map in the programme, would bring her to Brisbane; what else it would bring her to remains a mystery. You are presumably meant to think, approvingly, that she will keep on exploring life; if this were a musical, she would be singing an inspirational song about it. It is all very vague and, by that token, sentimental.

Mr Williamson does his best to be tough. He dwells on the awful snobbishness of Frances's family, and on Frank's crankiness, obstinacy and waning sexual powers. But he never writes a line that illuminates either character for us, and even his jokes are mechanical. ('Not another house in sight,' say the gratified couple arriving at their retreat. 'Anyone home?' inquires their neighbour, on the dot.)

There is nothing unbelievable about the play, and our attention is held, but at a moderate and unrewarding level. Frequent changes of scene elicit an unwontedly fidgety production from Michael Blakemore; and the supporting playing mostly lacks conviction. The two leading players, fortunately, have a grip; Peter Vaughan plays Frank's tetchy incredulity in considerable detail, and, as Frances, Rosemary Leach's shine is undimmed.

Travelling north myself, I came to York where the local cycle of Mystery Plays is again in session: a three-and-a-half-hour trip from the Creation to the Day of Judgment and, at that, considerably shorn. The medieval writers never had much time for the Old Testament--all they wanted was to see mankind getting itself thoroughly lost so that it could be redeemed in Part 2--and the present editors have less.

The plays are performed in the ruins of St Mary's Abbey, which helpfully arched pile shares the honours of the occasion with the arrival of darkness, an atmospheric blessing which falls, of course, on the just and unjust open-air productions alike.

Apart from Christopher Timothy's Christ, agreeable but not inspirational, the cast is composed entirely of local amateurs. They have been assembled in great numbers and the first stage-filling sight of them promises excitement. But it never materialises.

Patrick Garland's best production-touch comes at the Last Supper, where the disciples, having their feet washed, turn out to be ticklish. There is less of this detail in the writing than there is in the Wakefield Cycle, but more might have been imported. Mr Garland suddenly has his cast break out into Blake's 'Jerusalem,' which is contemporary neither with the plays themselves, the events they depict, nor the current production. The effect is half-hearted, and only flimsily related to the scene to which it is attached: the entry of Jesus into Jerusalem, on a real donkey. The shepherds also have genuine sheep, which may be seen grazing just off stage past a sign marked 'Actors Only.'

The Theatre Royal, York's Rep, has contributed to the Festival a Jacobean rarity, Webster's The Devil's Law-Case. The chief interest of this play lies in its central character, Romelio, who is the Websterian malcontent gone middle-class: not a poor scholar but a rich merchant, deeply contemptuous of the aristocracy and as capable as any of them of the behaviour expected of a good Renaissance Italian--debauching a nun, say; or persuading his sister to fake a pregnancy.

In The White Devil or The Duchess of Malfi, the most vital character is the commentator; here Webster turns him into the protagonist, but--in the interests of a kind of happy ending--cheats him of a tragic finish. This is frustrating; nothing but death should really stop Romelio; and the playwright has simply to fade him out. Elsewhere the comic format suits Webster's genius very well.

As important to the plot as Romelio himself is his mother: an elderly matron who falls for a young nobleman, she suspects her son of having killed him (he didn't, but not for lack of trying) and plans in revenge to disinherit him by declaring him illegitimate. This blackening of her own reputation suggests a reckless carnality which Webster obviously thought grotesque, and which Annette Kerr's discreet performance never communicates; however, since she too is dispatched to respectable happiness at the end, she may be right.

Damien Thomas gets a good measure of Romelio's cynicism, less of his bravado; I still wait to see this kind of role played with the right quicksilver touch. (No actor hung up on consistency is going to do justice to Webster.) The play, which incorporates much smoothly snarling satire on the venal lawyers, deserves attention at the highest level--a line touched here by Martin Johns's latticed chamber set.

Barrie Keeffe's She's So Modern (Queen's, Hornchurch) centres on a lady trying to cleanse television of everything save Come Dancing and the Eurovision Song contest. Mr Keeffe's reference point is not Mary Whitehouse (dismissed by his crusader as a sold-out softie), but Margaret Thatcher, and his real target, as suggested by the title and reinforced in song, is a state of mind and country that finds passionless toughness timeless and exciting.

Keeffe too is writing in a Jacobean comic vein, hindered I think by the fact that he doesn't really find his subject funny. It is not until the last 20 minutes of mayhem and straitjacketing that he comes near to making his point effectively. If he were really writing a satire on morality campaigning, his contention that the puritans are only randy hypocrites or plain crooks would be self-defeating; we know that their sincerity is what really frightens. After bigger game he is entitled to his angle, but he seldom makes it work for him.

It is as always a relief when Keeffe satirises his own side, in the shape of a chronically banned playwright. Otherwise his animus is liable to be self-defeating; one feels impelled to take sides against him. For the first time in my life--and aided by a straight-down-the-line performance from Charlotte Cornwell in stark contrast to some of the slap-happy approximations carrying on around her--I almost found the PM sympathetic.
