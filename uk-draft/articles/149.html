Li'l America

ROBERT CUSHMAN reports on a visit to the New York theatre.

THEATRICALLY, America this year is for the Americans. There are far fewer imports than in recent seasons. There's even an American critic on the New York Times.

Not only were there more, and more highly-touted, native plays than usual; they all seemed deeply sunk in their own mythology. The season's prize musical (there have been few contenders) was greeted as Americana incarnate.

Annie, the show in question, is based on the "Little Orphan Annie" cartoon-strip, and has been diagnosed as a rallying-point for reactionaries. Actually it's nothing that positive. Its hero may be a golden-hearted billionaire (God knows how such an angel made all that loot), but it balances him with a social-conscious Depression chorus. The original cartoon was rabidly anti-Roosevelt; he is mocked here, but affectionately. True he can only think up the New Deal after he has heard 13-year-old Annie sing a hopeful ditty entitled "Tomorrow," but a policy thus inspired obviously carries the authors' seal of approval.

This general benevolence, and the lack of a firm story, get a bit cloying; you keep waiting for the show to justify its ancestry, turn into Li'l Abner, and satirise something. But caricature is left to Dorothy Loudon as the old maid in charge of the orphans and hating them ("Do I hear happiness, in here?" she snarls after one juvenile chorus), and since she ends up not only funny but touching hers is obviously the right course. But despite the blandness, and the performing moppets (plus a dog) and a score that exhausts itself after the first three numbers, the show survives through a mixture of tidiness and confidence. It looks well, moves well, and seems to believe in itself.

The opposition musical, I Love My Wife -- announced, like Annie, for London -- has much better songs which never succeed in taking the stage. They neither further the action nor comment on it: not that this fable of incipient wife-swapping in New Jersey would bear much comment. It is an economical show: four in the cast, four in the band, all performing engagingly, with the musicians lending an occasional hand with the acting and doing it with charm.

Thwarted sex in suburbia is a small American legend which has outlasted larger ones. Vietnam for example. David Rabe's Streamers, which recently closed, seemed, at its premiere last year, dated and -- at least to an outsider -- inaccessible. It was the last instalment of a Vietnam trilogy; the middle section Stick and Bones may be seen in Britain soon, and the first, The Basic Training of Pavlo Hummel, has been revived on Broadway with Al Pacino in the lead. (Returning movie-stars have contributed greatly to Broadway's unprecedented prosperity this season.) Mr Pacino, looking like a cross between Dustin Hoffman and Tom Conti, slouches manfully, though with visible effort, as the lumpish private victimised by his platoon-mates, as Montgomery Clift used to be in fifties movies. The play makes the same point about military brutalisation over and over, and at the top of its voice.

An entertainment called For Colored Girls Who Have Considered Suicide/When the Rainbow is Enuf had to be taken on trust if at all. Coloured girls looking very far from suicidal crowded the theatre and broke up with delight on, for example, the word "sanctified." I could only register that the nine ladies on stage were alive and energetic, and that the poems through which they danced, chanted and recited their way were banal when attempting lyricism or profundity, but sharply effective when -- too rarely -- wit was the object e.g. in "Sorry," a mocking assemblage of the excuses men make to women.

This sexual reversal serves for an entire evening in The Club off-Broadway; an entire cast of transatlantic Vesta Tilleys perform music-hall and smoking-room ballads (in which women are alternately despised and sentimentally extolled) from the early years of the century. The conceit palls before the performers do. I reflected (a) that the girls seemed to be having a great time with the male routines; and (b) that the same material would sound a great deal sourer today if done by men. This cast, by adding stylisation, have disinfected it.

Winning more garlands than war, sex or race is death; the Tony and the Pulitzer have gone to The Shadow Box by a new playwright, Michael Cristofer. Three people have a terminal illness, generally assumed to be cancer, though the word is never mentioned: an omission which gives as good an idea of the play as anything could. They rage, or endure, their families do much the same, and a somebody called "The Interviewer," infuriatingly ill-defined, puts squishy questions to them all. This is the serious American drama at its sentimentally self-conscious worst.

Two new plays I enjoyed. Larry Gelbart's Sly Fox is Volpone transported to nineteenth-century San Francisco; it systematically coarsens and softens its original, giving Mosca a heart and so robbing Volpone himself of opportunities that he has to double as his own judge to justify his star-status. Its merits, in Arthur Penn's production, are solid carpentry and the opportunities it affords such seasoned vaudevilleans as Jack Gilford and Bob Dishy. They are among the glories of the New York theatre, which affords them too little opportunity.

And there is a new play. Vieux Carre (Probably two, but David Mamet's much-praised American Buffalo closed just before I arrived.) Albert Innaurato's Gemini, set in a Philadelphia backyard, juxtaposes an Italian family, the Irishwoman next door awaiting divorce from a Jew, her monster son (high IQ, but obese body) and a couple of visiting WASPS. Self-discovery may be the theme, but much of the dramatic energy comes from food. "Take human bites," one gobbling character is implored; another, having declared her lack of appetite, wrecks a party by stealing from everyone else's plate. This is comedy, funny, humane and unprocessed; a flavour missing from American theatre for years. The production -- excellent when not over-frantic -- is the work of the Circle Repertory Company, to whom my thanks; having sent this play to Broadway, they filled their Greenwich Village home with a delight called Unsung Cole. That there should still be good Porter songs to be discovered seemed unlikely; but here they were unfussily staged and, for the most part, immaculately sung. There is a golden girl involved called Maureen Moore, who sings "Nobody's Chasing Me," landing each phrase so exactly that one almost believes her.
