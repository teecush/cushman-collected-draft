Coward up-ended

EVERY generation has its own bright-young-thing play, and Doug Lucie's Hard Feelings (Bush) will do fine for this one. It actually both accepts and subverts that description; most of its characters are addled, but one delivers the minority opinion -- obviously endorsed by the author -- that 'people aren't kinds of things, they're people.'

It is Coward up-ended: 'Living for Design,' perhaps. In the spring of 1981, Viv, recent Oxford graduate in English, holds court on an island: more precisely, in a posh house in Brixton owned by her parents, currently living in California. Young plays are beginning to divide into those about the poor unemployed and the rich semi-employed; Mr Lucie offers a familiar enough accumulation.

There is one model, female; one rock musician, male, looking androgynous, but with a hearty heterosexual appetite; one floating entrepreneur, currently very big in frisbees. Also one law student, apt to make the others uncomfortable with her seriousness, not to mention her Jewishness, not to mention her heavy left-wing boy-friend prophesying the coming riots. He is boorish; and it is notable that Mr Lucie eventually makes him sympathetic, not by softening him or pleading his case, but by turning the screws on everybody else.

He gets his characters on stage with remarkable rapidity, four of them rushing in and out the door while the remaining two sleep, their curly tops just visible above the duvet. Subsequent development, as the people get established, is a little slack, but the action gradually crystallises around Viv, hugging her space. ('Hello, room, you're all mine,' she says pitifully, left alone at the end of a scene, after squabbling over lovers and electricity bills.)

In the second act the poster explodes as Viv turns relentlessly on Jane the lawyer, provoking her with a Hitler poster, telling her to leave and refusing explanations with a neurotic self-righteousness (the thin hysteria of style) that is beautifully observed, forbidding the household to talk to her. They acquiesce, with or without misgivings according to temperament. The end finds Viv frantically tidying up against her parents' return. You might call it reversion to type, except that she never really went away.

There is nothing very new here, but the writing and the organisation are first-rate. I even want to see it again. The acting, under Mike Bradwell's direction, is excellent all round the clock. Frances Barber is Viv; Diana Katis the model who echoes her at her worst; Jennifer Landor a troubled presence as Jane, and Stephen Tiller a disruptive one as her man. Ian Reddington does Rusty the rocker with a quivering mastery that suggests a great future in the more neurotic classics; Baz the businessman, in whom decency stirs and is squashed, is played by Chris Jury with a slow, queasy comic authority that suggests a past with Hull Truck. The programme confirms the suggestion, and also -- in describing the piece as 'a viciously funny play set in Brixton in 1981' -- writes its own (accurate) review.

Peter Tinniswood's You Should See Us Now (Greenwich) begins as a toothless suburban comedy in which the men behave like little boys (the hero makes model trains) and the women boss them. This turns out to be the point; the play states that we are forever trapped in our childhood states. This is probably true, but by that time the damage has been done; we have spent ages watching the cast exercising in Ayckbournian style without getting laughs, while incidentally standing around in situations when most people sit down.

Once the author's thesis has emerged, all he can do is restate it; and the device he chooses -- having his grown-up actors actually play children -- yields an embarrassment far out of proportion to its occasional acuteness. Christopher Cazenove goes valiantly through an endless routine of holding his nose, jumping round in circles, and hooting; Delia Lindsay plays a shy girl (which she does well) who grows up into a trainee Penelope Keith, with which she seems unhappy.

For old-style domestic naturalism -- mainly wives yelling at their shiftless husbands -- you have to go these days to black plays. But being an old style -- and a thoroughly artificial one -- it needs tremendous gloss if it is to be brought off; and neither the writing nor the acting of Bitter Milk (Drill Hall) supplies it. The play is described as 'written by David Clough with original plot and additional material by Alton Kumalo,' and rather a lot of the material sounds additional.

Mr Kumalo himself plays with some panache as an illegal immigrant (a musician, who, in Dave Frischberg's immortal words, is so hip he even calls his girl-friend man), caught between the woman he lives with and the white feminist with whom he tries to contract a marriage of convenience. There is some decent writing ('you need O-levels to clean the toilets here'), but the production is listless, causing the audience to clap before the end because they hope it is the end.

The same thing happened at Beau Monde (ICA; ends today), presented by Omnibus, of Montreal, who seem to be school of Pina Bausch. But where the Bausch group have a mysterious gaiety about them, this dance-mime troupe go very earnestly through some familiar motions about people being group-oriented, but self-obsessed. Their chosen period is the Fifties, and they parade in the crew-cuts, bouffant hair-dos, sports jackets and prom dresses, familiar now only from the sleeves of Frank Sinatra reissues. (Which reminds me; the music, billed as 'popular tunes of the 50s,' is invariably too early or too late.) What is interesting though is that their image of prosperity is similar to the one of recession in Hard Feelings.

Readers who don't take the Guardian may like to know that David Hare, responding in that paper to the reviews of his play A Map of the World, has let it be known (a) that critics who like his plays are good guys and those who don't aren't, and (b) -- with reference to a late scene in this play -- that aid to Third World countries is given with strings attached. I tell you, this man has insights.

On his main, philosophical, argument, I think Mr Hare has gone raving mad. He claims that the reviews, being biased, prove the point he was making in the play: that we all see issues and events partially, and from whatever angle suits us. You'd think he'd be grateful for the corroboration, though he doesn't seem to be. But if the distorting tendency is as universal as he seems to believe (and as I certainly do), then critics are not going to be exempt from it.

It is not even desirable that they should be. It is not the critic's job to paraphrase the play, even if he had the space to do so. It is his job to describe, and try to account for, its impact; and, unless there are violent scenes in the auditorium, this has to mean its impact on him. This will not usually, from the public's point of view, be very misleading. I reckon that the percentage of good reviews received by most plays is about equal to the percentage of the potential audience that will enjoy them.
