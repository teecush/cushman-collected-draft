The National Party

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Trevor Griffiths's new play about revolutionary politics.

TREVOR GRIFFITHS'S The Party is the final fruit of Kenneth Tynan's Literary Consultancy at the National Theatre. The National has in the past derived more sustenance from Tynan the showman than from Tynan the ideologue, but The Party, commissioned from our most explicitly political playwright, could be an attempt to even the score.

As ever, one searches for clues in the programme. There, amid a generous selection of revolutionary texts, one slogan from Paris 1968 claims attention: "When the National Assembly becomes a bourgeois theatre, all the bourgeois theatres must become National Assemblies."

Mr Griffiths has attempted to effect this transformation at the Old Vic. The central portion of his play is a debate between assorted radicals who, at the time of those same Paris explosions, sit at home in London and try to hammer out a programme for a new revolutionary party. If you detect a note of scorn in that sentence it is implicit in the situation, as the characters themselves testify by their fascination with the TV news; they are uneasily aware that the real action is going on elsewhere.

The discussion is inconclusive; theatrically, its main interest is that it throws up two marathon speeches. Their sustained seriousness suggests that Mr Griffiths wishes, straightforwardly, to educate his auditors; their length that he is daring them not to listen or even to fall asleep. The National Theatre audience, however, is a collective good sport, game as well as ripe for instruction. It swallowed Tom Stoppard philosophising, Peter Shaffer psychologising, and it will not strain at Mr Griffiths politicising. Nor should it; Mr Griffiths's tirades are extremely well written and superbly delivered.

Denis Quilley leads off as an LSE teacher who believes that nothing further can be expected from the Western proletariat and that the centres of revolutionary hope now lie exclusively in the Third World. He is thus inclined to regard much of his colleagues' activities as irrelevant. But there is a fair touch of irrelevance about him in Mr Quilley's performance: he is bland, a little prim, and his fingers, unzipping his briefcase to extract his notes and fastening it again once the lecture has been given, are deliciously self-satisfied.

But is this just jolly character detail or is it some kind of pointer to the play's theme? That the latter may be the case is suggested, though never positively established, by the treatment given to his antagonist, an old Glaswegian Trotskyist of majestic stature. In great tidal waves of rhetoric, artfully proportioned, he politely savages the middle-class trendies by whom he is surrounded: "You bite the hand that feeds you, but you'll never bite it off." Hear Olivier bark that last word; I have never seen him act better. Greater, but not better.

Few of the others present are more than caricatures, granted a couple of stereotyped lines apiece. Did Mr Griffiths intend his play to be merely a satire on the New Left? Or are they there because this is a National Theatre play, and should therefore have a large cast? Some of the best actors in the company and indeed the country are on view in this production (I hope Peter Hall is not intending to sack too many of them) and they do their best; but many of them are constrained to let their costumes do the work of characterisation. None of them is equipped to issue a direct challenge to Olivier's John Tagg, so this has to be done by underhand means. Not once but twice the instigator of the gathering, a television producer, is taken aside by his ex-wife (works for the Guardian, very forthright, understands him perfectly, God knows why they split up) and warned against succumbing to the old man's dangerous working-class sentimentality.

I am not sure whether this advice is meant to reflect on her or on Tagg or both; more damagingly, I am unsure of its effect on the recipient. He is the protagonist and what finally wrecks the evening is Mr Griffiths's failure to establish any relationship (even a negative one) between the play's public argument and the character's private life. He carries the full load of successful hang-ups: a refugee from his class, from his North-country background, and from commitment; he has a bitch of a wife, is maybe impotent, and certainly masturbates (the play begins with a sex-scene of cinematic explicitness). He also has a young brother who is touching him for 300 pounds to start a business. Initially he demurs at devoting his surplus cash to the furtherance of capitalism, though, to judge from John Napier's inordinately plush set - perhaps another necessary concomitant of National Theatre production - he has already invested so much in the consumer society that to scruple at this point is ridiculous; finally he delivers. But how, or whether, this decision relates to all that has gone before, or how we are to judge it, is never revealed.

In themselves, the scenes between the two brothers are good to watch: John Shrapnel is beautifully fresh, shy and bruisable as the nascent entrepreneur and Ronald Pickup brings his usual intelligence (subtly stepping up the Northern accent whenever he encounters a compatriot) to the vastly ungrateful central role, though he is physically too slight to compel attention when passive, which is most of the time. He has a further foil in Frank Finlay, playing, with appropriate richness, a successful and very drunk playwright several steps farther along the road to disillusion, who regularly punctures the pretensions of the other characters. This is often a superfluous exercise, but he has some very amusing lines.

Closing my eyes and holding on tight I would hazard that Tagg is the play's true hero and the others who disagree with him, deride him, ignore him, or put him down behind his back are meant to be condemned; in the words of John Lennon, "It ain't easy to be a working-class hero." But that is an abstraction from the play, not a response to it. To watch and listen to it is to be fogged and finally frustrated by directionless, inconclusive ironies. Overtopping them all, of course (and maybe it is the play's real subject - a gigantic escape-clause), is the presence of this author at this theatre, compounded by the casting of Olivier in such a role (and further compounded by his brilliance in it). John Dexter's production is excellent in its own terms; but they are glossy, prestigious terms. I am not blaming anyone; the machine goes into action and that is what it produces.

The result is another victory for showmanship. We take pleasure in the rhetoric; but in our attentiveness there is an element of self-congratulation by which Mr Griffiths might well be perturbed.

Still, if you're looking for a party the National is currently a better bet than the Roundhouse where Feast of Fools, a medieval frolic, is as uncontrollable as the Black Death and nearly as messy. For a really good group show try the Actors' Company, making an overdue London debut at the Shaw with their version of R. D. Laing's Knots. This company's egalitarian principles are their own affair; their standards are mine, and on this occasion are exhilaratingly high.
