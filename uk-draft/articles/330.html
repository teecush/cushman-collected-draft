National censorship

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Birmingham.

It would be nice to know how the GLC proposes to distribute the money it has withheld from the National Theatre. It will go, it says, to help prop up other artistic enterprises. That sounds virtuous; the National is hardly the only organisation in the city that needs money. All we want now are the details. What we have to be going on with is a remarkably spiteful piece of retrospective censorship.

The National is, quite confessedly, being penalised for having staged The Romans in Britain. Not, though, on the grounds that it is a bad play; maybe the GLC thinks it is, but it hasn't said so. The Romans received unexpected canonisation from Bernard Levin last week in The Times; Mr Levin exaggerates as usual, but his heart is in the right place.

Anyway not even the GLC could claim that The Romans is a worse play than the National's other new ones, Thee and Me and Line 'Em -- if only because there being no buggery featured or reported in either of those, the councillors probably never got round to seeing them. All the same they may have contributed; I suspect that, lurking in County Hall, as elsewhere, is a belief that it is about time these young (or, more accurately, youngish) playwrights were slapped down a bit. Howard Brenton provided the longed-for provocation; he may even, somewhere inside himself, have intended to.

Over the years there has been good, bad and indifferent work done on the South Bank, with the bad occasioning no squawking from the paymasters. I rather wish that funding bodies would make decisions based on standard of work (as the Arts Council has begun to do) and would make their judgments public (as the Arts Council has failed to do). But they should be consistent about it, and not confuse criticism with sanctimony. And they should have some common sense, maybe even some self-respect; after all, if you believe a playwright is using shock tactics, why give him the satisfaction of showing you are shocked?

Feminists have claimed that there would have been no outcry had the play showed a woman, rather than a man, being raped. They are right, but for the wrong reasons: what is proved is not our low opinion of women, but our unease at any graphic depiction of homosexuality. This may not, though, be the case in Birmingham, where a scene of mass sodomy in The Triumph of Death (Rep Studio) has so far produced little public reaction.

Here admittedly there is no question of rape; all parties are positively eager. The scene is a religious orgy, at which some medieval woodlanders offer themselves to their god, who seems to be Pan. They are then dispersed, and mostly killed, by the forces of the Church.

It is never too clear in David Rudkin's play what is going on or why, but I had the impression that the author was on the side of the pagans. They seem to be descended from the survivors of the Children's Crusade, which ghastly event occupies the play's first section. We have some gloating from an all-purpose Papal figure, with humanity -- represented by the rest of the company -- grovelling in the folds of his voluminous garment. A couple of clerks give theological proof that it's all right to kill Saracens.

Stephen, leader of the children, is discovered seated happily on the lavatory, and is summoned first by Jesus ('yes, it's me'), then by a seductive blonde lady, who is probably Death. Christ later mutates into Pan; Death hangs around, collecting victims, working in cahoots with what somebody Shavianly calls 'the good Cross-tian folk.' One of the survivors of the forest massacre is a girl called Jehan; she is brought before the Inquisition, found guilty and pregnant, brought to bed and burned. She seems more like Joan of Arc, unauthorised version, every second.

Flash forward to Martin Luther, who proclaims himself the child of Joan and the religious sadist Gilles de Rais. One of Jehan's lovers was indeed called Giles (he was last seen turning into some kind of werewolf) and there has also been talk of a Bluebeard. Luther of course is constipated; the progress of Christianity seems to have entailed a narrowing of the spirit and a clogging of the bowel. Only this isn't really Luther, but a present-day female mental case. Her keeper -- Death again -- pronounces a heavily ironic valediction: 'Heaven, hell, all that antique delirium, now, we've come down to earth, we've got it right.'

Mr Rudkin's thesis is not ludicrous, and is passionately felt; you can tell that from the trouble he's been to, and puts us to. Ours at least is not justified; the play's structure is bewildering and much of the language an archaic swamp. I doubt if any writer has achieved a first-rate play by abandoning the language of his own time; and though Mr Rudkin brings off the occasional arresting phrase or incident his method here effectively precludes communication -- not only between stage and audience, but between actor and actor. The players in Peter Farago's production just about get through the text.

The Golden Age (Greenwich), by A. R. Gurney Jr, goes in for a different kind of archaism. Its plot is confessedly based on The Aspern Papers, which makes it a kind of structural pun. For in this case the scholar who comes seeking details and documentation of the literary past has as his quarry an old lady who, if she didn't know James, knew all his cultural heirs. She was a famous hostess in the New York of the Twenties, may have had an affair with Scott Fitzgerald and may possess a manuscript containing Fitzgerald's only sexually explicit piece of writing.

This is the prize in pursuit of which our sleuth seduces the chatelaine's grand-daughter, though one of Mr Gurney's twists is that both ladies are thoroughly in favour of this arrangement. Constance Cummings does not, especially in a fright of a white wig, look like a piece of history; she is at her best when the script allows her to come down to earth, recalling the servants who were exploited for her comfort, or, up to date, rebuking her young interviewer for not liking M*A*S*H. (She, having lived through the Golden Age, has no need to despise the present, and can salute the persistence of wit; he is an earnest snob.)

Angela Thorne is really fine as the heiress, calculating the odds on repression. Bernard Culshaw has designed a thoroughly convincing brownstone. Mr Gurney's writing is graceful, sometimes witty, but ultimately bland. He has, respectably enough, borrowed melodramatic devices from other writers, but he has not re-charged them.

In Twisted Cues and Elliptical Balls (Arts), John Judd, with much audience participation, does a skilled and ebullient music-hall turn based on the lives and songs of Gilbert and Sullivan. He also throws in some acting; gruff and conventional as Gilbert, he is a touching and vulnerable Sullivan. There seems no particular reason to stop him.
