Murder, rape, and politics

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on plays by Howard Barker, John Mortimer and Shaw

The Royal Shakespeare Company's Warehouse in Covent Garden - formerly a rehearsal room and making few bones about it - is now open for business. Last week the critics were let in for a sight of That Good Between Us, a two-year-old piece by Howard Barker, the least doctrinaire of our youngish romantics, the readiest both to exploit "chaos" (a word he himself employs) and to be appalled by it.

That may sound a disreputable combination, but it has a respectable enough lineage. The greatest English practitioners were the Jacobeans, but they covered themselves by setting their blood-boltered scenes either in the past or in Italy. The eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Romantics - often more thoughtful writers - did the same: cf. Musset's Lorenzaccio, also currently aired by the RSC, though in an adaptation which substitutes for the author's voice that of any writer of the 1970s. (Interpreting a classic has come increasingly to mean asking it to tell us things we already know rather than letting us be disturbed by its otherness.)

In his present play Mr Barker deals, like Musset or Webster, in rape, murder, temporising sententious politicians, inefficient conspirators, stormily lascivious ladies and ambitious, discontented spies. The difference is that Mr Barker's setting is not fairyland; his action happens not only here, but now, or at least - since he posits such mutations from the present order as a female Home Secretary and an army almost wholly devoted to strike-breaking - in a few minutes' time.

Useless to accuse Mr Barker of melodrama: he has only to point to the headlines. The trouble is that the play's texture remains that of the headlines; its people are very thin, creatures of their author's hectic pessimism, which they are obligingly good at verbalising. There is wit at work here; an agent named Godber is allowed to offer exactly the same rhetorical credentials - viz, the disillusion awaiting a bright grammar-school boy when offering himself for Government work - as when infiltrating military subversives in the course of that work. Fine; but he sounds in the same note of brittle toughness throughout the play; obviously he thinks it's clever. His author, unfortunately, seems to think it's real.

He is the middle man of a trio: above him spymaster Knatchbull, fond of a handicapped daughter, respectful of the existing order and prosaically ruthless in the protection of both; below is McPhee, the tartan yob, bound in service to Knatchbull, won over by the opposition's talk of freedom, and finally hunted by both sides. All three are expertly played: by John Nettles, the young master of hard, bright social deviance, Patrick Stewart in his cosy commissar's vein and Ian McDiarmid, king of the underdogs. His McPhee is as wild as his shopman in Destiny was respectable; he gets his full naked height and yells triumphantly "what sounds like 'I ...? Some new rough beast, swimming towards Bethlehem, to be born?'"

Barbara Leigh-Hunt as the Home Secretary has two humane and eloquent speeches, one on Fascism, the other on sex: the second is belied by her daughter, who jumps into bed with Godber while despising him, and the first by her own action in licensing murder and muzzling the Press. Since this is accompanied by a full complement of moral qualms, one wants to know in more detail the circumstances which prompt her to it. Doomwatch drama has become a mannerism, almost a reflex, and it comes here with full trimmings: a stageful of debris, dialogue to match, and a marginally relevant gang-bang, graphically staged. Some of it grips; Mr Barker's generation of playwrights differs from its predecessors in providing stories, and this one, confusing at once, is worked out with jigsaw precision. But there is the scent of bravado about it all, natural perhaps in a classical company always sensitive to any charge of respectability.

My favourite line in John Mortimer's The Bells of Hell, in its one-act form at Greenwich, was the closer, "he's a gentleman," applied by the rector's wife to the (literal) Prince of Darkness who had seduced her, and who had temporarily restored her husband's faith by filling his fridge with loaves and fishes. At the Garrick the finale has turned to frantic gyrations backed by fireworks, and the rest of the play has been similarly coarsened. Two new characters have attenuated the plot and have pushed the wife's awakening, which once was crucial, to the side of the stage.

Phyllida Law may well be for general purposes a better actress than Eleanor Bron, but following in Miss Bron's steps without her caricaturist's line she just seems to be over-acting. Peter Woodthorpe's floppy rector, Tony Britton's hearty curate, and Trevor Baxter's efflorescent bishop, are all hard-working performances worked too hard, and the same fault overtakes the small but amusing situation at the heart of the play.

Shaw's The Apple Cart furnishes Chichester's statutory good production for the year. Keith Michell's Magnus has charm, tact and a good memory - the basic requirements - and his Cabinet is excellently filled; you can even tell them apart, which is more, I fancy, than the author could manage most of the time. In the approved feature roles are Paul Hardwick as a jovial and innocent Boanerges, June Jago, whose attack on the Breakages Corporation is as usual the highspot of the play, and Nigel Stock, whose Prime Minister is a guileful assembly of celebrated mannerisms, timed to a turn or done to a T or both. The very secretaries (Brian Spink and Adam Bareham) are excellent, setting the tone for a stylish examination of monarchy, symbol and reality.

It is not their fault that no such thing emerges. Magnus's royalty, as Shaw said himself, is incidental; what counts is his individuality, but since no-one else is allowed any, the contest lacks bite. I spent some time wondering whether Penelope Keith was wasted or just miscast as Orinthia the Beloved; finally, settling on both. Miss Keith is a supremely businesslike actress, and Orinthia had no time for business.
