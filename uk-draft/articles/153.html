Of liberty and damnation

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN revives Stratford, Manchester and London.

While the Stratford-upon-Avon main house takes us to the Middle Ages, the Other Place is going Renaissance. A notable seventeenth-century tragedy has been revived in modern dress, while by way of balance a modern play by a contemporary playwright has gone to work on sixteenth-century Florence in period costume.

Paul Thompson's The Lorenzaccio Story is not, strictly speaking, a new play. It is based on the Lorenzaccio written in the 1830s by Alfred de Musset. Musset's piece is saturated in revolutionary disenchantment and has all the hallmarks (dozens of brief elaborate scenes, banquets for 40 people, horses) of the insouciantly unstageable. Looked at another way it cries out to be staged, and one can imagine Franco Zeffirelli putting it across as a lush Medici documentary or Terry Hands as a masque of death.

Its subject is the assassination of a young debauched Duke by Lorenzo, his cousin, friend and procurer. Lorenzo has always wanted to be a tyrannicide, but to fulfil his ambition has had to get so close to his victim as to take on his colouring. Few Florentines will trust him and none will take him seriously.

Before committing the murder he tries to rouse the leading Republicans; they tell him to go home and stop playing the fool. Earlier he confesses to Filippo Strozzi, the local leader of progressive opinion, that his project has become an end in itself; he will go through with it, but he has no hope that it will lead to a better society and no interest in constructing one.

Mr Thompson retains both these scenes and they are the best in his play. Elsewhere he seems an efficient craftsman, though his personality is none too easy to make out. Musset's play seems simultaneously to have appealed to him and to have got up his nose. Lorenzo's fatalism is retained more or less undoctored, though in both writers it is an elaborate form of words rather than a dramatised process and the new words are much flatter than the old. He is also more explicit, and harsher, with Strozzi the liberal aesthete (well played by David Swift) roundly condemned by his own son, who ends up leading a students' revolt: a foredoomed gesture but, it is suggested, a noble one.

Nor that this son is faultless; he shrugs off Florentine poverty as no concern of his. This attitude was shared, incidentally, by Musset, for whom ducal tyranny seemed mainly to consist of treading on the toes of old-established families and seducing their women. Mr Thompson throws in some economic colouring, writing up the roles of two trade-conscious bourgeois onlookers and having Lorenzo fall victim to a poor man who needs the reward money: a fate our hero accepts with his usual cankered equanimity.

Musset, endorsing Lorenzo's despair, got close to tragedy; Mr Thompson, relishing but not quite following it, winds up with cynicism. Peter McEnery, long, lean, and lank-haired, looks conscientiously depraved, yet his overstatement of the corruption suggests that he does not really believe in it. James Laurenson, less effortful and more contained, gives the Duke a poisonous romantic flair; he is one of those rare actors made for Jacobean tragedy.

It would be good, for example, to see him in Ford's 'Tis Pity She's a Whore, the other Other Place play. As it is the most Italianate performance in this story of incest and butchery in Parma is given (as an expatriate Spaniard, oddly enough) by Geoffrey Hutchings: grey hair neatly combed, stocky and menacing in his blue pin-stripes, definitely a man of respect. And also of religion; when he learns of the bedding of brother and sister, his response ("to what a height of liberty and damnation hath the devil trained our age") is quiet and heartfelt; staggered, he looks for comfort to one of the religious pictures which dot the stage.

Though Ron Daniels's production lays great stress on the transgressed orthodox code, it comes out with no clear attitude towards it; friars seem to be good and helpful, cardinals bad and grasping. (Here he is true to his author; there is a book to be written on the shifting attitudes of Elizabethan drama to the Catholic Church, treated as venal superstition or moral authority, often within the same play.)

The plots and sub-plots are laid out neatly, but neither Simon Rouse nor Barbara Kellerman are up to the leading roles, though he at least attempts to grapple with Giovanni's rising insanity and she lends Annabella a pleasing teasing quality, like a girl trying to convince herself that she is having an ordinary affair with ordinary rules. Mr Hutchings apart, the play belongs to Tim Wylton, emerging from his Bardolph make-up to play the fool Bergetto, whose death signifies that the play is getting down to business.

Having given us life with father as seen by Peter Nichols and John Mortimer, the British stage has delayed in bringing us Hugh Leonard's Da (King's Head), who proves the best of three: an unstoppably winsome eccentric, rejoicing in a pension of 19 shillings a week because it comes from the Quality, and provoking from his son the anguished comment, "If you knocked him down in a motorcar he'd thank you for the lift."

Even within this convention I found Eamon Kelly's old man excessively invertebrate, but I have never gone much on Irish charm; I prefer the steelier, worldlier Irish actor of whom Tony Doyle (playing Mr Leonard or his surrogate) is the finest specimen since T. P. McKenna. Mike McCabe, touchingly blank-faced, acts out the struggle of his younger self in quest of a job or (the best and funniest scene in the play) a girl; Kevin Moore is beautifully shockable as a contemporary who never made the break.

Whatever the structural differences between a play and a musical, it is unwise to use for the latter a story that would not pass muster for the former. That is the lesson of Trevor Peacock's Leaping Ginger (Royal Exchange, Manchester). Which hangs some attractive numbers - including ensembles which approach the Sondheim standard of musicalised conversation - on a frayed and indeterminate fable about a young ex-con falling foul of big business man (assumed to be his moral inferior though the case is never proved) and in love with his rehabilitation officer. She is black, though the show only registers the fact too late to make anything of it but portentous melodrama. Malcolm Rennie's villain is the only performance with much personality, but Graham Murray's production makes the best use yet of this theatre and its stereophonic sound effects.

To the Cottesloe comes Steven Berkoff's production of Steven Berkoff's East starring Steven Berkoff. The use of pastiche blank verse for this black impression of the East End is still irritating (an endless repetition of the anachronism joke), but the mime remains excellent and two prose passages - Anna Nygh's outcry against male domination and Barry Philips's bitter saga of life in the rag trade - deserve to be classics.

Previously at the Cottesloe we had Chris Harris in Kemp's Jig, a one-man show about the Elizabethan comic who left the Lord Chamberlain's Men to dance to Norwich in nine days. Mr Harris is now on the road himself and worth catching if he rigs your way; less for his own clowning or the narrative of his travels than for his recurrent memories of his feud with a snuffling, scratching Shakespeare.
