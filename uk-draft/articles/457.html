Capturing the whale

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN
In Manchester and London.

By all accounts including his own Michael Elliott has for years been as intent on bringing Moby Dick to the stage as was Captain Ahab on bringing him to the slaughter. A director can convince an audience with complex machinery or seduce it by extreme simplicity. Mr Elliott, at the Manchester Royal Exchange, does both.

He makes super-subtle use of his theatre's unrivalled acoustics, and his towering arena is ideal for evoking first a port, then a ship, finally the sea itself. Laurie Dennett's setting, as one would have expected, fills the stage with rigging. Also as expected, the cast, in the cause of atmosphere, spend a lot of time and energy heaving at capstans while singing shanties.

And yet, when the hunt is on and the boats are out, we easily accept a new convention whereby groups of actors sit straining at invisible oars, before being tumbled into invisible waves. Finally, by a magnificently simple trick that it would be heartless to reveal, the naked stage becomes simultaneously the ocean and (Tynan's phrase when reviewing Orson Welles's adaptation) the supremely unstageable whale, unstageable no longer. For a moment, Mr Elliott captures him.

The whalers prove unexpectedly harder quarry. Mr Elliott keeps faith with the language Melville gave them, that flaming amalgam of Victorian badinage and Jacobean oratory. Ahab's 'I'd strike the sun if it insulted me' is one perfect iambic among many. His last harpooner's rhapsody ('What ho, Tashtego, let me hear thy hammer') is shirked, but we do get the second mate's request, when on the verge of becoming whalemeat, for one red cherry ere he dies: one of those lines that passes in a mere book, but shrivels when exposed in a theatre.

I usually think of Moby Dick as a novel I never finished, but under pressure my memory reveals that I did indeed get to the end, though only by skipping large portions of the middle. What stays with me is the beginning, and I recall in Ishmael's narrative a humour, and even wit, that is missing here; Mr Elliott would hardly have cast so truculent an actor as Nigel Terry had he wished to preserve them. Something of them survives in Terence Wilton's Queequeg; and there are worthy performances from John Cording, earnest as Starbuck, and Tim Healy, jovial as Stubb.

No other individuals come through, but maybe none needs to. Everything must depend on Ahab: a natural dramatic subject (the classic avenger), but conceived in a totally undramatic fashion. He cannot be acted, he can only be embodied; and whom would you cast? Brian Cox approaches him with devout intelligence, but he seems even saner than Gregory Peck. His eyes grow wild and his hair matted, but his voice refuses to follow suit. He seems a strict but amiable man with a sound Presbyterian upbringing and, it has to be admitted, an unaccountable thing about whales: not enough, you feel, to keep him from yielding to Starbuck's entreaties and sailing right back to Nantucket.

Failing to draw us into Ahab's obsession, the production cannot involve us with what it might actually mean. (Mr Elliott does some regrettable circling in his programme note: the whale must be meaningful because the novel is so great, and the novel must be great because the whale is so meaningful.) But if we are not caught up in the captain's quest, we are enthralled by the director's; by quiet things like the swaying of the crew even when the seas are calm, and by overwhelming ones like the vision of St Elmo's fire, which strikes black magic from the lighting grid.

Bernard Slade, the Broadway playwright who through taste or economic instinct specialises in two-handers, presents us in Special Occasions (Ambassadors) with the passage through the years of a divorced couple. Always each other's best friends and confidants, they remain so, whatever the breaks: an easy situation to swallow, but a difficult one to develop.

By the end of the second act Mr Slade's dramaturgy has degenerated into a prolonged tease over whether our hero and heroine are going finally to remarry. (The slob in you hopes they will, the pedant prays they won't; choose your weakness.) Some of the earlier writing connects with real pain, though the fatal flaws -- she drinks and he writes -- are never very believable.

Somebody has transferred the characters from there to here: a decision that involves them in uttering such typically English remarks as 'You resent the hell out of me' and in claiming to have got drunk on the flight from London to Manchester. (Try even getting a drink.) Jan Waters does handsomely, though John Alderton is hamstrung by the author's refusal to acknowledge what a bastard the fellow is. Mr Slade also directs, on somebody's misconception of a unit set.

Peter Pan (Barbican) impressively consolidates last year's uneven promise. With everyone sure of the special effects, events in the lagoon and on the pirate-ship are twice as lucid as before. Katy Behean, as Wendy, unemphatically brings out all the facets of the child-mother; and Mark Rylance, with his vigorously piping voice, gets both the boy Peter and the spirit Pan.

Edward Petherbridge is a winsomely confidential narrator, and though Stephen Moore hardly tries to make Hook frightening, he does make him very entertaining. There has been a definite rise in the quality of pirate and lost boy, and the wildlife remains endearing, especially the crocodile, the only character in world drama of whom it is reasonable to inquire what makes him tick. (Cap'n Ahab's life would have been transformed if Moby Dick had swallowed a clock.)

The revival of Oliver! (Aldwych) is glorified by the return of Ron Moody to the role of Fagin: capering in his juvenile thieves' kitchen with his tattered cloak outstretched in all directions, like an inspired animated cartoon of the Pied Piper of Hamelin. Sean Kenny's copious set, wonderfully lit in blues and ambers, shows its age with dignity.

Some of Lionel Bart's lyrics may be slipshod (not all) and some of his music cheap (again not all), but his fairy-tale version of Dickens's horror-story has its own integrity; like My Fair Lady, it corresponds to a romantic image which, however perversely, we have all carried away from the original.

Hello Dolly (Prince of Wales), by contrast, merely trims and decorates its source. Its historical importance is that it institutionalised the idea of the Broadway leading lady, forever descending staircases. Carol Channing, when she finally climbed down in London, was playing like a drag-queen; to cast Danny La Rue seems the logical next step. Unfortunately Mr La Rue's talents are not musical; to play Dolly you do not have to sing exactly, but you have to move the earth with your voice. Here we are asked to get our kicks from a parade of frocks.

The only performer I felt like applauding was Lionel Jeffries, a beleaguered walrus. 'Hello Dolly' depends on its original staging; in David Toguri's new choreography respectable shops are invaded by dancers who plainly have no business there. Several of Jerry Herman's tunes still appeal. When a musical is described as having a one-song score, the description is always false and the song that is meant will be far from the best. That is infallible wisdom. Goodbye.
