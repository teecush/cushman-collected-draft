But what comes after?

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

SNOO WILSON writes great entrances. His new comedy Loving Reno (Bush) begins with Philip Donaghy walking in through the double doors of what the programme tells us is a private airport lounge in Florida. Mr Donaghy looks dazzling, being dressed for polo; he is also moustachioed and oily all over, especially on the crown of his head. He is a Latin American stage magician. Tony Reno is his name.

The play lines him up with his wife and the mother of his children: two different ladies. These children, now grown, are twins, both girls. Reno has exploited them: used them as assistants in his act, and also used them sexually. One of them hates him for it; the other loves him and wants to marry him. She's retarded.

All the women in the play seem to have suffered from paternal abuse. Reno is macho, indeed machissimo; for him, male genes carry the power and the glory, especially the power. The play is a cool blast against patriarchy, and it makes its points with satisfying neatness.

Having made them, though, it has little left to do but talk. Mr Wilson may bring his character on in a storm, but he becalms them once they have arrived. There is a plot -- involving an undercover agent hired by Reno's wife to shadow and expose him -- but it cuts little mustard. Anyone remembering the ceaseless activity -- verbal, physical and intellectual -- of Mr Wilson's The Glad Hand (my favourite play of the past 10 years) is likely to be disappointed. He is almost the wittiest playwright we have, and this play is a good listen, but there is something closed-off about it. It is presumably the product of Mr Wilson's recent year in America, but I suspect he would have written it much the same if he had never been.

If unforced arrogance makes a star, Mr Donaghy (who is also very funny) should now be one; certainly it makes a Reno. He must have had a daunting time in rehearsal, being directed by Mr Wilson who wrote the role and Simon Callow who could well have played it. Judy Elrington gives an on-off performance as the soberer sister but Cassie Stuart tucks into the kewpie-doll nympho (of whom it is said that she must have cost a fortune in broken-down hotel doors). Anna Raitt and Tony Guilfoyle are worth their weight.

Mr Wilson was one of the first fringe playwrights, class of '68, and he sometimes seems likely to be among the last, his contemporaries having mostly moved on to larger stages and his juniors having hardly made it to any stages at all. This means he has spent 15 years as a member of an endangered species. Fringe theoreticians were prophesying the death of the playwright when he started, and they are at it again. Their temple is the ICA, now specialising in works that claim to yoke the creativity of the stage performer to that of the visual artist. Which, you must admit, sounds absolutely fine.

The trouble is how it looks, or at least how it looks after the first 10 minutes which is as long as most of us are prepared to spend gazing at a set. The art then has to reside in the actors, who are liable to swan about making elliptical passes at the incomprehensible, occasionally surfacing with a meaning that seems, literally, too banal for words. They may look pretty doing it but it hardly makes an evening.

Rococo, brought to the ICA by the Rational Theatre Company, meets us verbalists more than half-way; it is full of chat. It also suggests that performance art (a term I have been trying, lucklessly, to fend off) is developing its own cliches; we get a surround of mirrors, and a white-clad actress who stands dimly surveying (accusing?) us before the lights go up. She then meets a flock of old-style stage aristocrats who draw her into their world where they proceed to bemuse, humiliate and seduce her; her experience is a bit like that of Lewis Carroll's Alice, and a lot like those of the real, virtuous heroines of Jean Anouilh. Her tormentors fancy themselves, and their dialogue sounds like a poor pastiche of Restoration comedy, delivered by actors who don't know how. The Glasgow Citizens' Theatre do this kind of thing all the time, often throwing in a good text as well. At times the show justifies its pretensions and makes a witty visual statement, as when a pinky lady haunts a white gentleman into taking all his clothes off.

A lady called Sandy Powell (can you hear her, mother?) is the designer; Jim Whiting, guest artist, created a couple of ingenious mechanical contraptions. You can get good trick effects at the Palladium too. A handout says that the show 'supposes a society in a state of advance future shock,' but I wouldn't have guessed. Rococo and the Wilson play are really in the same bind: what do you do after you have astonished the audience? The prophets are of course right in saying that most written drama is deadly, but the playwright still has the best chance of following through. We want shock, and then logic, and then more shock.

Jukebox (Astoria) is a rapid-fire, one-chorus-and-off, pop-music retrospective. It starts with cavemen, the theme from 2001, and a voice telling us that rock music started long ago, in a galaxy far, far away. This is not true, even if it ought to be; meanwhile, the unabashed plagiarism gives the theatre the air of one of those outlying cinemas that mysteriously survive through endless programming of what used to be cult movies.

A jukebox duly materialises; emanating from it is the sound of Teresa Brewer singing 'Music, Music, Music.' She is brutally silenced. This may not mean much to you, it may even be aesthetically just, but that 1950 song was the first pop record I ever knew, and I resented it. I resented some of what followed even more. Behind the performers there are projections. An all-purpose 1960s protest-song is backed by a blown-up photograph of a real, agonised Vietnamese child. But it's all right, because the child disappears in time for the next number which is 'Oh Happy Day.'

Not all of the show is that offensive, but it is all that mindless. This is not always a fault; it suits the bubble idiocies of vintage rock'n'roll and it cannot do much harm to the machine-gloomy, sex-on-the-percussionness of the 1970s, though when the earnestness starts selling it needs resenting. As for contemporary British pop, do they come much more American perhaps than Boy George? This sounds like bitching, but does the choreographer, if I may abuse the old quip, is possibly better than it looks.

For an antidote there is Bertha Reading. Every inch a Lady (King's Head), the inches extending out rather than up. (She does a brisk balletic interlude as Sugar Plum Fairy who seems to have overdosed on the sugar.) Miss Reading is where critics go to enjoy themselves, a jazz-queen who can be blowsily hilarious or rollingly emotive. She should know better; though, then, to sing Shirley Bassey dirges, especially Send in the Clowns. Her accompanist is Jules Rubens, a venerable imp who looks like a bit of old Soho and can swing with his left hand while knocking out saloon-bar basses with his right.

The world which has been waiting for two whole weeks to know what I really thought of Michael Sloan's thriller Underground (Prince of Wales), may now unbate its breath; it's tough to admit but I agreed with everybody else, especially about the unlikelihood of supposed strangers conversing in the Tube, especially when the first line is 'Why have we stopped?' Then there is a tramp who forces his demented conversation on everybody but waits for gaps in the dialogue to do it, just like a character in a play.

All one can do to the cast of TV favourites, led by Raymond Burr, is commiserate with them on their woodenness. One might perhaps get angry with Peter Wynarde; he can still put on a turn of speed impressive enough for us to wonder just what he thinks he has been doing with his career.
