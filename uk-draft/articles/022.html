O sweet, O lovely Wall!

THEATRE

Robert Cushman on 'Cockie'.

London's latest nostalgia trip is *Cockie* (Vaudeville), a tribute to the most celebrated of West End impresarios, Charles B. Cochran.

Since Cochran's speciality was revue, Peter Saunders (who hereby qualifies as the impresario's impresario) has chosen to commemorate him with a melange of songs and sketches on the pattern of the Mermaid's *Cowardy Custard*. What would be the equivalent culinary title? *Cock-a-leekie*, I guess. Thematic unity is not the evening's strongest point. The opening number, appropriately enough, is "Anything Goes," given a spirited, featureless song-and-dance rendition by a scrubbed, anonymous crew who apparently constitute the full company. At the prospect of being entertained all night by a bunch of chorines, male and female, the heart sinks.

It stays sunk as two young men detach themselves from the corps and start in on the biography. With stolid mid-Atlantic cheeriness, they dispatch some rhyming couplets which, since no author is named, might just have been dreamed up by Mr Saunders in his bath. Some of the terms in which they apostrophise Cochran are a little obscure. For a taste: "Reinhardt and Hackenschmidt were one to you." Not to me. I've heard of Reinhardt.

The narrators vanish, for good, having informed us that Cochran's early failure as a performer left him with no recourse but to turn manager. Just like that. C.B.'s conversation is informative rather than sparkling; unexpectedly rescued from bankruptcy, he tosses off, "I'm going straight into production at the Adelphi with Noel Coward's new revue *Words and Music*." That's telling us.

At other times continuity becomes the responsibility of disembodied voices reverently intoning the names of Cochran's leading ladies. Alice Delysia gets the fullest treatment; her euphonious surname is sent fairly echoing round the stage. I might have been more awestruck had I not recently seen the Bristol Old Vic's *Dracula*, where the same trick is played with the word Transylvania.

The ads for *Cockie* give top billing to the composers of the twenties and thirties. They can be sampled here in some quantity: eight numbers by Coward, four by Cole Porter, three by Rodgers and Hart, two each by Jerome Kern and Irving Berlin, and one by the Gershwins. The prospect waters the mouth, but the actuality dries it up a bit. I expect shows like this to introduce me to neglected material, but here most of the melodies are familiar, and there is little pleasure in hearing American standards given the English musical comedy treatment. Why shackle them with Palm Court arrangements and nondescript, interchangeable routines? (The Coward songs are far more dependent on a sense of period, so it is perverse to offer a beat arrangement of "Dance Little Lady" in a disco setting.)

What we hear of the Cochran lavishness is belied by what we see in William Chappell's production. Peter Rice's sets centre on a pair of rickety steps and a receding series of false proscenia; a starry sky-cloth is visible when romantic atmosphere is required. Four choreographers are listed, of whom Lionel Blair registers most strongly, having masterminded an invigorating tap trio danced to Kern's bouncy "She Didn't Say Yes." (Otto Harbach gets programme credit for his equally bouncy lyric, which is nice; it would be nicer still if somebody actually sang it.) But Mr Blair is also responsible for a sad staging of "Dancing on the Ceiling": in Lorenz Hart's lyric, the heroine clearly states that the hero "dances overhead / On the ceiling near my bed." Only the last three words appear to have been heeded by Mr Blair, whose danseur merely twirls about on the deck in unbecoming blue-grey pyjamas. There is nothing for it but to amend the lyric:

He dances round and round,
Both feet firmly on the ground,
Drably clad,
Silly lad.
I whisper "Get aloft, my lover,
Climb the wall;
And let me, snug beneath my cover,
Watch you fall."
I find my ceiling bare:
Put the blame on Lionel Blair,
And on my love.

Most of *Cockie*'s faults are concentrated in the first act. After the interval it develops the elusive rhythm, essential to revue, whereby the good numbers give pleasure in themselves and build confidence for what follows. The orchestrations get richer, Mr Rice's costumes more opulent, and at last, in a medley from *Bitter Sweet* (another step towards the full-dress revival for which the whole West End scene has been softening us up over the last five years), a performer takes full possession of a song: Avril Angers quietly and steadfastly sings "If Love Were All," which is the one, probably Coward's best, about only having a talent to amuse, and stills the house until it roars again.

So, in a different way, does Max Wall, who comes on next to closing, ostensibly to sing "Birth of the Blues." If the bulk of the show suffers from its uncertainty of attitude, alternately lecturing and cajoling us, Mr Wall has no such problem. He treats us with outright disdain, a disdain all the deeper for being edged with dismay. Terrible things happen to Mr Wall while he is on stage. He is goosed from behind the curtain and he falls over. (He says) a hole in the stage, a misadventure which prompts him to put the dread, unanswerable question, "How desperate can a comedian be?" As it happens he cannot spare much of his hauteur for us since he needs it for the orchestra, an insubordinate assembly which is plainly trying to give premature birth to the blues; at least, they are finishing long before he is. With his staring eyes and death's-head grin, the patient mask of a man at the end of his tether, this Wall, this sweet and lovely Wall, is a genius. And since, forty years ago, he was a Cochran discovery, he justifies the show.

*Won't You Stay for Breakfast?* (Apollo), which is a good notch and a half above the recent run of commercial farce, enables Derek Nimmo to demonstrate the depths that can be found within a well-drawn stock character. He is a bumbler, but a bumbler of principle and no fool; within two minutes of curtain-rise we have seen him cook suki-yaki, soothe a troublesome sister, and circle a room all more or less at the same time. No one else in the play has the same opportunities, but Nimmo and his director, Ray Cooney, are model craftsmen, meticulous but without appearance of spontaneity. Mr Cooney also wrote the play, in collaboration with Gene Stone.

The Young Vic revival of Harold Pinter's *The Caretaker* is admirable in its lucidity and its avoidance of cheap laughs. But it also misses some perfectly legitimate ones, thereby reducing the play's range and in the end its stature. What remains is a tight, bleak tale without resonance.
