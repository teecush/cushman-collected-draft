Gay times for the CIA

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on two very different products of the American theatre.

FOR their new experimental season at The Place, the Royal Shakespeare Company have abandoned the steeply banked arena seating that they adopted there two years ago. The actors now face the audience conventionally head-on; most directly they face the critics who, on first nights, are lined up in front in what must look from the stage like Murderers' Row.

This week a new American comedy called Section Nine came up before the bench which was seen, at various points in the evening, to shake with laughter. There were other points, though, at which it trembled with irritation.

Philip Magdalany has given us the latest specimen of doomsday comedy, a genre whose rules were firmly established some years ago by Dr Strangelove. An apocalyptic global threat is posited, generally with a severe time limit, and the action consists of frantic attempts to defuse it. In this case an absentee scientist (a multiple Nobel prize winner and refuser; some kind of genius) has the vital formula tattooed on his penis. Since it is only legible when he has an erection, the CIA faces a double imperative: (a) to find him--no easy task since he has had plastic surgery (on his face, stupid, go wash your mind out with soap); and (b) to arouse him. This also is no cinch, since the discovery that his girl-friend of three years standing has been keeping elaborately mechanised government tabs on him has turned him off women and American Intelligence has apparently no homosexual agents to call upon. It has, however, a system for creating them--the eponymous Section Nine.

The induction into this bizarre training-scheme of three reluctant anti-heroes is, to some extent, the matter of the play, though to exactly what extent Mr Magdalany never quite decides. He has a lot of plot-threads to handle, and somehow he has to weave them all into a second-act situation out of Feydeau. All the characters are brought together in a bath-house (some more logically than others) and proceed to chase one another through its cubicles as unrestrainedly and amusingly as if they were in a French hotel. But Mr Magdalany cannot maintain this farcical vein for very long, and his ending, designed to establish a connection between sexual and political up-tightness, is a mess.

The crappiness of the text is reflected in Charles Marowitz's production, which is crisp and soggy by disconcerting turns. The cast, like many intelligent British actors, give off a generalised impression of knowing all about America (especially in its Terry Southern States), but fall down on embodying it in detail. Their performances are consequently patronising, as if they have pre-judged the characters. Harry Towb, who hits an accurate note of buttoned-down hysteria, is an exception; so, to an extent, is Geoffrey Hutchings as the most craven of the recruits. But even Colin Blakely, after a splendid introductory grin, makes a generalised sort of general. David Waller adapts his usual steam-rollering performance to a Senator from Texas (I go by the LBJ stetson rather than the accent), and two lady agents are turned into fair movie stereotypes by Judy Geeson, the ever-loving, and Margaret Whiting, the ever-lusting. Just about everyone in the play is some kind of spy, and the merest ashtray, daffodil, nipple or ear-lobe may hide a bugging device. The piece has post-Watergate ambitions, though hardly post-Watergate significance; coming out of The Place to be confronted by the billboards ('Bitter Battle for Agnew's Job') I realised that Mr Magdalany could hardly compete.

The King and I (Adelphi) belongs to an altogether more assured period of American theatre, and this is its strength. Oscar Hammerstein's well-carpentered succession of scenes, perfectly underpinned by Richard Rodgers's music, are the confident product of an age when musicals were generally expected to be good. Nowadays we go anticipating the worst and even the best musicals show the strain. (Consider the vertiginous brilliance of Company.) Roger Redfarn's production, brought down from Billingham and scenically and economically under-nourished, nevertheless communicates this authority; The King and I is a surprisingly homespun musical (and more accessible to a British cast than any other short of My Fair Lady), but it provides one spectacular opportunity--the Siamese children's ballet The Small House of Uncle Thomas--and this is excellently taken, both by the dancers and the designer Terry Parsons, who suddenly burgeons in silver and black.

By the standards of the 1950s the show took risks; by any standards it takes diabolical liberties, notably by persevering, a full half-hour after the last song has been sung, in killing off its hero as a lachrymose gesture towards artistic integrity. (Rodgers and Hammerstein, who have been mistakenly credited with bringing the Broadway musical to life, in fact came nearer bringing it to death; they were applauded for their daring in disposing of their villain in Oklahoma! and it went to their heads.)

Actually Peter Wyngarde's King dies as splendidly as he has lived, confronting the Western values in which his authors have such touching faith with dapper, bewildered authority. The scenes of his education suggest an unexpected source for Peter Shaffer's account of the meeting of two cultures in The Royal Hunt of the Sun. But if the King is excellent, a dignified clown, I is a definite disappointment. Anna should stand up to his majesty with the force proper to a Victorian governess, widow and mother--as Deborah Kerr did in the film, as Gertrude Lawrence must have done on Broadway. Sally Ann Howes never stands up to anyone; all her lines are sent straight into the auditorium while she smiles beatifically like a golden-haired doll from The Sound of Music. (That stage in the degeneration of Rodgers and Hammerstein--a path paved with the best of intentions--was still to come.) Still, she sings beautifully and the songs are the evening's real justification. Rodgers is the nonpareil of theatrical composers and the serene, floating waltz Hello Young Lovers represents him at his best (as the candid benevolence of Getting to Know You shows him at his worst).

The Howff in Regent's Park Road is probably London's pleasantest theatre; you sit at well-spaced tables, eat good food, listen to a balalaika duo and drink enough wine to forget the play, a dismal piece of domestic in-fighting called Wait till the Sun Shines, Nellie. The acting, though inevitably tarred by cliche, is efficient and the direction adroit.
