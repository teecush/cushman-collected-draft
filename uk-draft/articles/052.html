Back to the kitchen

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

THE most urgent task confronting me this week is to estimate the profits and losses incurred by the National Theatre's Saturday Sunday Monday on its transfer from the Old Vic to the Queen's.

First, the theatres themselves. The Queen's, having a narrower proscenium opening, is able to offer the Priore family snugger, and therefore more convincing, accommodation than the Neapolitan mansion which they perplexingly inhabited in the Waterloo Road. Donna Rosa's kitchen is now on a scale befitting a conscientious housewife rather than an under-equipped restaurateur; and even though the dining-room is still decorated in the blood-red tones which I associate with symbolist productions of Ibsen, it too has contracted enough for the family to get properly to grips with each other.

They have a harder job, though, to get to grips with us. I missed the cosiness (not a pejorative word) of the Old Vic auditorium, and the friendliness engendered by a group of actors whom one had grown used to seeing on that stage for the past 18 months. Permanent companies are always being lauded for the benefits they bestow on their members; nobody ever mentions how pleasant (never mind about aesthetically rewarding) they can be for audiences.

Of that original team only six remain. (Higher figures have been reported in some newspapers, but do not be fooled.) The casualty-list includes Laurence Olivier's maniacal hatter and with him has departed much of the impact of the play's best running gag. Some glee has also departed with Denis Quilley's accountant, though his replacement, Stephen Greif, is still sharp and friendly enough for all reasonable purposes. Besides, Joan Plowright, ferociously fragmenting macaroni at the end of the first act, supplies as much culinary passion as any play could hold. Not that there is anything joyous about Miss Plowright at this point; her attack on the macaroni is a silent raging protest.

There is a fervour in Miss Plowright which was missing last year and which now offsets what I then took to be the excessive gaiety of Franco Zeffirelli's production. This remains full of excellent farcical routines, but they no longer camouflage the play's pain. They rather intensify it; most notably in Frank Finlay's performance of the head of the house, which I do not think the author himself, Eduardo de Filippo, could better. Mr Finlay plays a man hugging a grievance; he is accordingly quiet and watchful, darting thunderous glances at his many troublesome relatives. Sometimes he comes far enough out of himself to mount a verbal attack, short, stinging, and masterfully timed. The timing is of course the actor's accomplishment and we delight in it as such; part of the joy of the play is its utilisation of traditional theatrical skills. But the expertise never obscures the line of the performance, erupting into rage and scarcely subsiding even in regret.

The cast changes have blessed Mr Finlay with two suitably volatile offspring (Celia Gregory and John Hug) to maintain the illusion of a family out of the Commedia Napolitana and with a delightfully gangling nephew. (David Firth, his predecessor in the role, was also fairly delightful, but he did not, if my memory serves, gangle.)

The Taming of the Shrew (Shaw) also betrays the influence of the Italian commedia, a tradition of which the director, James Roose-Evans, obviously possesses much knowledge which he has used deftly but also a little pedantically; as if it were there to do us good rather than to make us laugh. It can be rather exhausting; Biondello, the play's comic messenger-boy, becomes a tight zany, smiling and skipping about the stage with a head full of bubble curls, like a half-crazed version of Fotherington-Thomas, the immortal swot of St Custard's.

The production, as is now customary, retains the introductory figure of Christopher Sly the tinker (Peter Bayliss, touchingly bemused), but departs from modern tradition by jettisoning him at the point where his role ends in the Shakespearean text (most productions go back to the source-play for material to keep Sly on stage throughout). This presumably is the moment for the main action to take on a life of its own, but it does so only fitfully. Nicky Henson is a splendid Petruchio, alternatively reflective and rumbustious; he goes into the taming business with some diffidence, scarcely fooled by his own bravado. Really, though, his fears are needless; there is little conviction in Susan Hampshire's shrewishness (though much in her ultimate surrender, which is subtly and tantalisingly played) and she scarcely needs subduing. So the action seems for once as sadistic as it is commonly, and groundlessly, held to be.

Another comic tradition was revived this week at the Royal Court, where it has been spasmodically at home these 20 years. Ken Campbell's The Great Caper belongs in a line of elaborate wordy farces with everything to recommend them except humour. They are generally concocted by dramatists earnestly dedicated to entertaining the populace, and it is sad to see Mr Campbell (whose Roadshows really were entertaining) fall into their company.

The play is set in the recent past (at any rate it begins with a scene in Strand Underground station, apparently fully operative) and it concerns a trio of characters with interlocking obsessions; since they truly do interlock it is useless to accuse Mr Campbell of formlessness. The themes--a fascinated abhorrence of technology, a compensating drift towards the occult--recall those of Heathcote Williams's AC/DC, which is drily referred to as "that lively piece." This seemed to me the drollest line of the evening, though there are other good throwaways up there with it: Richard O'Callaghan parenthetically remarks of his girl-friend's boy-friend that "he was away, but he was 6 ft 6 in. if he came back." The piece is terrifically well performed by Mr O'Callaghan, as lively, not to say febrile, an actor as ever twitched; by Mr Campbell, who looks lugubrious; and by Warren Mitchell, who hardly ever shouts. The sets (cut-outs among the cut-ups) are by Bob Ringwood and they are nice, too.
