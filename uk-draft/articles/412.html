First, the bad news

THE Broadway theatre is in bad shape: official. The news was broken in the New York Times on the first Monday of 1983. Statistics were flourished, among them the fact that 15 theatres were dark.

A commonly reiterated view is that admission is too costly for theatregoers to afford. A more rational one is that the prices merely make them selective, and that recently there has been precious little for them to select. Even so, two productions managed to play to audiences of more than 100 per cent, a feat accomplished by counting those standing up. Of these, one was the held-over 'Dreamgirls,' the other the season's only new hit, 'Cats.'

When 'Cats' opened in London, there was a thrill in discovering that English performers could dance. Of Americans though we expect it; the show uses some of the best dancers in New York, but leaves them looking underemployed, even if continuously active. Singing, though, is far better than in London; Betty Buckley gives a piercing account of 'Memory'; her Grizabella is a creature of emaciated sensuality, though her apotheosis seems more arbitrary than ever.

Where the production has been elaborated, the results are unfortunate; Mungojerrie and Rumpleteazer have been put into clown-costume and are not even allowed to sing their own song; in 'Skimbleshanks the Railway Cat' -- musically the best number -- the train is now made of objets trouves rather than of the performers themselves. This signalises the show's main loss: good fellowship. We can take a local pride in Trevor Nunn's showmanship, while hoping that he won't make a career out of it.

'Cats' was a sell-out in New York before it opened; they were selling mementoes last summer, long before there was anything to mement. Now in the lobbies they sell everything, even the book: 'Buy the poems,' cried the hucksters, 'by T. S. (a quick look down) Eliot.' Broadway programmes are institutionalised and Thomas Stearns Eliot (Poet and Lyricist) duly gets his biography, just ahead of Andrew Lloyd Webber (Composer and Co-orchestrator).

The New York theatre is raining dogs as well as cats. 'Snoopy' is the second small musical to be based on the 'Peanuts' strip; since the mutt was the de facto star of the first one, it seems reasonable to give him a show of his own.

A sturdier mini-musical, due in London soon, is 'The Little Shop of Horrors,' which is real trashy and means to be. Who could resist a man-eating plant that sings soul? Nobody, that's who. (We are told in a final recitative that 'the plant achieved its purpose, which was to eat Cleveland.') Its inventor, a Bowery Faust residing at the double unlucky number of 1313 Skid Row, christens it Audrey II after his girlfriend. Audrey I, a dumb blonde, is a tour-de-faiblesse for the actress Ellen Geer.

With these last two shows, we have wandered off Broadway. Back on, there is 'Nine,' which last season wrested most of the Tony awards from Michael Bennett's 'Dreamgirls.' 'Nine' is also the creation of a gifted choreographer who has apparently foresworn choreography; Tommy Tune's staging, stylishly set and elaborately costumed, ranks very high as mobile sculpture.

Dancing is kept to a minimum; a number called 'Folies Bergere,' which most directors would festoon with high kicks and ostrich feathers, is here restricted to a series of languorous arm movements by the seated ladies of the chorus who comprise most of the cast. The result is to make the scene dreamily effective if bewilderingly irrelevant. This, though, is a trouble that pervades most of the evening, and one which goes back to source.

The show is based on Fellini's '8 1/2': a much overrated movie, but at least it gives you the director's narcissism neat. Somebody else trying to tell his story is in trouble, mainly because he hasn't got one. 'Nine' gives us the conceit of the hero (an unappetising blank as played by Raul Julia) presiding like a conductor over the vast orchestra of women in his life. At the end he is reconciled with his wife: a happy ending (like that of 'Dreamgirls' and indeed of 'Cats') about which it is impossible to care.

Some of Maury Yeston's music is operettishly attractive (to my chagrin: I was looking forward to reporting that the only Good Tune was the director) and Mr Yeston also wrote the lyrics. The hit number ('Be Italian/you rapscallion') stays with you however much you wish it would leave.

A widely noted phenomenon is that Broadway, like the West End, prefers to get its dramas pre-tested. Currently this means buying British.

Sometimes, though, Broadway's liberal conscience will support a play that would not survive commercially in London. Athol Fugard's 'Master Harold . . . and the Boys' has had a good run. It is the liveliest of his plays, but still suffers from there being nothing Mr Fugard could say about South Africa that we would not all heartily agree with; he is not enough of a poet to make his particular case (in this case, a white youth who has grown up on intimate terms with black servants and is now pressurised into rejecting them) take on general significance. There was a performance of expected dignity from James Earl Jones, but the other acting was weak.

That often happens in New York. It afflicted Jonathan Reynolds's 'Geniuses,' an established off-Broadway success that, in the sage judgment of Variety, would have been a natural Broadway choice before economic panic set in. It certainly belongs to the favourite Broadway genre of showbiz satire, being set in the Philippines during the shooting of a megalomaniac movie which could hardly not be 'Apocalypse Now.'

It is really an extension of the scene in 'Once in a Lifetime' in which the captive East Coast playwright rails at Hollywood and all its works, and it hardly amounts to more than sporadically effective joking at arbitrarily assembled figures; not at least until the third act when Morgan Fairchild takes control.

Miss Fairchild is a soap-opera queen whose bio reads like a joke. (She has played 'paranoid murderess Jennifer Pace Phillips,' 'warped actress Dulcie Warwick' and 'spoiled bitch Constance Weldon Carlyle.') Playing a blonde starlet of limited mental horizons, but unlimited sexual ones, she is the realest person on stage, and the play takes wing when she is allowed, at remarkably inoffensive length, to beat up a macho set designer.

A more complicated revenge play is Christopher Durang's 'Sister Mary Ignatius Explains It All For You.' For most of the play Sister Mary gives a simple interpretation of Catholic dogma: a hilarious parody, but an easy target. Then some of her ex-pupils intrude, and the play rollercoasts. Nancy Marchand, a crisp, mannish actress, is fearsome and funny in the title role.

The nicest trend is late-night intimate revue, usually staged in or above restaurants. 'Forbidden Broadway' sends up the theatre by rewriting familiar songs to pillory successful shows, actors and (most often) actresses. Spotting the tunes and the victims affords low-level enjoyment.

The best joke (to the tune of 'Well, Did You Evah') is at the expense of the lyricist-director of 'Annie': 'Annie's closed and Charnin's blue/I hear he's copying some revue.' In fact Martin Charnin's copy, 'Upstairs at O'Neal's,' betters their original. For one thing, it has a wider range of subjects and writers; for another it has an insolent punkish style in staging and performance; for yet another it has Mr Charnin's own best lyrics, one of which, called 'Stools,' is addressed, devastatingly and ungainsayably, to the one prop without which no revue can do or be done.

What is enviable about these shows is that they assume, and attract, a knowing homogeneous audience. New York, unlike London, is still theatre city.
