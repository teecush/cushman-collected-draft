Breathing life into 'Ghosts'

THEATRE

by ROBERT
CUSHMAN

WHEN Ibsen selected the title Ghosts he made an unconscious prophetic joke; none of his plays is harder to bring to life.

We are all, of course, far too sensitive and sophisticated to believe that the play lost its validity when science came up with a cure for syphilis. We have been told, and can appreciate, that the disease which destroys Oswald and which he inherited from his father is merely a figure for the destructive influence of heredity, tradition, conventional belief, and all those bequests from the past which are never likely to be vanquished by a shot of penicillin. We can see, too, that the true carrier of the disease is Oswald's mother, Mrs Alving; and that it is all the more virulent in her because she imagines that she has fought free of it.

We can see it, but in performance it is usually very difficult for us to feel it. We are aware instead of portentous dialogue, obviously heavy with symbolism; why else would the actors pace their lines so funereally, weight them so oddly, and look so intently past one another's shoulder-blades? Should we want something to look at, the stage will generally be heaped with heavy Victorian furnishings, though these may be somewhat obscured by the dim light that seems to have pervaded Norway throughout the last century. In these circumstances it is impossible to take the action seriously, let alone to admit its significance. The atmosphere becomes not so much obscure as oppressively muggy.

Jonathan Miller's enthralling production at Greenwich clears the air, as decisively as Ingmar Bergman's Hedda Gabler (and with rather more respect for the text). Like Bergman, he has refreshed our senses with a non-realistic set; Patrick Robertson has enclosed the play in a huge string cage, somewhat resembling Josef Svoboda's setting for the National Theatre's disappointing Three Sisters. The abstraction that proved fatal to Chekhov works wonders for Ibsen; uncluttered and clearly lit, the play is set free.

There is nothing abstracted, however, about the performance itself, even in the woollier passages of the script. Ibsen's exasperating vagueness concerning Mrs Alving's scandalously adventurous literary tastes (talking of Advanced Books as if they were an accredited series like Penguins) is generally underscored by a shocked inflection from Pastor Manders: "You read books of this kind?" In Robert Stephens's reading of this line, the emphasis is on the word "you"; attention is directed away from the author's mannerisms and on to the relationship of the characters.

This discreet understatement is wholly typical of Mr Stephens's performance, which amounts to a major job of rehabilitation. The author's credit is saved with the character's, for the tiresome old buffer we usually see could have been created only by another tiresome old buffer. Mr Stephens's Pastor is liable to clasp his hands at preacherly comments, but he is personable and intelligent enough to be forgiven that; only as the play progresses do we come to realise the abrupt and painful limits he has set to both his intellect and his emotions for the sake of his self-esteem. This being his ruling passion, a performance that has avoided caricature attains in the end to high comedy when he tumbles into Engstrand's blackmailer's trap. At this moment, too, Antony Brown - who has timed Engstrand's Dickensian hypocrisies with sly individuality - suddenly and memorably ceases to be funny.

A similar, though more complex, balance is struck between Manders and Mrs Alving in their long scenes together. Remembering the past they also relive it; Irene Worth is particularly acute when she recalls the Pastor's earlier rejection of her. A wiser woman now, she watches him (watchfulness is indeed the keynote of her performance) intently and amusedly. When he mentions ideals, she repeats the word, tastes it on her tongue, chews it over as a good Ibsen heroine should. When the Alving orphanage, the supposed embodiment of those ideals, burns down, she responds with an uncontrolled burst of bitter laughter: the boldest stroke in the production (and perhaps, all told, the most tactful way to greet such a bludgeoning stroke from one's dramatist). Only at the close does Miss Worth's intelligence betray her; invited to perform euthanasia upon her son she objects, "I gave you life," as if it were a reasoned debating-point rather than an instinctual cry.

The end still packs a Grand Guignol punch; Peter Eyre's Oswald is a young man with the gaunt looks of an old one (freezing rather than burning up) and he gets full value from his marvellous simile for softening of the brain: "Like cherry-red velvet curtains." Hardly less gruesome is the family carousal with Nicola Paget's Regina keeping a distrustful distance, stiffly nursing a glass of champagne; her inevitable progression into her father's brothel is one of the most clearly charted lines in the production.

Mr Miller's version of Measure for Measure (a National Theatre Mobile Production, which last week fetched up for a matinée at the Old Vic) is less admirable, though it boasts in Julian Curry's murderously prim Angelo a study in uptightness to rank almost with Mr Stephens's. Here too, as in Ghosts, are long duets played with a minimum of movement and a maximum of lucid flexibility. Mr Curry's attempted seduction of Gillian Barge's Isabella is hardly finer than her subsequent encounter with her brother (David Bradley), whose disobliging inclination to save his life at the expense of her honour is rewarded by a vicious swipe from her handbag.

This, you will gather, is a modern-dress production; its Vienna is not merely post-Shakespearean but post-Freudian. Escalus (a dignified performance by John Gregg) wears a frock-coat, Mariana is a 1920s vamp in black dress and beads (with matching cigarette) and Angelo is timeless in pin-stripes. Some characters are amusingly transformed: Mistress Overdone is not the usual raddled old bawd, but a chic, dykey coeval of Norma Levy, while Pompey (a sharp performance by Neil Cunningham) is, to match, a thin-lipped, red-tied Soho ponce.

The action passes in an all-purpose waiting-room, which is yet too detailed to be accepted as neutral territory; outdoor scenes look very odd there and the final dispensation of justice "without the city gate" is so cramped as to be meaningless. The show is cramped in other ways too: it is under-projected (only Mr Curry is compellingly audible throughout), over-solemn (our most revered directors, from Peter Brook on down, apparently regard Shakespeare's farce as beneath their notice) and under-populated (Juliet, the girl whose pregnancy sets the plot in motion, is reduced to an offstage voice).

The end is very strange; Isabella greets the Duke's proposal of marriage by backing away in horror, and the general mood is, in P. G. Wodehouse's phrase, if not actually disgruntled then far from being gruntled. I think myself that it should be almost wholly gruntled. Of course my views are governed by preconception and prejudice since I have dreamed of directing the play myself, but would it not make sense to see it as a progression by all the characters (notably the Duke, Angelo, and Isabella) towards an acceptance of the love they all reject at the beginning in the name of principle or ideals? Could it not be, in fact, that this is a play in which the lesson so grimly taught in Ghosts is actually learned?

Further performances of the touring Measure for Measure: Nuffield Theatre, Southampton University, 2 February; Old Vic, 5, 20, 21 February; Uppingham School, 16 February.
