Comedy in the round

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

CONVERSATIONS WITH AYCKBOURN
by Ian Watson
Macdonald £8.95

THIS BOOK is an extended interview sustained, according to the acknowledgments, by 'several memorable meals.' Ian Watson points out that Alan Ayckbourn is the only major playwright in this country not to have been accorded a critical monograph, but seems to feel little inclination to fill that gap himself. The reason, he suggests, is that Ayckbourn is 'temperamentally incapable of standing still long enough for critics to pigeon-hole him' but, to the limited extent that that is true, it also applies to plenty of other writers.

The real difficulty Ayckbourn presents to critics is that his plays are so unquotable; he gives you less on the page than any other notable dramatist. I can recall numberless hilarious moments from 'Bedroom Farce,' but if I started transcribing them you wouldn't believe me. Everything depends on inflection and on situation. Credit to the managements who have seen the plays' potential; although of course to a great extent Mr Ayckbourn is his own management, trying out his own plays at the Stephen Joseph Theatre-in-the-Round, to whose direction he annually devotes the 50 weeks he doesn't spend knocking off a new comedy.

Critics have not, I think, really been blind to what he is doing. He has been writing drawing-room comedies which acknowledge -- without getting hysterically assertive about it -- that times have changed. To a great extent one praises his plays in negatives: he does not foist wisecracks on characters who would never utter them; he does not draw back from confrontations; he does not steer his characters towards artificially happy endings; he does not give them prettier homes than they could afford; he does not tell lies. All this is true both of his 'light' and 'dark' plays (it is a little disconcerting to find that he makes this distinction himself -- I thought it was just a reviewers' game).

All the same he does give us trouble; we recognise his achievement but are unsure how important it is. 'Absurd Person Singular' and 'The Norman Conquests' both made me laugh a lot, but I found their sombre elements both over-calculated and over-rated. 'Bedroom Farce' captivated me, and repeated viewings increased my respect for it. It keeps the audience amused for two hours, making them aware as the lights fade of the cost of the action in battered lives: something comedy writers always say they want to achieve but rarely do. It may well turn out the classic English play of the 1970s.

Since then I have been generally on Ayckbourn's side (a position made easier by the loutishness of most attacks on him); but I have to say 'generally' because the sheer volume of his work is making it difficult to judge his plays individually. This does not mean that he would write better if he wrote less, but that the plays are beginning to assume the aspect of a continuous tapestry.

Seeing his plays in situ at Scarborough has helped. Some of the best passages of this book deal with the establishment of this theatre by Stephen Joseph, the quirky English prophet of theatre-in-the-round, and Ayckbourn's inheritance of it. He characterises it, quite rightly, as a community theatre, dedicated to new writing and ensemble acting, and it's a pleasing irony that these aims should have been achieved in a milieu execrated by those who usually profess them. ('I'm on a crusade to try to persuade people that theatre can be funny; but one must start doing that; some bugger from the Left comes in and tells them it's instructive and drives them all out again.')

If Ayckbourn never wrote a word he would still be vitally important as a theatre director -- though it's his writing that enables his theatre to function. He has, incidentally, an unrivalled theatrical grounding as actor, technician and everything else; and he turns out, unsurprisingly, to be an unpretentious, wary, illuminating and entertaining talker. There are unexpected appearances (he has lovely tales to tell of Phil Silvers, the great neurotic comic who starred in 'How the Other Half Loves' on Broadway) and equally surprising absences; Eric Thompson, who directed most of his commercial successes in London, is never mentioned. A book of interviews is usually a disgrace; this one is worth all the critical examinations that haven't been written and most of those that inevitably will be.

Addendum, characteristic and revealing, from 'The Norman Conquests' -- 'I think other people's marriages are invariably a source of amazement. . . . I have found quite often it's the people you look at and say, well they won't last long, who cling on grimly till death. Maybe they're so aware of public opinion, they're determined to prove it wrong.' I found that at random. So he is quotable.
