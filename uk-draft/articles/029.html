In praise of 'Chez Nous'

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

MEMORIAL ENTERPRISES, who are presenting Peter Nichols's Chez Nous (Globe), have asked the critics not to reveal a couple of surprise developments in the first act. After all, they reminded us, we kept mum about Sleuth and our forefathers did the same for The Mousetrap.

However Chez Nous is neither Sleuth nor The Mousetrap. It is the best new play in London and the best its author has written. It can withstand a few critical indiscretions, and, in the cause of intelligible discussion, it will have to. All I can do, as with The Wolf last year, is to issue a caveat: you may prefer to see the play first and read this review (which you will of course preserve, with reverence) later.

Well, how did you enjoy the play?

You will remember that it concerns Dick and Liz (I am not sure whether Mr Nichols intends mythological overtones), who invite their old friends Phil and Diana to stay with them in their farmhouse in the Dordogne. (The bit of it we see looks like a de luxe barn; it ranks as one of the two best sets in the West End, along with that created by the same artist, Michael Annals, for - most appropriately - Design for Living.)

The association of these two fortyish married couples stretches back to their single days. The only significant blemish on Robert Chetwyn's production is that it is sometimes difficult to accept this fact. Liz and Diana are said to have gone to school together, but Diana is the fastidiously mannered Geraldine McEwan, whom I cannot imagine at school with anyone except Glynis Johns, Joan Greenwood and Fenella Fielding, plus occasional trips to stay with Maggie Smith at weekends. She certainly seems to share no audible point of origin with Pat Heywood's determinedly placid and ordinary Liz.

On second thoughts, and with an effort, I can accept that both these ladies, whatever they may have been like as young girls, are as we now see them because of a process of choice, conscious or unconscious. Diana, with her bleached, wire-drawn frame and her dreams of elegant ravishment by some suave Scott Fitzgerald playboy, forced herself to grow up into Geraldine McEwan; Liz settled for comfortable domesticity and wound up believing that, sunning herself down on the farm, she could feel like Mrs Tolstoy.

Given these predilections it is unfortunate that Liz's marriage should have turned out the remunerative one. Dick is a paediatrician who has struck it rich with a book which someone has providentially persuaded him to entitle The Nubile Baby (Mr Nichols's other jokes are better than that, but less easily quotable). His attitude to his success, and to its source, is complex. He professes to be pained by the title, and by the sexy schoolgirl photo which adorns its cover - of which, however, he keeps a large blow-up on the premises. He knows he should despise money and fame but he enjoys the attention, having always resented his own bashfulness. In particular he resents the fact that his wife is the only woman he has had; surely there must be more to sex than he has known with her. So in The Nubile Baby he rails against conventional sexual restraints and, of course, against the family. Which is ironic, since not only is his wife family personified, but they have three children of their own.

Only they have two. Their new baby son is actually their grandson begotten on their 14-year-old daughter (first surprise) by old pal and present guest, Phil (second surprise).

Now about Phil. He is an architect, though we don't hear much about that; we hear more about his habit of taking his wife to jazz clubs (not her idea of a glamorous good time), where he invariably ends up sitting in on banjo. He wants a child; when they married she was pregnant by another man, and after the birth she had herself sterilised. His discovery that he has a son sets up a battle for possession of the baby which comes to dominate the play. Given something to fight for, Albert Finney's roused performance takes on extraordinary strength; this is his definitive roaring boy.

But it is Dick on whom the play finally closes in; for funnier, for sadder, Mr Nichols has here developed and enriched the themes of loneliness and aching regret which he adumbrated in Forget Me Not Lane, and A Day in the Death of Joe Egg. Denholm Elliott gives him occasional surges of joy (as when, making a hopeful leap at adultery, he sings out - and him a specialist - that children are really very boring) rising from a sea of angst: the resigned agony of a man who has reached the middle of his life and the end of his tether simultaneously.

At least two scenes should make the history-books: a terminal silent solo by Mr Elliott, and a central quartet of recrimination which plays like classic comedy. I must mention Denis Carey's enchanting old villager who turns out to serve a purpose beyond local colour; and Mr Nichols's magisterial use of solid objects (the blown-up schoolgirl, a pram, a swing) to focus his themes. Formally this is his least obviously experimental play, and his most successful.

David Mercer's Duck Song (Aldwych) is, by contrast, vociferously unconventional and a failure. We were repeatedly told before the opening that this play represented Mr Mercer's break with naturalism and, more surprisingly, we continued to be told so on the first night. The break turned out to consist of the furniture which filled the stage in Act I disappearing, more or less in a puff of smoke, by the beginning of Act II.

From where I sat this was a distinct gain in terms of sight-lines, but the inexplicable catastrophe - to which the characters never ceased referring, as if they could hardly credit their author's audacity - proved less successful in clarifying the play's inner landscape. Regret and recrimination were again the themes; but it is difficult to take much interest in the baring of souls which have never inhabited credible bodies, whether of Socialist cracksmen or idle painters living on inherited wealth. The acting is nothing much, though David Waller is fruity, Elizabeth Spriggs creamy, and Gareth Hunt, as a Red Indian, impressively stony. David Jones's direction assumes some odd shapes, but they are probably unavoidable.

Adrian Mitchell's Mind Your Head, set on a bus, has now been furnished at the Shaw with an afterpiece, James Saunders's Double Double, set in a bus-drivers' cafe. I doubt if much of the dialogue would actually be encountered there, but it is very satisfying stage-rhetoric (the subject - again - the decay of youthful dreams), excellently delivered by Lesley Joseph and a memorably enraged Sam Kelly.
