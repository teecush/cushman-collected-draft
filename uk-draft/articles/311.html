Busy beaux

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on new productions by the National Theatre and the R.S.C.

I WONDER if Peter Wood was scared as a child by a particularly bad production of a Restoration comedy.

When he directs one of them himself he seems to have in his mind the memory of two actors on an empty stage in pretty clothes just talking, talking, talking. Mr Wood has no intention of letting this happen in his own productions, which are extremely busy. In his staging of Vanbrugh's The Provok'd Wife (Lyttelton) actors have crossed the stage on floats, tavern songs have been carolled, before we even get to the first scene, which Vanbrugh wrote largely as a brace of soliloquies for the discontented married couple, Sir John and Lady Brute, but which is here transformed into an exchange of recriminations across the breakfast table.

An exchange between the two beaux, Constant and Heartfree, is enlivened by having one of them surprise the other at shaving. Even more mobile than the actors is the scenery. When Sir John, disguised as a woman, is on the run from the watch, the stage suddenly sprouts a set of chimney tops on which to conclude the chase. It is a charming effect, as are most of the others, but it does nothing to make the scene funnier. It fractures the rhythm.

The adornments have the curious effect both of prettying the play up and damping it down. At its black heart the play is the study of a misalliance from which neither party has the means or the courage to break. Watching this sad state are another pair, Heartfree and Bellinda, wondering whether to risk their intelligence on a similar venture. Watching them is an envious flirt, Lady Fancyfull, playing an outdated sexual game in which no one will join her. And in the outermost circle are the servants, pursuing their own intrigues with a jaundiced eye on their masters'.

The pattern takes some time to emerge here, though Mr Wood finally brings the threads together in an ice-ballet - the happiest of his scenic inventions - on the frozen banks of the Thames; no director is better at elaborate Restoration finales. By this time, too, his actors have come through. Peter Bourke and Brenda Blethyn supply a cynical ground-bass as the domestics, Michael Kitchen as Heartfree escapes from his earlier stolidity to let loose some wry calculations on the worth of a bachelor's freedom, and Dorothy Tutin, posing before the longest of mirrors, turns Lady Fancyfull (Affected? moi?) into a precursor of Miss Piggy, though unfortunately without the same volcanic temperament.

Sir John Brute seems written to be a squat and stupid tyrant; John Wood, being of a different shape and temper, misses out on much of the humour, but supplies some of his own. He analyses his own plight with a masochistic sharpness (and, as far as I could tell, with actual lines) not to be found in the original text; when scorned by his wife's lover his stupefaction speaks for all the cuckolds in comedy. John Gielgud once defined style as knowing what kind of play you're in; Mr Wood goes further and tells us.

There is a similar awareness in Geraldine McEwan's Lady Brute, whose command to her swain, "into the closet," in its sophisticated weariness, is itself worth the price of admission. Miss McEwan has the advantage that her author meant her to be intelligent; with Nicky Henson's admirably flexible Constant and Lindsay Duncan's Bellinda, she makes up a splendid trio of quizzical good sense.

The evening's highlight is a scene in which the two women lie together discussing their respective amours while Mr Kitchen tosses in another chamber on the other side of the stage. Two beds opposed: the picture could hardly be more apposite.

At almost exactly the same time, but across an ocean rather than a town, the Puritans who, to stretch a point, had left England because of plays like Vanbrugh's were building in Massachusetts the civilisation that was eventually to be the subject of Arthur Miller's The Crucible (Cottesloe). The Provok'd Wife was premiered in 1697; The Crucible is set in 1692. Now here they are, side by side, but still keeping their distance.

Bill Bryden's production of The Crucible is puritanical in tone as well as subject: sometimes achingly so. The first scene, in which it is determined that the teenage girls caught dancing in the woods are under the influence of witchcraft, is taken with an extreme slowness that should serve to highlight nuances, but in fact flattens them out.

The great compensation is that the piece emerges as something more than the drama of one brave man against the crowd. John Proctor, the enlightened farmer, is of the crowd, as much concerned as they with personal righteousness and tormented by his own failings; Mark McManus, choking back bitterness, is here finely partnered by Dinah Stabb as his wife, a walking conscience. There are other strong performances by J. G. Devlin and Tony Haygarth.

The girls were historically in their early teens. Mr Miller made them 17 and 18; most productions cast them in the twenties. I don't know how old Mr Bryden's actresses are, but they look and sound about 14, which adds to the horror of the story, but produces some toneless renditions of key scenes. It also underlines the author's equivocation about Abigail, the ringleader. She is not, considering her part in the action, onstage very much; but the plotting seems to ascribe to her almost sole responsibility for the Salem inferno. Other factors are hinted at but not explored. The subject in fact cracks out of the form, though this tension may be partly responsible for the play's undoubted power.

If Mr Miller warns against hunting witches, David Rudkin in Hansel and Gretel (Other Place, Stratford on Avon) counsels against ignoring them. His comic guignol version of what was already a pretty terrifying story has it in for everyone. Children are horrible, disruptive things whose parents wish to be rid of them. After dramatising, in Ashes, the misery of not having children, Mr Rudkin turns to the misery of having them. There's no pleasing some people. Mummy is worse than daddy; she turns out to be the witch's heir. Gretel is worse than Hansel; she collaborates, and deserts her brother.

The twin totems of Margaret Thatcher and Mary Whitehouse have made misogyny politically respectable, and both are duly invoked. The witch (a smashing performance by Brenda Bruce) informs her victims that she doesn't want their productivity impaired. "We," she says, "are people of quality, we take tea on the lawn." Invited to address a school she warns against the frightening effect of such stories as that of the Three Little Pigs, however salutary its message of self-help. (I detected, at this point, some hopping from hoof to hoof.) Barbara Kinghorn as the witch's daughter, Olive, who is literally faceless, grunts and squeaks as engagingly as her namesake, Popeye's girl, but on the whole, not having caught Mr Rudkin being funny before, I am not crazy to catch him at it again. For scares, however, he remains unrivalled.
