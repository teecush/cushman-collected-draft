Dog Days in Vienna

ROBERT CUSHMAN visits an unusual outpost of British drama.

JUST two Sundays ago Simon Gray made his debut as a director with, as you would expect, one of his own plays.

What you might not expect is the location of his debut. It took place in Vienna; not, though, on one of the Austrian national stages, where Pinter and Stoppard are regularly unveiled in German translations under the batons of their original British directors. The Gray corpus has been seen in Vienna at a much smaller theatre, and in English.

Just about the first thing you see in the Vienna airport, if you have flown in from London, is a strategically-placed poster for Vienna's English Theatre. If you seek the theatre out, you find a modestly plush 250-seat house, described in the programme, with a proper respect for geography, as "the only full time, fully professional producer of English drama east of Great Britain." With the exception of the Donovan Maule Theatre in Nairobi (which makes a surprise appearance on the Spotlight list of British reps) it is British Equity's farthest outpost.

Its guiding spirit, however, is an Austrian named Franz Schafranek, a director (and former actor) married to an American actress, Ruth Brinkmann. She was born in Berlin, but her family came to America as refugees from the Nazis when she was three. It was, however, in Vienna that she met her husband, whose career had already taken in a year with Brecht's Berliner Ensemble and had most recently been working successfully in Sweden.

This, however, was one place where Miss Brinkmann had no desire to live; she wanted either to stay in Austria or to go back to America. Schafranek favoured Austria, but finding work there for an American actress was a problem. So he created some. In 1963 he presented her opposite Anthony Steel (remember Anthony Steel?) in a production of Dear Liar, the two-hander based on the letters of Shaw and Mrs Patrick Campbell.

He hoped for an audience of English-speaking tourists and found one. He also got rave notices in the Austrian Press. In the succeeding years he repeated the experiment, though for some time with less encouraging results. He was now offering plays of which, unlike Dear Liar, his audiences hadn't heard: an adaptation of the Edgar Lee Masters narrative poem Spoon River, or Gallows Humour by Jack Richardson, once regarded (along with Albee, Gelber and Kopit) as a promising American playwright. Only the memory of Dear Liar kept him going. (You might call it Brinkmanship.) Finally he hit on the right format: "modern classics."

From 1969 to 1973 the project operated in a basement theatre in Vienna's Amerika Haus. In 1974 it moved into its present quarters, in a building in the Josefsgasse, owned by the Teachers Association of Vienna, who undertook the renovation of the auditorium. It now operates a year-round season. (By his own account Schafranek never takes a holiday. He says that he tried two years ago, but found that after four days he couldn't stand it.)

Nine or 10 shows are put on every year. And the audience has grown far beyond the tourists. The Viennese come. (It is comforting or disconcerting for the British visitor to Vienna to find that everybody speaks English to him.) There is a subscription audience of 6,000. There is also a subsidy; between the Austrian Government and the city council the English Theatre receives £120,000 this year. By Austrian standards this is pretty small lager; total theatre subsidy in the country amounts to a hundred million. Between them, the Vienna State Opera and the Burgtheater (National Theatre) receive £45 million. As well as its home activities, the English Theatre mounts touring productions of set-texts for schools. Not Shakespeare though; nothing too elaborate.

The programme in the Josefsgasse is pretty much that of a British rep: Ayckbourn, Coward, Wilde, Neil Simon. In 1976 Schafranek brought off something of a coup by directing the world premiere of Tennessee Williams's The Red Devil Battery Sign. Last month, the English Theatre paid its first visit to London, when the Half Moon housed its production of another play by an American veteran, William Saroyan — the distinctly odd Playthings, directed by Mai Zetterling.

There is no permanent company; plays are cast individually in Britain. Like many people, Schafranek mourns a shortage of British directors: "You end up with television directors, and they hire television actors, and nobody can hear them." Top-line actors are hard to come by if you're not paying West End money, but every so often he nets some kind of star.

Joan Fontaine (remember Joan Fontaine?) did excellent business, though her unbudgeable choice of play (The Lion in Winter) was, Schafranek freely admits, "terrible." Anna Neagle, Siobhan McKenna and Peter Wyngarde have also appeared on English Theatre playbills, in very large type. Schafranek believes in promoting his assets. I doubt if any author has received more prominent billing than has Simon Gray on this occasion; his name as playwright, actually eclipses his name as director, not to mention the play itself.

The play is Dog Days, staged at the Oxford Playhouse in 1976, but never seen in London. Gray was persuaded to direct it after three other of his plays had been done in Vienna. This being his first production, he was especially nervous on the first night. (It really was the first night. There had only been a public dress rehearsal, attended by all the ambassadors in town. Embassies in Vienna are very hot on cultural patronage, and seating them according to their seniority is apparently a regular headache. The solution is to give them their own preview, letting them in on a first-come, first-served basis, and damning protocol.)

Reckoning on a quiet debut away from his compatriots, Gray was surprised, and not pleased, to find British critics in attendance. The production was rough-edged in some respects, but it was a great improvement on the rather dingy version in Oxford. Time had helped too; the play was written before Otherwise Engaged and there are similarities which make it look like a trial-run.

In both plays the central character is a publisher with a crumbling marriage, and with a shambling schoolmaster brother to act as a foil. In Otherwise Engaged the fraternal relationship is pushed to one side of the play. In Dog Days it is central. If the publisher here is not exactly Alan Bates, he has some pretensions to worldly and amatory success; the drift of the play is to prove that under the skin the brothers are — well, brothers.

The wit is less glacial than in Otherwise Engaged since the protagonist is much more obviously on the defensive; the play glitters less but touches more. Michael Byrne, who achieved his first success playing opposite Bates in Gray's Butley, is splendidly nervy in the lead.

Gray has carefully labelled the play "a period piece," mindful perhaps that though life in Muswell Hill and Chalk Farm, his chosen locales, has changed little in the past decade, it is fashionably considered bad form to write about it. Gray has a broad-faced resemblance to Howard Brenton and seems to occupy much the same position in fringe-theatre demonology as Brenton does on the left. The Viennese audience seemed puzzled by the play; its nuances are perhaps too English even for the English Theatre. But this tourist was glad he went.
