Putting Henry James on stage

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'The Aspern Papers'

THIS year's Chichester programme includes two plays from the West End season of 1959.

I imagine that '59 is seen as a haven of reassurance: the last year before the barriers went down and the Visigoths of the sixties started fanning out from Sloane Square, writing their new plays, forming their new companies and building their new theatres -- Chichester, for example.

I can get quite nostalgic about it myself. 1959 was the year in which I began regular theatregoing, and I remember it as the last date on which the commercial theatre still had a substantial share of the best new plays. The Aspern Papers, Michael Redgrave's adaptation of the Henry James novella, was among them; though at the time I was less taken with the play than with the acting of Flora Robson, Beatrix Lehmann and Redgrave himself.

The Chichester revival would be justified purely as a seventieth birthday tribute to one of our greatest actors, but it also reveals him as having written an almost perfect play: probably a minor classic. It must certainly be the shapeliest of all attempts to put a novel on stage; it unfolds in five scenes, not one of which is weighed down by more narrative than it can plausibly carry.

To a peeling Venetian palazzo comes Harry Jarvis, an American literary sleuth, on fire with the scholar's passion. His quarry is a near-centenarian expatriate lady, once the mistress of the deceased Jeffrey Aspern, H. J.'s own candidate for the role of great American poet. Hugging Aspern's literary and emotional relics to herself, the lady is appropriately dragonish. H. J. proposes to circumvent her by making love to her middle-aged spinster niece; and since he has all the charm of indifference (the pursuit of letters having absorbed his sexuality) and she the vulnerability of seclusion, he seems set fair to succeed.

He does actually get his hands on the papers, only to be surprised by the aunt. "You publishing scoundrel," she calls him -- obviously the filthiest term in her vocabulary -- and drops from the excitement. The treasures pass to the niece, who makes it discreetly plain that he may have them only as part of her dowry. He jibs at this; maybe he would change his mind, but he is not given the chance. The Aspern papers go into the fire.

As usual with James, the American, who thinks himself the sophisticate, proves the innocent; he may plan emotional blackmail, but it is the Venetian ladies who practise it. That he gets even as far as he does is to the credit of his ambitious and unscrupulous Italian valet: a good supporting performance by Keith Drinkel to balance an uneasily patronising one by Irene Hamilton as the hero's conductress.

Cathleen Nesbitt is (as who would not be?) a less-terrifying old lady than Miss Lehmann, but she is also a wittier; she gains her victories in hand-to-hand combat. Her big moment of discovery is badly mistimed in David William's production. The particular heights of the original performances -- Redgrave's suavely brutal disengagement, Dame Flora's brief blossoming, when taken out for the evening -- are missing too. But there is a compensating strength of detail in Kenneth Haigh's calculating brashness and in Jill Bennett's, who has the recluse's efficiency as well as her innocence. When the pair at last elude each other, the emotional geometry is exactly outlined. And the climax, with Miss Bennett in effect burning her lover's child, reminds us what a good Hedda she was, and also of how zealous an Ibsenite was James himself.

Irish Eyes and English Tears (Theatre Upstairs) begins, in time-hallowed fashion, with a group of young roughs harassing an old tramp. Just to prove that this is 1978, he is busking (singing the title song in fact) and they are on their way to a football match. One of them, who runs with the pack out of discontent but is discontented with that too, saves the old man from being beaten up and strikes up a friendship with him.

Brian Hayes plays the tramp, Karl Johnson his protector; both maintain a constant sense of surprised awareness throughout the evening. They are strongly supported; at half-time I found myself wondering what would happen to these actors when they grew up. The author, Nigel Baldwin, turns out to have similar preoccupations; his young loner is merely a younger version of his old dosser and in fact ends the play like him, settling down for the night wrapped in newspaper.

The young, in fact, are older than we think; one of the best-drawn characters is an unmarried mother of 31, still playing the games of her previous decade and earlier. "Teenagers" you think when you first look at the cast; they aren't, but there is nothing else they want to be. The highspot of Ian Kellgren's production is a scene on the terraces: the cast bunched together, each over-reacting in his own way, with the tramp, taken along, standing dazed among them. The gallery set looks amazingly like a prison. The West End seems to have latched on to the documentary craze as once was, but at this date, and at these prices, who will care?

Mr Baldwin chips incisively away at his subject; his lines ring true and make patterns. But he is not yet a builder; too much happens between the scenes, with a crucial reversal in the interval. The tramp is adopted by the rest of the gang; spruced up at a jumble sale, he is unrecognisable as his former self -- and in fact the more we learn of his descent the less credible it seems. The kids meanwhile interact convincingly and compellingly (Annie Hayes and Alfred Molina are especially good) grabbing us more securely than the foreground action ever does.
