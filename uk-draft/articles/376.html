Party and passion

ROBERT CUSHMAN on plays by Sartre and Steve Gooch.

SARTRE'S The Assassin (Greenwich) is about as good as a play can be while remaining strictly unbelievable. It is the piece written just after the war that the British theatre used to know as 'Crime Passionnel,' but whose French title was 'Les Mains sales'; 'Dirty Hands' has never been thought to be much of a come-on for home audiences.

Anyway, the assassin who gets his hands dirty through what may have been a crime of passion is Hugo, a new-born Communist of middle-class origin whom the party sends to liquidate Hoederer, who is supposed to be one of their most prominent own, but is suspected, with good reason, of compromising with the enemy. (The class enemy, stupid.) We are in an unnamed small central European country, of the kind so useful to parabolically-minded playwrights, and the war is on. The themes of collaboration, betrayal and violence are those which were rattling around France at the time and for years afterwards. It now seems a shame that Sartre did not set the play firmly in his own backyard.

He does, on the other hand, make distance work for him. The most impressive aspect of the play is the remorselessness with which Sartre dissects his own party. As often happens with left-wing playwrights, the most sympathetic figure on stage belongs to the opposition: no mere bourgeois (how could you think such a thing?), but the ancien regime incarnate: a prince indeed, written (and played here by Stephen MacDonald) as affably imperturbable.

This of course is mischievous devil's advocacy of the kind any playwright might permit himself. The pain comes when Hugo, having accomplished his mission, emerges from prison to find that his comrades have now adopted the principles of Hoederer, whose fault, it is now revealed, was merely that of being ahead of his time, out of step with history. (In other words, directives from Russia have changed their tune.) Hugo the idealist is stranded, bitterly and forever.

All that is likely. What strains credulity is that the young and totally untried Hugo should be trusted with such a mission in the first place; that having been introduced into Hoederer's home as his secretary, he should take his wife along with him; and that he should reveal to her the full details of what he plans to do. She is a teasing, unawakened girl who calls him 'my little honey-bee.' She persuades him to ask Hoederer to defend himself intellectually; and this Hoederer does, producing a highly convincing defence of his heretical opportunism. Hugo is not convinced, but he still surrenders to hero-worship, as does his wife to downright love. Hugo discovers her in Hoederer's arms and, finally, he shoots. Just which of them he was jealous of remains an open question.

You can see why Sartre, meshing personal and political passions, needed the characters of Hugo's wife and of the doctrinaire girl at HQ, who loves him, but he hardly animated them; nor did he do much better with Hugo himself. A playwright of ideas can bring characters to life, but only if they express not so much his own beliefs as his own temperament. In Sartre's case the tougher the better. A coldly intellectual production--such as we fondly imagine to be typical of the French--might make all viewpoints equal; Frank Hauser's direction, unremarkably human, reinforces the biases. James Simmons flails as Hugo, promising more than he currently delivers.

The play perks up when a pair of bodyguards appear, one (Richard Cubison) sullen, the other (Michael Melia) engagingly flip; and erupts when Edward Woodward comes on as Hoederer. He has the practical confidence of a successful trade unionist, the lilt in his voice under control but dominating the stage. The arguments between Hoederer and his murderer--pragmatist against intransigent, worker against theorist--are strongly, even excitingly done, but we already know all the arguments. It is a sad augury for political drama that not even Sartre can surprise us.

Acknowledged or not, he is the father of the British playwrights who arrived in the late Sixties and early Seventies and who have specialised in polemical melodrama. Of them all Steve Gooch has remained the truest to his roots; that is to say he has continued to write chiefly for fringe or touring groups. One of these--Solent People's Theatre Company, operating out of Southampton--has arrived above the Crown and Castle pub at Dalston Junction with Mr Gooch's Fast One.

A disillusioned adventurer (written cockney, but played scouse) turns up with his barmaid girl-friend in another unnamed country--South American this time, time and the theatre having moved on--and commences running guns to whoever wants them. He can't really tell the difference and neither--since Mr Gooch's dialogue and characterisation here lag rather far behind his action--could I. It is a good thing I don't demand uplift from my drama; there seemed to me nothing to choose on the evidence offered between military oppressors, moderate opposition and revolutionaries. But Mark Donford-May's production achieves one signal triumph; its sub-tropical characters neither look nor sound ridiculous.
