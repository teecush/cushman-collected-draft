Enter the Barbican

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the RSC's first productions in its new home.

I HAVE nothing to add to other reports on the Barbican complex. It does combine the attractions of airport and subway station; you can get lost following the bright directional arrows. I only want to add that the main entrance reminded me of a Trust House Forte hotel and that so, unfortunately, did the catering in the Waterside Cafe.

The main theatre, though, is a triumph; in the fanned auditorium you are excitingly aware of being part of a large house in contact with both actors and fellow-spectators. I don't know how the stage will take, say, Chekhov; but a proof of its present success is that 10 minutes into the opening production of Henry IV I had forgotten the occasion and was following the play.

Or plays: we get both parts. I have an image of the opening of Part One, derived more from bad dreams than from any particular production. A king sits on a wooden throne, stage centre, flanked by three or four equally wooden courtiers. He looks out-front and talks vaguely about being tired and wanting to go on a crusade: then he looks left and interrogates his cousin Westmoreland about the doings of a host of unseen characters with even less inviting names. It all sounds very historical. A couple of scenes later, if we are lucky, the play begins.

Trevor Nunn's production actually capitalises on the divided nature of that first speech, refusing to slough it off as mere exposition. Patrick Stewart as Henry does the crusading half surrounded by clerics. He is clearly racked by remorse and by compensating religious fervour. Words relating to longed-for domestic peace get the works. Then the scene changes and the king is in the council-chamber, facing rebellion. And so, throughout both plays, it goes: minute exploration of the text and at least one detailed set-up for every scene. Between scenes, too, Mr Nunn is concerned to keep England before us: segments of the population regularly make pilgrimages across the stage, often wearing cowls. Half the country appears to be in orders.

Behind them is John Napier's set, a towering timbered maze. It recalls Nicholas Nickleby, as does much of the production. There are bursts of song; we end with a hymn; there is a manic chase-sequence; actors overpeer scenes in which they do not take part; Falstaff's Shrewsbury recruits look like graduates of Dotheboys Hall; the chorus of Rumour is divided among the company, a line apiece. These are details, but the aim seems to be to involve actors and audience together in the creation of a national panorama.

There is some wonderful small-part playing along the way; actors of the calibre of Bernard Lloyd and Griffith Jones are liable to turn up as sheriffs or carriers, and somebody unnamed makes a character of an ostler who in the text only says 'anon, anon;' and that offstage. Anything in the plays that can reasonably be called Dickensian--the daily life of tavern or stable--is fully realised. But Nickleby was a staged novel and needed our collusion; we had to feel that the actors were, in some sense, making it up as they went along and needed our help. We know who wrote Henry IV.

It is not so much that the detail swamps the plays. Mr Nunn has a firm grip on what Henry IV is about; and he presents with great clarity a struggle in Prince Hal to choose between two fathers, his own and Falstaff. Joss Ackland makes a really fine Sir John, vocally rich, adept at button-holing the audience (the Barbican stage seems fine for this), and increasingly unscrupulous: shocked by the ruthlessness of the age, as exemplified by the treacherous John of Lancaster, but still entering into it. To him belongs the show's one moment of greatness: a laugh after Hal's rejection speech, as if this were one more inexplicable practical joke.

Gerard Murphy's Hal, forever embracing the old man, clearly has a struggle to escape him; he plays the first reconciliation with the king as a partial, angry thing. This presumably is to explain why there has to be a repeat performance in Part II, but it implies a strangely linear view of human affairs; relationships in life double back on themselves and have to be repaired.

Mr Murphy eschews the usual lyrical approach to the 'golden care' speech about the crown; he hammers it, physically and vocally, with an emphasis I associate with players who know, somewhere, that they are doing it wrong. (The whole Bolingbroke clan are prone to hysteria, reaching psychotic levels in Prince John.) Hal's other main antagonism goes for too little, since Timothy Dalton makes an obstinately lightweight Hotspur with no chest-notes.

I am tempted to describe the event as an elaborate frame too sparely filled. But that would be to ignore a host of minor splendours: the cannon-shot that heralds the last fight of Hal and Hotspur; the entire battle, the first one worthy the name in years; the frantic irruptions of Mike Gwilym's pistol-packing Pistol and the insolence of Miles Anderson's Poins; the contrast in tavern scenes, the first rowdy and Hogarthian, the second beautifully contemplative; the revellers in Shallow's orchard all crowded on a single bench; Mr Lloyd's suave Glendower and the interplay of the Percy family; Falstaff's habit of playing to the crowd; three performances by David Lloyd Meredith. This is recognisably our best director working on the most capacious plays in the language. Only by the standards expected of such a collusion is it disappointing.

The Barbican's studio theatre, the Pit, is a spruce subterranean gymnasium, where things have commenced with Peter Flannery's Our Friends in the North. Actually the opening line of this is 'there is no beginning,' and certainly there seemed to be no end. This is less a comment on the play's considerable length than on Mr Flannery's purpose to convince us that political life--at local, national and international levels--is an untieable knot of corruption.

The past 15 years are his time-span, London, Newcastle and Rhodesia his foci. There are some character-links, but neither they nor his dialogue are strong enough to hold the strands together. The man probably meant as the protagonist has an unfortunate tendency to merge into the crowd. The actors who do best are George Raistrick as an atypically honourable cop and Jim Broadbent as a great ooze of a businessman.

You have to admire Mr Flannery's tenacity, and he is rarely glib, but his journalistic method (more short scenes than both Henries put together) is too thin to stop comparisons mounting. The publicity cites David Edgar's Destiny, but that play kept surprising itself, whereas you feel that Mr Flannery knew precisely where he was at before he sat down. And then the analogies become wilder. Episodes in Soho pornotopia recall Fings Ain't Wot They Used To Be; the northern featherbedding John Arden's long-ago Workhouse Donkey; police cover-ups the current W.C.P.C.; the conspiracy theories Illuminatus, all fantastic works, but all, in their insobriety, more convincing.
