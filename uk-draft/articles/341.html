Back to Arden's Dance

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and at the Toronto Theatre Festival.

MY GENERATION was trained to consider Serjeant Musgrave's Dance a masterpiece done to death at its premiere in 1959 by philistine clodhoppers.

The truth, as revealed in the Cottesloe revival, is slightly less colourful, but only slightly. Colour, as John Arden knew very well, is one of his play's strengths: red uniforms on a black-and-white coalfield. The contrast works superbly on Peter Hartwell's set with its gleaming curtains and cyclorama, dotted with bare, significant props as specified by the author. Indeed John Burgess's lucid production is a paragon of fidelity; even to the point of rashness.

According to prescription, Annie, the village barmaid, drops her tray with a banshee yell. If she seems merely like a waitress suddenly taken queer, that's the author's fault. So is the fact that she and her employer both speak in folk-ballads; part of a playwright's theory rather than of a play. Mr Burgess's colliers don't get drunk enough, but he organises action sparely and well, especially the final dance of vested interest that displaces Sar'nt Musgrave's own.

He comes to Yorkshire from colonial service, sickened by reprisals exacted in a ratio of five to one, and determined by the end to exact the same from the sheltered guilty folks back home. Whether he has it in mind all through is an open question; his author doesn't seem to have decided whether he's decided and this confusion is the play's main flaw.

Musgrave's boasted logic derives from military life and the Old Testament; there is indestructible logic in John Thaw's performance, building a thoroughly convincing avenging angel out of a thoroughly convincing NCO.

Weaving his pattern of pacifism, revenge, love, industrial strife, Mr Arden never drops a stitch; every line has function and flavour. 'This town's wintered up,' says the Mayor; the compression makes the fancy writing of today seem more indefensible than ever. The closing note of fatalistic optimism (characters doomed, cause not) is beautifully judged. In an excellent cast Peter Sproule, Patrick Drury, Ron Pember, Philip Jackson, Leonard Fenton, Michael Gough and David Troughton are standouts.

The trio in Harold Pinter's The Caretaker (Lyttelton) are now West Indian. They have no particular problem with the rhythms, but they get little fun out of the play and only a prefabricated idea of tension. 'This ain't my bag,' says Norman Beaton's downtrodden tramp, and he never spoke a truer metaphor. It's a trudge of an evening.

Britannicus (Lyric Studio, Hammersmith) is more of a funeral march. Christopher Fettes's production catches Racine's sense of omnipresent trap, but little else. A pedestrian translation gives the actors little excuse for emotional flights, but they attempt them anyway, shouting above the odds approved for so small a space. In quieter moments Jonathan Kent's Nero pouts; Siobhan McKenna's Agrippina booms; she comes on first in red, down to her stockings and shoes, then in a dress of, I thought, green-tinted silver paper. Britannicus, shortly before being murdered, has been given a dance expressing misplaced confidence immediately preceding a line expressing misplaced confidence. Isn't irony a wonderful thing?

The play is given a police-state set, for no good reason; not all 'tyrannies are alike.' Compare Ben Jonson's Roman tragedy Sejanus, which is crawling with spies and informers and, if not crying out for revival, is emitting the odd insistent murmur.

Theatre in Canada was born, according to official reports, in 1953 with the founding of the Shakespeare Festival at Stratford, Ontario, which now occupies the position of a National Theatre: that is to say something to be regarded by various factions at various times as an object of pride, complacency, embarrassment or contempt. But it has served as the model for a string of civically-financed repertory theatres across the country--all calling incidentally on the services of the same small corps of leading actors.

There is also a lucrative touring network and, especially in Toronto, a proliferating small theatre movement, with some similarities to the London fringe, but rather more to off-Broadway. It has a commercial as well as a subsidised sector, and some producers work both sides of the street.

Such is the backdrop for Toronto's first theatre festival: an attempt to show off Canadian theatre in an international context, perhaps even to force a golden age. Four days were occupied by a business conference tailored to Toronto's mixed economy (there is more money there, public and private, and more enthusiasm than in almost any other theatrical capital).

The Habima Theatre came from Israel and Ekkehard Schall from East Berlin. There was a bunch from Britain. There were gratifying reactions to Hull Truck (down atypically to one actor, the frightening Alan Williams with his 'Cockroach' trilogy) and most especially for Shared Experience: the inspired story-telling of their 'Arabian Night' (the erotic one) was greeted as a revelation of what theatre is actually for. Those of us who have been saying precisely that about them for the past five years can--and indeed did--feel smug.

Mabou Mines, currently the most idolised experimental group in Manhattan, presented 'Dead End Kids,' a baleful look at nuclear power: an atypical subject for them, and indeed for the whole New York underground, which has scarcely touched politics since the Sixties. With facts, figures, choreography and special effects precisely marshalled, it was harsh, electric, and insistently un-benevolent.

And there were of course Canadian shows: about 50, covering the established groups and others who seemed to have sprung up for the occasion. There was, perhaps, rather more ferment than achievement. The biggest native hit was the local equivalent of 'Anyone for Denis?', 'Maggie and Pierre,' whose author, Linda Griffiths, appeared as both Trudeau and as a linking journalist called George: sometimes she held conversations with one of her selves, a confusing idea, since she played all three characters Jewish (and Barbra Streisand sing-song Jewish at that). With the possible exception of the Quebecois Michel Tremblay, there are no world-class Canadian playwrights, but there are a clutch of promisings.

George Walker, whose 'Zastrozzi' was seen at the King's Head a few Christmases ago, specialises in mannered spoofs and his 'Theatre of the Film Noir,' adapted from the French and taking off from post-war Bogart, might have been a delight had the cast had any idea how to play it. The group responsible, Factory Lab, are said to have fallen into a rut. ('For every play,' said an ex-employee, 'we have to hire Nazi uniforms.')

The most intriguing piece I saw was semi-professional, and questions of acting standards hardly arose. The idea was all. It was called 'Tamara' and it took place in a house. Not a set representing a house: a real mansion, through which the audience could wander at will, confident of finding a scene in progress in any room. These scenes added up to a play, set in Fascist Italy (a black-shirted guard saw us on to the premises) and concerning the life, loves and household jealousies of Gabriele D'Annunzio. The dialogue sounded commonplace, though the organisational feat was considerable; the furnishings, perfectly detailed, were what mattered and more fascinating than any stately home because we knew they were a stage set. The kitchen was a particular triumph, and in the interval we gathered there to consume almond cream gateau that for some of us marked the artistic peak of the festival.
