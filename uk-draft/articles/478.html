The Trouble With Thrillers

THE climax of Alan Drury's Sparrowfall (Hampstead) is the death of a comparatively innocent man. Just what special providence has decreed the fall of this particular sparrow is difficult to determine; though the motives of Mr Drury's characters - greed and self-protection - would seem to be conventional enough, it is difficult to come at them while the play is in progress. The form interposes. The play is a thriller, but not the kind that is usually found in the theatre.

If Time Out magazine is to be trusted (though its preview article laid so much stress on the play's extremely incidental music that I arrived at Hampstead half expecting a rock-musical), the influences were Alfred Hitchcock, Howard Hawks and (some inconsistency here) B-movies. But Hampstead has a small stage, and so one is more inclined, watching the play, to think of the small screen. Mr Drury gives us a business setting - his main character is something big and nasty in investments - and the effect is rather as if The Brothers had grown brains and sex organs.

Mr Drury's dialogue is studiously light and steely, and John Chapman's production matches it scrupulously. Nobody is allowed to betray any emotion except fear. As for the performances, they are well-tailored and well-groomed; collectively the cast displays the finest head of hair to be found on the London stage. The designer, Tanya McCallin, has divided her stage into a series of battleship-grey compartments, allowing the action to progress from flat to office to aerodrome with no immediate signs of strain.

There is strain, though; the play, production and design are faultlessly in style, but their success is self-defeating. Even in the hands of Hawks and Hitchcock the thriller is a pretty empty form, telling us things about human corruptibility which we knew already. The writers may supply smart dialogue - so does Mr Drury, at least some of the time - but nobody goes primarily to listen to it. Our object is to watch, and both cinema and television give us more to look at than the theatre ever can; even when the scene is stationary, the camera moves. Watching a single multiple set, however elegant and ingenious, is comparatively poor fun; all the action has to take place in corners and one aches to see the action take possession of the stage.

This play is the successor of the comic-strip pieces which a decade ago were going to save our theatre from bourgeois naturalism; they never get beyond the one-act lunchtime circuit because, by definition, they lacked staying-power. The form saves the playwright the trouble of exploring his characters; though Mr Drury's The Man Himself, which has been turning up at six o'clock performances in the National's Young Vic season, shows that he has the desire and the ability. In The Man Himself he exercises them in the simplest available form - the monologue - but he gives promise of becoming a public writer who can depict people as they are rather than as he would like them to be.

Emigres by the Polish writer Slawomir Mrozek, who has been absent from our stages for several years. He has been away from his homeland even longer, and this piece eventually supplies some piquant thoughts on the predicament of the expatriate. I say eventually since, at least in Kevin Billington's production, the play takes an unconscionable time to get going. Jim Norton, who is sort of intellectual, and Brian Cox, who is sort of thick, share a room in some city foreign to both of them; unfortunately both they and their environment seem very British, which saps the base of the play.

They might be in any old two-hander, and from time to time I was driven to think of them as two halves of the same character: a notoriously desperate critical gambit which proved as unhelpful here as it usually does. However, when Mrozek gets more specific about them - when each is forced to consider the possibility of the other being an informer, when Mr Norton forces Mr Cox to realise the unreality of his dreams of returning home and when Mr Cox half-heartedly and amusingly attempts suicide to Mr Norton's considerable impatience - both text and performance sharpen up.

Alan Badel is the leader of the lost generation of English classicists who give the St George's Playhouse its main reason for being. Appropriately, he is now appearing as Richard III. His Richard is less a devil than a driven imp, rolling downstage to deliver his first speech as a deadpan joke on the audience, and winning his way to the throne more by cheek than imposture. (This is reasonable: Richard, unlike Iago, is trusted by almost nobody.) His eyes are his tensest feature; they are hypnotically fixed as he waits to see how his victims will respond to his poison; always there is the chance of failure.

Richard's disabilities allow Mr Badel to act, fascinatingly, at an incline; even when he has reached the throne he hugs its side. As an ironist Mr Badel is in the Olivier class; though the St George's acoustics are cruel to his throwaways; he can never secure a full-throated laugh. This is the more unfortunate because he falls short of Olivier in passion and in vocal, as opposed to physical, command; there seem to be no heights or depths to his voice, no bark, no ring. He is best in the prolonged cat-and-mouse game he plays with Richard Gale's Buckingham, or in the briefer contest with Lynn Farleigh's Anne, who ends up actually flirting with him; weakest in the frenzy of the end, by which time he seemed to be acting by rote. Most of Richard is there, but you have to dig for some of it.

Douglas Campbell's production is a very enjoyable piece of storytelling in the Guthrie tradition; sometimes for worse (there is a spectacularly but pointlessly doddering Archbishop), but often for better. Richard's court is presented as a vast comfortless ante-chamber, the courtiers going on cold continuous progresses around the enthroned protagonist. The same idea works less well for the Bosworth ghosts, ceaselessly wandering at one and a half times normal speed (the approved theatrical rate for spectres). The cluttered facade of former productions has gone (and with it some of the Elizabethan theorising in the programme), leaving only the church's gothic pillars. These and the platform stage enable the production to look good even when its sound is muffled. Rosemary Leach's Elizabeth is the most commanding performance; and much of the personal interplay is on the road to being interesting.

The Pleasure of His Company (Phoenix) is, I suppose, well-named; it exists to introduce us to Douglas Fairbanks Jr, who is dapper if not exciting. But there is hardly scope for excitement in Samuel Taylor's comedy which, though written in the fifties, has now been set in the thirties, so that its anodyne unreality may pass for period charm - prompting the thought that if Mr Fairbanks had brought over a genuine antique of the period (Philip Barry or S. N. Behrman) the lines could have been wittier, the sets just as plush; the fans would not notice the difference, and the rest of us would have a little more to keep us awake. As it is Peter Dews's production is the slowest thing imaginable; Mr Fairbanks reduces its pace to a stroll, Dinah Sheridan (though hers is the most conscientious performance) takes it down to a crawl, and Wilfrid Hyde White finally stops it in its tracks. The pungent satisfaction with which he surveys its corpse - as if he had drowned it in dry Martini - is the most amusing thing in the evening. Mr Hyde White refers to himself as inhabiting an ivory tower and addresses his grand-daughter as "person." It is that kind of play; at least it would like to be.
