In the marriage trap

Robert Cushman · The Observer · Review · 1975-12-21

LAST year I attacked a production of a Franz Kroetz play for transferring its action from Bavaria to London, on the apparent assumption that no audience can take any interest in a situation farther away than its own backyard. This adherence to fashionable dogma effectively destroyed much of the play's fibre.

Now at Hampstead there is another Kroetz play; and from its title Morecambe you may guess that it too has been given a local injection. And there goes one more critical theory, for in this case the text has been so resourcefully transplanted that the play stands and the critic kneels.

Today, of course, the name Morecambe has two connotations, and Jane Brenton's translation exploits both of them. The play concerns a young marriage, and the two partners begin the evening by expressing admiration for Morecambe, near Wise and, associated with this, a desire to visit Morecambe, near Lancaster. It is not made too clear whether this would mean popping into the next town, or taking a long trip by Inter-City. The second sounds more probable; the dialogue and its delivery, in Annabel Leventon's production, are very London.

Whatever the distance, it increases in the course of the play; Morecambe, which would once have been a nice place to visit, becomes an impossible goal. So do a lot of other things. He works as a delivery driver, she in a shop. But their finances are precariously balanced, and an accidental pregnancy is enough to unsettle them fatally. Almost the husband's first thought is of an abortion; the wife resists and though she lets him arrange an appointment she ends up refusing to keep it.

Despite this the tone of the relationship and of the play is remarkably cheerful. A happy marriage is a rare phenomenon in a good play, and given the acting of Lesley Joseph and Philip Sayer one would be tempted to call this a perfect match. Sometimes, of course, it frays; there is a memorable pub scene in which she has to seek him out in the gents (the only place she can get a word alone with him) in order to beg him to spend no more of their substance on drinks for his mates. The very brightness and kindness of the relationship makes the situation hurt the more. In the most memorable scene the two of them, accepting the inevitability of parenthood, go through their budget wondering which items are the most expendable, and knowing throughout that in fact nearly everything will have to be. I don't know how it sounds in Deutsch Marks but it rings fearfully true in sterling. (Though while immediacy is in question: I don't find that when a play touches on my own problems I concentrate on it harder; I am more likely to embark on trains of thought of my own.)

Kroetz may be sorrowing that his characters have wrapped up so much of their happiness in consumer goods, or he may be raging that so basic a thing as birth should prevent them from doing so any longer. Decide for yourselves; the important thing is that you will want to. Both the trap and its inmates are solid and inescapable; the play resonates accordingly.

The difference between Morecambe and John McGrath's Yobbo Nowt, presented by the 7:84 company at the Shaw, is the difference between a created work and an enjoyable piece of theatrical journalism. Mr McGrath also deals with a woman trapped by pregnancy: in her case, into marriage with a man who leaves her some 17 years later with two adolescent kids, no job and no qualifications. Her encounters with the National Assistance lady, very sweet and smiling and deadly, are excellently done as lemony revue-sketches. Eventually she lands work in a factory, finds she is being exploited, and decides to find out about the system that is doing it to her. The investigation becomes, as she says, her hobby; but the more questions she asks, the more predictable become the answers. Suspense disappears; and so, with the leaden cartooning of the one Establishment figure in the show, does conflict.

Meanwhile the vanished husband reappears and is dismissed, our heroine having had her consciousness raised in matters of sex as well as class. Altogether she shapes up into an indomitably faultless heroine, on the lines of The Mother; and though Mr McGrath's play lacks the odious sentimentality of Brecht's it is equally one-sided. It rambles, too, without attaining to the picaresque quality of his earlier Fish in the Sea. It is saved by the spirit of the cast, and especially by their musicianship. The evening is packed with songs, all of them well performed. Mark Brown's music happily utilises a collection of pop styles, and Mr McGrath's lyrics, in this instance, are sharper than his dialogue. An aggressive number disposing of the love generation (if any of it is still around) is particularly good.

The Dream of a Ridiculous Man (ICA) is, as you would expect from the Pip Simmons Group, more like a nightmare. Based, how faithfully I cannot say, on a Dostoevsky short story, it puts an intending suicide to sleep, conjures up for him a series of painful or pleasurable hallucinations, and lets him wake up to the conclusion that life is pretty good after all if approached in the right spirit: sentiments of a singalong, which in fact is how the show ends. All the same, anyone joining in will have been had. There are fangs behind the smile. Even more disturbingly there is a leer.

In the middle of the dream, blending wit and enchantment, is the most idyllic tableau to be found on a London stage: a Polynesian paradise, represented by palms, bare-breasted girls, a couple of doves, a fruit vendor and a zany but harmonious addition--an old man knitting in a deck-chair and a live chicken. The scene is, of course, created only to be destroyed--which would be fair enough except that the cynicism is cranked up in excess of the situation.

Lonsdale's On Approval, revived at the Haymarket, begins as dead drawing-room comedy. Then it moves its four characters to Scotland where the air is much more bracing. Two of them, a rich woman and an impoverished Duke, are intolerable; gradually the two others realise the fact. Edward Hardwicke and Jennie Linden manage decency without dullness (Miss Linden even runs to a little sparkle) but in Frank Hauser's snappy and thoroughbred production the running is naturally made by the two monsters. They differ in approach. Geraldine McEwan, unbelievably white of face and thin of limb, is a terror from the start; but then she can afford to be while Edward Woodward, with only blood to recommend him, has to lace his way into awfulness.

Miss McEwan is like a sea-creature, jealously guarding her own rock, thrashing out with her tentacles whenever someone comes too near for decorous comfort or absents himself too far for speedy service. She can stretch her voice like elastic; some of the things she does with it are indeed scarcely human.

Edward Woodward, with his odd mixture of threat and drawl, has never been funnier. The ending, which maroons the two of them for an indefinite period in a snowbound house, reveals that Lonsdale saw Hell in much the same terms as Jean-Paul Sartre. Concerning the influence of the English boulevardiers of the twenties on the French philosophers of the forties, much work has still to be done.
