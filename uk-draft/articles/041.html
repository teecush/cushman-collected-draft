Nightmare in Nottingham

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

HOWARD BRENTON'S new play at Nottingham Playhouse begins chillingly. The body of Winston Churchill is lying in state in Westminster Abbey; four soldiers, dimly lit, flank the coffin. In a roaring monotone one of them begins to explain the situation to us. He tells us that his mother was moved to tears when she heard of his assignment to so honourable a post. For her Churchill was God.

His rage over her sentimentality--the two co-existing while threatening to cancel each other out--promises an evening which might equal Mr Brenton's Magnificence in richness of texture. The body, apparently, will not remain hearsed; Winston arises, but before he has a chance to say very much the stage becomes fully illuminated, and we find that we have been watching a play within a play. Or rather, a rehearsal within a play; a rehearsal now interrupted by stern outside pressures.

Mr Brenton's full title is The Churchill Play, "as performed in the winter of 1984 by the internees of Churchill Camp"; somewhere in England; Churchill Camp is where the Con-Lab coalition government dumps its political--predominantly working class--malcontents. Somebody describes it as "the English Dachau"; unless the word "English" is meant to convey a certain cynical moderation, that description, on the evidence of what we see and hear, is excessive. The camp hardly compares with the cellars of Orwell's Ministry of Love.

Mr Brenton's vision of the future involves less sadism than Orwell's but rather more practicality; he shows the Army mentality, hardened by years of what is known as peacekeeping work into the attitudes required for the efficient running of a camp. In one of his most memorable scenes a liberal officer is rendered effectively impotent by his contemptuous subordinates in the most proper manner; they simply bellow him into defeat, never once forgetting to call him "sir."

Some of Mr Brenton's best writing has gone into the depiction of Churchill Camp, as well as into the spare evocation of what lies beyond it. The prospect of escape is finally spurned by one inmate in the words "that outside there lies only more of the same"; the grey concrete world over. The bleakness is admirably realised in Richard Eyre's production and Hayden Griffin's set; but though the overall impact is powerful the details are blurred. On a wide and cavernous stage the voices boom and the characters elude our grasp. They may have eluded the author's as well.

For the whole structure is as unwieldy; the play is built on two resonant myths--Churchill and 1984 are talismanic labels--which finally fail to cohere. That Churchill-worship leads inexorably to Churchill Camp is a good debating-point, and one that Mr Brenton might well be capable of proving. All he does here, though, is to get his rocks off, passion declining into aggro (the distinguishing mark of most of his contemporaries). When the internees finally get to perform their play its upshot is that, whatever may be believed by soldiers' sentimental mothers, Churchill did not win the war; "People won the war." True or false, that makes little difference to one's view of concentration camps.

Mr Brenton manages one extraordinary image: "We are told that Churchill referred to his constant fits of depression as 'Black Dog,' and we see him actually communing with this animal (a large, shaggy creature, rather like Nana in Peter Pan)." The didactic point may be that supposed heroes are as vulnerable and pitiful as anyone else, but dramatically the scene wins our sympathy for Churchill (a marvellous ungainly performance by Paul Dawkins), in a manner with which the author cannot have reckoned. The final scene leaves him grappling with a pile of unrealised themes; they explode in violence, but the impression is of a dramatist's desperate expedient, rather than--as in Magnificence--of the action's inevitable resolution.

If Mr Brenton momentarily makes us warm to Churchill, David Cregan in The King (Shaw) makes no such mistake. He is out to debunk King Arthur and he does, remorselessly, for three hours. The trouble is that though he may dislike the Arthurian ethic he is obviously deeply in love with the legends themselves and cannot bear to pass any of them up. But from each one he wrings the same moral--that Arthur's idealism (and, by extension, all idealism) is merely a mask for human, and specifically sexual, inadequacy. He is probably right but I believed him the first time. Some of his jokes at the expense of Galahad, Gawain, and the rest of the Malory gallery are amusing, but not enough. The evening is whimsically interspersed with doggerel songs, in a manner which begins distressingly to resemble a Shaw Theatre house-style; in other respects, though, Peter James's direction is unimpeachable, being lucid, good-humoured, and thoroughly well-acted, with Geoffrey Hutchings a bouncy and resourceful Arthur. The seats are now free.

With Mr Eyre at Nottingham and Mr James about to take over at Sheffield the push reps may be in for a good time. Down from Edinburgh to prove the point comes Bill Bryden to direct Spring Awakening for the National Theatre. Triumphantly he creates an enclosed world. Geoffrey Scott's set is an open plane, occasionally decked by essential furnishings and always liable to be savagely shut off by vast, steely sliding doors. Just so are the pubescent schoolchildren of Wedekind's play crunched by the jaws of German society at the turn of the century.

Puberty in this world has been turned into a particularly forbidding exam; and from each year's crop of competitors the adult world claims a sacrificial quota of failures. That at least is how it appears to young Moritz Stiefel, the more unfortunate of the play's two heroes, whose ineptitude, both sexual and scholastic, finally drives him to suicide. His is a desperate progress, but there is something comic about it, too; at least there should be, but Michael Kitchen's performance is surprisingly stolid, and in general Mr Bryden's production underrates the volatility of the writing.

The only actor to do justice to Wedekind's savage elegance is, fittingly enough, Cyril Cusack, who appears at the end to give a wittily dandified account of the author's own role. (In two senses; the character speaks for the author and the author once played the character.) The other elders include a fine array of monstrous pedagogues, dominated by the rococo rhetoric of William Squire; a pair of equally horrific fathers (one of them sternly depicted by James Mellor in a single line); and two rather more human mothers. One of these is Susan Engel, exuding a gracious sexuality which helps to drive poor Moritz (she turns his head by calling him Herr Stiefel), over the edge.

Miss Engel's performance begins better than it finishes; but there is no falling off in Beryl Reid, whose frightened Hausfrau, hiding her daughter's head in her lap while she fails to tell her about sex, is the most achieved performance of the evening and of her career. Veronica Quilligan is a sweet sight as the girl; most especially when she awaits the fatal attentions of the local abortionist, her frightened eyes peeping above the sheets; Peter Firth is properly cocky as her seducer, the play's survivor.

So much for innocence. Experience is rampant in Vanbrugh's The Confederacy (Chichester) but to little effect. The production is roguish, frilly, expertly manoeuvred, and thoroughly dead.
