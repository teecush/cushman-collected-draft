The best of Bruce

THE acting profession is getting uppity. The first night of The Travelling Music Show (Her Majesty's) ended with Bruce Forsyth coolly informing us that, though we had disappointed him at first, by the second half we had pulled ourselves together and made some kind of audience of ourselves, was how he put it. It had been, he implied, uphill work.

I don't think he knows when he's well-off. Two points should be quickly made. First, I enjoyed the show very much. Second, Mr Forsyth is probably the best thing that subsequent audiences fresh out of their coaches will eat from his hand instead of gnawing at it. If returned, he will presumably get the Bruce of Beat the Clock and The Generation Game. We had something better, something too more in accord with what the show seems intended to be about.

Careful. Those theatre journals which conscientiously reprint the logbooks of assistant directors working on experimental productions in Warsaw might do us all a service if they could let us know just what befell The Travelling Music Show on tour. We were led to believe that it would be a showcase for the collected works of Leslie Bricusse and Anthony Newley. Many of their songs are surtitles (nothing, alas, from Mr Bricusse's first and best score, Lady at the Wheel) but they are neither explored nor exploited. They might be any songs: in fact it is part of the scheme of the show that they should, to an extent, be done badly.

Mr Forsyth plays Fred, a Limelight entertainer and family man, gambolling all but bringing London a revue about itself. He has a supporting cast of five, of whom one absents herself from the premiere. So the show goes on, with the others frantically covering for her. Their plight is amusingly and economically illustrated in an opening number which is brightly staged and has a gap. Throughout the evening we are whisked very smoothly from backstage where the cast are panicking, to onstage where they are triumphing.

Well, three of them are. Fred's wife and Fred's daughter and some young man or other step into assorted breaches, in assorted dresses, and knock 'em dead. (It says here. No, really, they do all right.) Another young man is played by the amazing, malleable Derek Griffiths, who announces at the beginning that he's never been on a stage before. I can think of no performer of whom that seems less likely. In Round the Horne once there was a sketch about an unknown who went on in a concert-party, with no experience, no rehearsal, and would you believe it, he was absolutely diabolical. This is Mr Griffiths's role here, though a man of his parts can hardly keep that up all night, and he is allowed some delightful reprises.

Fred meanwhile, with fame to win and fortune to keep, goes through the night in a spirit of desperate aggression, brilliantly embodied by Mr Forsyth, all talents bristling. He plays magisterially off a recalcitrant house. Yes, he tells his colleagues, they're a nice audience; there is no viciousness in them. That's a beautiful line. Is it in the script? Is there a script? Is the show's structure a springboard or a safety net?

Tucked away in Burt Shevelove's production are some happy digs (mainly costume changes) at the bits of theatrical ingenuity that are plainly tending towards mayhem, have to be forcibly rerouted in the interests of the next plot. But the show is a triumph of more than ingenuity; the Papal scenes approach magnificence, and the madhouse episode actually attains it.

Lark Rise

Lark Rise (Cottesloe) also conscripts its audiences: those at least who stand and mingle with the players on the ground floor. This, like The Passion last year, is a promenade production. Watching them from above, I was fascinated by their ebb and flow; I've seen far less interesting movement from companies who've rehearsed. In one scene nine men go, do allegiances; to me a meadow, advancing in a straight line on the punters who respond with a first-rate impromptu mime of the parting of the Red Sea. Later some of them allow themselves, with due bashfulness, to be dragooned into joining in a children's tug-of-war game; and at the close there is country dancing.

Keith Dewhurst has dramatised the first part of Flora Thompson's rural trilogy, Lark Rise to Candleford, ingeniously shaping its scattered details of milk, wood, fashion, into an account of a single day in the life of an Oxfordshire hamlet in the 1890s. The family who are meant to be at the centre of it (one of its indistinctive features: the faint performance by Laurence Hanman as the boy), but the periphery is alive and the directors, Bill Bryden and Sebastian Graham-Jones, have moulded some excellent groups. It makes a pleasing and informative entertainment, marred only by the hint of do-goodery that seems to afflict any show involving folk-songs.

The Duchess of Malfi / The Second Mrs Tanqueray / Eejits

Julia Blalock, grappling with three ambiguous heroines at once, fuses them by main force; if her trial scene is exaggeratedly impudent (she might as well poke out her tongue at her judges), her final passion makes amends. The whole cast, under Philip Prowse's direction, breathe in the poisoned Renaissance air like petrol fumes.

At the Sheffield Crucible, Peter James has directed an almost violently unstuffy revival of Pinero's The Second Mrs Tanqueray; Mel Martin and Richard Easton emote with a fury and velocity that are exhilarating even when they exceed credibility.

Meanwhile in the Crucible's studio Ron Hutchinson's Eejits, a successor to his Says I, Says He, this time dealing with emigrant or second-generation Irishmen, each a poor imitation of the wrong kind of thing entirely. This means they drink, swear, quarrel and shoot past the point of monotony (and are fully, though not disgracefully, indulged in this production). Mr Hutchinson remains, how you say, promising.

If Rayner Bourton's script had chosen to follow this line it might have given us something which the Stone's could never have managed themselves. As it is the show turns out to be a harmless but rather limp little offering.
