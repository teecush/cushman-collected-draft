Clearing Ostrovsky's good name

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'Russia's first and most prolific professional.'

IT HAS been written: 'No great European playwright -- not even Calderon, Corneille, Racine, Marivaux, Musset, Goldoni, Goethe, Schiller, Hauptmann or Schnitzler -- is more unrecognised in this country than Alexander Ostrovsky.'

That sentence, a choice piece of neglected Tynan, appeared in the programme for 'The Storm' which was the National Theatre's attempt in 1966 to do Ostrovsky -- and which actually, as far as the English theatre is concerned, did for him. It also did for neglected foreign classics generally, few of the other names on Tynan's whimsically accusatory list having reached the National since. The RSC meanwhile has sheered off from every one of them, maybe on the grounds that none of them is Russian. But Ostrovsky of course is; in fact he is the national playwright, Russia's first and most prolific professional. Chekhov, who came along two decades later, was just a doctor who dabbled.

Ostrovsky mainly wrote comedies; 'The Storm' was one of the few exceptions. Last week the RSC cleared his good name by presenting The Forest at the Other Place.

'The Forest' raises the number of plays about actors in their current repertory to three. (Four; I forgot 'Hamlet.') A couple of provincial strollers, down on their luck, meet up. One, looking like a broken-down Scottish Highlander, is Richard Pasco, unexpectedly but rewardingly cast as a low comedian sunk even lower. ('I became a prompter.') 'Were you good, sir?' he asks his companion: ill-advisedly since his companion, looking like a melancholy gypsy, is Alan Howard, mocking himself with a serene, drawling arrogance, a flashing eye, and a perfection of timing that makes his similar role in 'Wild Oats' look like the merest trial run.

Actors don't change much; at least Ostrovsky seems to have written a large number of RSC in-jokes. 'I've got an easier temperament than you,' says Mr Pasco, settling into his role as Sancho to Mr Howard's Don. Earlier they have shared a grumble about 'university people shovelling themselves on to the stage.' This scene is a self-contained delight, but at the end of it they decide to make contact with the rest of the play, which takes place on the estate of Mr Howard's aunt.

She is a rich skinflint, a Madame Ranevsky happy to sell every tree in the wood if she can get a good price for it. Barbara Leigh-Hunt gives her a classic performance, merging lust and cupidity; allowing a favoured suitor to peer briefly into her cash box as if she were giving him a quick flash. She is meanwhile making life hell for her niece, driven by her condition (in love, no dowry) to throw herself in the river, but fished out by Mr Howard who sees in her suicidal passion promise of the leading lady he has been looking for. Not surprisingly, since Janine Duvitski, sensible but odd, balances her emotions beautifully.

This is Mr Howard's cue to confront, with histrionic but heartfelt generosity, the entire menage of the rich, which includes Raymond Westwell, booming on two sticks. (One reason, I realise, why I like Mr Westwell so much is that he sounds like the great radio barons of my childhood: Felix Felton or Norman Shelley.) Between his two scenes in this show, he nips down the road to give his definitive Tubal at the big house. An award is in order.

Mr Howard confounds them all with subversive sentiments that turn out to be quotations from Schiller, passed by the censor. Ostrovsky's own comment comes in the reactions of the gentry who admire his pluck but refuse to drink with him, but he enforces his own kind of class distinction; Mr Howard may be noble, but Mr Pasco stays cunning.

It is a long and surprising play; and though Adrian Noble knows how to marshal its forces at any given moment, he allows the length to show and some of the surprises to surprise him. But it's an occasion, especially perhaps for Mr Howard. Acting for the first time in a studio space he confirms that he is, when not trumpeting, the kindliest of actors and suggests himself as ideal casting for Bottom the Weaver; he really can roar you as gently as any sucking dove.

According to Warehouse director Howard Davies the newest playwrights are beginning to move back towards closely observed personal relations. His last three presentations bear him out, and I wish I liked them more. Hanif Kureishi's Outskirts is a particular disappointment since the relations are actually so well stated: unemployed South Londoner to former friend, now a teacher; unemployed to psychologically battered mother; most elusive of all, friend to mother.

It adds up to an engulfing landscape of despair, with no facile emotions or solutions, and reflected in the setting; an upturned chair represents a wasteland, right way up it signifies a room, and there's not too much to choose. But we are transported in time as well as in space, from past friendship to present estrangement, and the information provided is too fragile to support the structure.

It all hones in on one past act of racial violence, committed not by the present sullen Fascist supporter but by the liberal teacher. It makes you think, as they say; but it doesn't gain from restatement; I felt an unregenerate wish to see the characters abandoned and the social issues explored. I also tired of the bloodlessly artificial dialogue ('a mechanic I'll be, a trade I'll have') that was the previous generation's worst legacy. There is a supple performance by David Bambur, a more gangling one by Tony Guilfoyle; Marjorie Yates is in danger of being typed below her worth as the actress you send for when you want someone to look ill.

Two echoes of the music-hall. In Gone with Hardy (Tricycle) an Australian playwright, David Allen, tells how Stan Laurel, not the innocent he seemed, climbed to success over the discarded talents of two colleagues, a Scots comic and an Aussie chanteuse. This is a story of stifling ordinariness, not much enlivened -- in fact desperately protracted -- by having its every stage acted out as a vaudeville routine. Mercifully the three performers -- Sylvester McCoy, Jimmy Logan and Toni Palmer -- excel.

As do Roy Hudd and Billy Dainty, thoroughly charming comics who in Just a Verse and Chorus (Greenwich) play Weston and Lee, authors of together or apart 'I'm 'Enery the Eighth,' 'With 'Er 'Ead Tucked Underneath 'Er Arm,' 'Goodbye-ee,' 'Paddy McGinty's Goat,' 'Knees Up Mother Brown' -- in fact just about every song you ever associated with the halls except the gritty, realistic Marie Lloyd specials.

There are some goodies in the Weston-Lee catalogue; some outrageous rhymes, but many are just repetitions of jokes that weren't too funny the first time. And don't ask which of them wrote the music; with those tunes it doesn't matter. When a song called 'You Give Me Ideas' turns up, with an atrocious lyric but some suggestion of melodic shape and harmonic movement, there turn out to have been others involved. The show is well presented with both songs and information pointedly dispensed. Mr Hudd's rendition of 'Brown Boots' (the boys' best) is very touching, but popular songs rank with news magazines ('Now' is not the 'Time' for all good men) among the things in which Britain lags behind America.
