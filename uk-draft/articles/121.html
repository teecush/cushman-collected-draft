Molière caught in a trap

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

The Théâtre National Populaire from Lyons is visiting the currently unpopular National Theatre on the South Bank (ground floor, Lyttelton proscenium department). It began with Tartuffe, unexpectedly demonstrating that the French have the same worries over Molière as we do.

Once we used to ignore him, or treat him as rough rep. farce, freely adapted by Miles Malleson. The advent of acceptable translations in rhyming verse stepped up the number of productions (I have seen more productions of Tartuffe than of any play not by Shakespeare) and tore their style. Making sense of couplets, and making it quickly and gracefully, can easily become an end in itself.

It insulates the characters from recognisable psychological pressures; if you analyse the action of the play, you may see that in Tartuffe spirit and flesh are at war, that religion is being employed as a cloak for cruelty, that the duped Orgon under the spell of the sham-Puritan Tartuffe is unloosing all his worst passions on his own family, and that he passes from this moral danger into extreme physical peril; he is suspected of treason and may lose his life.

You may even see this when watching the play; it is very clear-cut. But it is, by virtue of that, very difficult to feel: no disfiguring emotion ever spills out. Complexities may be suggested, but they are not explored. Molière, too, peopled his plays with stock figures and put them through stock actions; this combination of style and substance is apt to leave you, at the end of a short evening, wondering "Is that all?" Something so fast, so basic, so simple--above all so soon over--is not what we expect of a masterpiece. We equate significance with the space between words, and in most Molière productions, English or French, there isn't any.

In Roger Planchon's production, however, there is almost an infinity of it; the running-time is three and a half hours, riddled with pauses. There is space in a more literal sense as well; Hubert Monloup's setting is immense. It is, to begin with, one of the highest I have ever seen; it stretches in fact to heaven, surmounted as it is by the life-size image of a suffering Christ. There is much scaffolding, suggesting that Orgon's family have either the builders or the bailiffs in. On the whole, they seem to be doing all right, living in a palace rather than a mere bourgeois mansion; and when Orgon himself appears, he looks like the Sun-King.

Each of the play's five acts is accorded a different background, each ready behind its predecessor, so that the play becomes a steady progress towards the back of the stage. Everything that involves a door or window is masterly, and André Diot's lighting is superlative. The production is a magnificent feat of stage engineering; its size, its length, its iconography, its music, the thunderclap with which it opens, are the trappings of a great theatrical experience. But it is the play itself that is trapped. It suggests the aesthetic which can sometimes be found--though on a necessarily smaller financial scale--in the work of the RSC, or indeed of the Glasgow Citizens': you testify to the stature of a play, and of your own thoughts about it, by encasing it.

But inside the case the same old text is unfolding, with much of its spirit denied. True, it is given great weight, and some of the family relations have great tension (quite possibly strengthened for us by the language-barrier; we expect a certain amount of boredom and the grandiose silences are more acceptable than they would be from a home team).

Planchon has been a great Brechtian, and he retains enough of his inheritance to give his actors household tasks; these always seem to me more immediately helpful to the actors than to the play, and they certainly add to the authority of Arlette Gilbert as the bossy maid. She even gets a few laughs. But consider: there is a passage at the end of the play in which Orgon's mother refuses to believe evil of Tartuffe. However graphic his descriptions of the man's attempts to seduce his wife, the old lady tells him he should have waited for further proofs. If that is not gag-writing I don't know what it is; and in this production I don't know what it is, except perhaps a testimony that those concerned are far too serious (again Stratford comes to mind) to bother with laughter. But Molière made serious jokes; anyone can preach serious sermons.

Planchon, like Jouvet, Barrault and Vilar before him, is his own leading man. France, whatever they may tell you, is the true home of the actor-manager, often to the detriment of the production. Though he goes through impressive motions (including, as far as I could see, the gnawing of a chair), he has not much presence.

The final entry of the king's officer becomes almost an invasion; soldiers fill the stage, the floor opens up, and the family find themselves in instant dungeons. The tables turned, Orgon practically assaults Tartuffe; and his brother-in-law's admonition not to waste words on the villain hardly meets the case. Louis XIV's intervention is deprived of its suspended wit, and made brutally corrupt. This, like most stage violence, is a cliché, an attempt to thrust significance upon a play by main force. Planchon keeps returning to Tartuffe, but he may now have loved it to death; as a compromise solution I would take John Dexter's Misanthrope in English, which showed that sparkling waters, too, can run deep.

A milder clap of thunder begins Arnold Ridley's The Ghost Train (Old Vic) and mild is the word throughout. This twenties thriller is pitilessly, not to say cravenly, guyed. Line after line is tossed to the audience, who are dared not to laugh; the first-nighters duly dissolved but less interested parties may not be so easily pleased. Boy and girl perch on a table as if about to render a duet from Very Good Eddie; the costumes are displayed; the thrills disappear. James Villiers slips commandingly into style, though I fear that prolonged immersion in it may damage his health; but it all seems pretty pointless.

So does Mr Laurel and Mr Hardy (Mayfair), which suggests, undramatically, that there was no tension between the team's on-screen and off-screen personalities: they merely played themselves throughout. John Shedden and Ian Ireland are thus compelled to do what others have done peerlessly before them, though Mr Shedden in particular comes wondrously near to Stan's agility and to his down-at-chin air of perky self-deprecation.

Tom McGrath's dialogue is Hollywood bio-pic, though derived less from the gossip columns than the cineaste's paperbacks; Stan discourses, with impersonal judiciousness, on the harm done to their style when the studio took them out of two-reelers and into features. But when they escape from words at the end they blossom; their final dance is one of the most enchanting things in town.
