Wolf from Hungary

I HAVE no wish to keep The Wolf from the door of any London theatre; indeed, the sooner this Oxford Playhouse production comes to town the brighter town will be. All the same, I have to present intending patrons with a stark choice: either you see the play or you read this review.

I cannot, I fear, discuss this play intelligibly without giving away its surprises. Its narrative twists are not the only pleasures it provides, but they are substantial--if that word may be permitted of a play which is largely concerned with manipulating levels of fantasy. In this connection the expertise of Ferenc Molnar, the Hungarian master of the boulevards who wrote this piece in 1914, prompts comparison with that of Jean Anouilh. I admire much of Anouilh's work, but despite his reputation for theatricality I have found no moment in his plays as arresting as the revelation at the end of Act II of The Wolf that the preceding scene has taken place inside the heroine's head. The information is craftily, economically conveyed. One is momentarily bewildered, then accepts it as a coup de theatre; only in the course of Act III does the psychological accuracy of this dream-sequence finally become apparent.

In the first act we have witnessed this heroine dining with her husband in a restaurant, and seen his jealousy aroused (not that it is ever exactly dormant) by the sight of one of her old admirers. After the interval we find the couple preparing to attend a diplomatic reception; and still embroiled. She unearths an old letter from her former swain in which he promises to return to her some day--as a soldier, a diplomat, an artist or a mere servant. We next meet her, duly arrived at the party, exposed and succumbing to the allurements of just such a quartet. Each appears in turn, and each exactly resembles her cast-off lover.

Here Molnar's manipulation of his audience is at its most magisterial. When the second suitor appears as the double of the first, we are bemused, and begin to hunt for realistic explanations. At the third appearance we think we see the dramatist's game and agree to play it with him. A similar stage of tacit connivance is reached in An Inspector Calls, but Molnar's strategy is more audacious than Mr Priestley's. There is a traditional understanding that running gags come in threes; when we are faced with the fourth mutation--the suitor as servant--we catch our breath. (We do; I heard us.) It is at this point that the dreamer awakes (and decides that it might be best after all not to go to the ball) and the release, though a theatrical shock, is as necessary for us as for her.

Frank Hauser's 17 years as director of the Playhouse (now sadly about to end) have never been notable for ensemble playing. His achievement has been to bring distinguished texts to the light, and to attract star players to their service. On this occasion the simultaneous (and well directed) presence of Judi Dench, Leo McKern and Edward Woodward renders the gaps below the top level fairly harmless.

Our theatre is not short of clever comediennes: Miss Dench is special because her brittleness is always an abstraction from a hard core of common-sense. She is thus able to combine the lady's languorous visions (happily flaunting her flunkey to the horror of Budapest society) with the practicality that can eventually send her lover on his way and out of town. She is, if you like, a classic bourgeois hypocrite, and the play could certainly be accused of coquetting with adultery while saving itself for marriage. The denouement on the surface could hardly be cosier; so why does the final curtain descend on the husband, alone and in tears?

The answer is supplied by Mr McKern, who plays the eternal cuckold in his own imagination, unable to credit his wife's fidelity in a world full of wenching hussars, seeking always to buy himself into her favour. He is a man of outward substance and inner vertigo. Finally confronted by his supposed rival he takes firm hold of the back of a chair and promptly loses his balance.

These are two consummate performances. When the wind is to Woodward the air is somewhat thinner; playing the lover in his various guises he is constrained to offer a string of what I can only call tours-de-farce. All are funny (a haughty impersonation of Mario Lanza brings the house down), but I still wait to see on stage the relaxed, compassionate, humorous actor who once shone out on television in an Evelyn Waugh trilogy.

The Actors' Company (currently at the Arts, Cambridge) offer at certain lunchtime and late-night showings a double bill of new plays. The first half did little to allay my suspicions, aroused by their Iris Murdoch premiere last year, of this company's susceptibility to the spuriously literary: Gabriel Josipovici's Flow intercuts five reminiscent monologues, a line at a time. Banality chopped up and counterpointed is still banal.

Edward Petherbridge's stage version of R. D. Laing's Knots is likewise fragmented, but here each segment makes a point. A wry, humorous and occasionally desperate image accumulates of the slippery delusions that clog up human relationships. Mr Petherbridge's brilliant production is a company showcase packed with surprises; we learn that he himself is a virtuoso mime, that Caroline Blakiston can play the organ, and that John Moreno can do almost anything.

There is more good company acting in And They Put Handcuffs on the Flowers (Open Space): Malcolm Storry, Anthony Milner and Ian McDiarmid hurl themselves with anthropoid abandon into Fernando Arrabal's demonstration that prison can be good sadomasochistic fun. The arresting title phrase is Garcia Lorca's; and due tribute is paid to his death at the hands of a Franco firing-squad. The play's intentions are of the best; Arrabal wrote it at the request of the Madrid prisoners whose incarceration he shared for 25 days in 1967, to tell the world of the conditions they endured. But, faced with the enticing prospect of actually writing about three men in a cell, this Peter Pan of the avant-garde goes all giggly. There are one or two passages of authentic horror, but there are many more (priest-baiting, excremental reveries and phallic frolics) which play like private jokes. Most unforgivable is the bonelessly reverential treatment accorded to the play's lone activist, who is most respectfully groomed for martyrdom. It seems that jail doesn't humiliate everyone after all; insulated from all the buffoonery he even gets to keep his clothes on. He represents the gesture to commitment of an apolitical talent, forcing itself to keep a straight face.

At least the production (by Arrabal himself) is physically and scenically striking. You look in vain for such attributes in The Cilla Black Show (Palladium), except in the performance of Philippe Genty & Co., invisible puppeteers who contrive a bewitching dance for a trio of emus, one of whom keeps burying his beak in the pink feather-duster that serves him for a body. Nobody else on stage evinces this becoming bashfulness. The star herself, as healthily winsome as ever, sings her older hits very well. Either that, or sixties nostalgia is on its way and I am its first victim.
