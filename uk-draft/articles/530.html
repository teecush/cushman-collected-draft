Multiple Exposure

Robert Cushman · Radio Times · Arts · 1975-11-14
Profile

Keep an Eye on Albert

CompanyBBC1PlaywrightBrian Glover

Multiple exposure

When is a wrestler not a wrestler? Answer: when his name is Brian Glover and he's an actor who writes television plays. As Leon Arras, he's a star of the wrestling circuit. As Brian Glover, he has played Shakespearean fops and telly convicts. This week sees Glover's latest play. It is set in a Yorkshire village based on his childhood home and stars two Alberts -- a pigeon and a man. Robert Cushman asked him about his triple life

Keep an Eye on Albert, Tuesday BBC1

ASKED TO NAME a time and place for the interview, Brian Glover was tempted to suggest tea at the Ritz. To my regret he thought better, or more modestly, of it, and so we met instead at a Soho patisserie, where the cakes are excellent but where there are no cucumber sandwiches. At first glance, Mr Glover, Yorkshire-born actor, playwright and all-in wrestler, is not a cucumber sandwich man, but he has a keen appetite for the good things of life. He certainly did a hearty job on those cakes.

Something funny happened to him just as he was parking his car in Old Compton Street. A couple of blokes came up to him, and one of them asked to shake his hand; hadn't he been in the telly play about the three friends who went up to Stratford by barge? This play, Peter Terson's Shakespeare or Bust, is now three years old, but it still remains fresh in many memories, and Brian Glover is used to being associated with it. So he took this approach calmly; he was less prepared for the handshake to be followed by a kick. Not at him, at his little four-year-old Volkswagen: 'It wasn't good enough for them, not their idea of what a successful actor should have. They decided I was pretty ordinary after all.'

Glover is 41 now; his acting career only really got started when he was 36, so it has taken him a fairly short time to reach this equivocal position of being simultaneously revered and resented by the fans (or, as he likes to call them when they're paying customers, 'the punters'). He wears it well and cheerfully, but then he's been some kind of a star for 20 years. That's the length of his wrestling career, but that fame has been won under another name. In the ring he is known as Leon Arras.

He has always been pulled in two directions. He grew up in a mining village just outside Barnsley; his parents kept the corner-shop. 'We were firmly at the top of the bottom drawer.' His mother had wanted him to get a nice, steady job: say, a draughtsman. He obliged her to the extent of winning a scholarship to Barnsley Grammar and then going to Sheffield University. But his father supplemented the earnings from corner-shop-keeping with a spot of wrestling which, he pointed out, could earn Brian more than any amount of education. So, with some exertion of influence from Glover Sr, Leon Arras was born.

L. Arras received so much more from the wrestling promoters than B. Glover did from the education authorities that at the end of a year Sheffield University lost a student. However, heedful of his mother's advice, he took a teachers' training course.

Actually though, he was still leading a double life: slipping off at weekends to throw and be thrown in Paris (Saturdays) and Zurich (Sundays), returning to Barnsley Sunday night just in time for a drink before closing time. So he was a jet-setter long before he was an actor, acquiring the confidence which one day was to enable him to negotiate with West End producer Binkie Beaumont for a higher fee than any agent could get.

Between the staff-room and the Haymarket Theatre had come Kes. The Ken Loach film was based on a novel by Barry Hines, who happened to be teaching at the same school as Glover. Glover browbeat -- his word -- Hines into getting him cast. It wasn't quite his professional debut; he'd done some commercials.

If his final capitulation to show business might have pleased his father, the solidity of his success should also have delighted his mother. 'I'm a very successful actor. Producers know what they're going to get when they get me. A big, square figure which should at least have a lot of presence, something definitely northern, and at times slightly humorous.' Add a pink, open-face with some fairish fuzz circling the back of a shiny dome, making him look at once tough and cherubic. He's very likable.

Television has always found him valuable, whether sharing a cell with Ronnie Barker in Porridge or as one of the three men in that Stratford barge. The year after doing that he was at Stratford himself, which he thinks may have had something to do with the fact that Janet Suzman, wife of Royal Shakespeare director Trevor Nunn, was in the Terson play. He pictures 'Trevor and our Janet' watching it at home on television and thinking, 'We could use him.' So they could: they happened to be doing the one play (As You Like It) that definitely calls for a real wrestler, as well as Richard II, in which he gave what you might call a regional performance as the Bishop of Carlisle. More recently he has worked a great deal with Lindsay Anderson at the Royal Court.

There have been films: Anderson's O Lucky Man and Brannigan, in which he got to be beaten up by John Wayne. Photographs of this encounter have come in very handy on his wrestling publicity.

He also writes plays, and received perhaps his most valuable advice from Tony Garnett: if somebody says they'd be interested to see a script of yours, have one in your pocket to show them, immediately. Operating in this way he was able to sell his first two plays to BBCtv simultaneously; they hit the screen within weeks of each other -- one of them, naturally, was a wrestling play and his greatest critical success. It was called A Drink Out of the Bottle -- 'that's a wrestler's term, that's used to give the word between rounds that no quarter is going to be given.'

Another play, Pig Bin, got him into trouble 'because it's the most truthful play I've written.' It depicted football hooligans and it upset people. Glover's new play, Keep an Eye on Albert, is about a shaky marriage in a northern village. Pigeon-racing is involved as well. 'What triggered off the play was watching miler pigeons; you try to release the cock when it's desperately wanting to fertilise the egg -- just when the hen is about to lay -- so it'll fly home like a bullet. If the hen has laid the night before, you've had it. And that, of course, can be a very potent sexual metaphor.' There are two Alberts in the play, one a bird and one a man.

I asked him why he wrote for television rather than the stage. 'I like to know how much I'm going to get from my effort. I've still got that Yorkshire sense.' His parents would be proud of him.
