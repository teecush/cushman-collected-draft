Shakespeare As I Like It

ROBERT CUSHMAN reports on the Berlin Theatre Festival.

WEST BERLIN seems to be a city in a continual state of arts festival; rather as if, having lost its role of capital, it felt compelled to acquire a new one as West Germany's purveyor of cultural hospitality.

The Berlin Theatertreffen, a three-week event closing today, is itself an assertion of the German theatre's identity: more particularly of the identity of its directors. A jury have chosen 10 productions from Germany's subsidised output over the previous year. Once they have come to Berlin (only two of this year's offerings actually originated there), the critical function passes to the audience who are the most vocal connoisseurs I have encountered.

A pretty way-out production of Goethe's Iphigenia in Tauris was greeted with cheers and boos of equal volume. I assumed that the barrackers were conservatives who objected to seeing Goethe staged on a setting suggestive of a bomb-site. A colleague, wiser than I in the ways of Berliners, told me that they were actually radicals who objected to seeing Goethe staged at all.

Of the two Berlin productions granted entrance to the festival, one, expectedly, was the work of Peter Stein. Surprisingly, however, it was not his celebrated version of As You Like It, though this show has probably made more noise in the world than any other German product since the decline of the Berliner Ensemble. It was, however, still on view and I took myself to it. Or rather was taken: the show is performed in an outlying film studio and a special bus is laid on from mid-town.

On arrival, you are ushered through a series of outhouses into the first playing-space: a long white room, crammed and balustraded, where the audience stand huddled to watch the play's first pre-Arden scenes. If the intention is to make us feel like members of the court then it fails, but there is enough in the action itself to hold us. After 40 minutes the doors open and we are off to Arden. We walk for five minutes or more through a maze of passages, hung about with details from the Elizabethan World Picture, and emerge properly footsore and expectant into a mind-blowing Forest of Arden: a cross between Disneyland and the tree-walk in the Battersea Pleasure Gardens. This time we can sit down: just as well, since the entertainment lasts for three more uninterrupted hours. But then in Arden you lose track of time; there's no clock in the forest.

The production has a tremendous primitive appeal; the studio set is a huge toy and we yearn sometimes for the actors to be turned off it so we can play there ourselves. But the treatment of the play itself is sophisticated and meticulous.

There is no uniformity to Stein's greenwood; Audrey may be realistically churning butter in one corner of it, while Phebe and Silvius are adopting conscious pastoral poses in another. This simultaneity (tentatively explored over here by Terry Hands and Pip Simmons) is an enormously valuable technique for staging Shakespeare. It allows us - like the best imagistic criticism - to see a play's world whole. And Stein does it without sacrificing the narrative; indeed he enhances it, bringing off stage-events before our eyes, right down to the invasion of Arden by the usurper's army. This might not work with the original text (Shakespeare's rhythms put up their own defence against intrusion), but a translation can afford to be explanatory.

The production is steeped in Elizabethan lore: the only reason I can think of for its exclusion from the official festival canon. Official opinion seems to prefer its classics dragged into the twentieth century, though preferably not kicking and screaming. The only piece to be really manhandled was Kleist's Prince of Homburg, whose directors (guests from East Berlin) devoted the evening to a tedious demonstration of their own dislike of the Prussian militarism in which the play is admittedly steeped. This was a night on which boos outweighed cheers - and on which one of the directors made his own charming intervention from the stalls, to point out that a crucial effect (actually the raising of a curtain, which is about as crucial as you can get) wasn't working properly.

Even here, however, there was visual imagination at work, even if of a monotonously louring kind. The production came from Hamburg, which also offered a straight version of Ghosts (a play it is difficult to imagine done crooked), which still outpaced most British productions in its fanatical attention to detail. Oswald, at his first appearance, slid down the banisters to illustrate his Bohemianism, but stopped halfway - to illustrate his debility.

Even the most libertarian productions displayed a firm sense of responsibility to their texts. The Iphigenia was directed by the highly regarded Claus Peymann of Stuttgart (though soon to be of Bochum following a political quarrel with his employers), who seized on the play's contrast between civilisation and savagery. Goethe's treatment of Euripides is a rather smug Enlightenment rebuke to ancient barbarism. Peymann makes Iphigenia into a prim but genuinely troubled keeper of the Hellenic conscience, her brother Orestes into an unscrupulous adventurer and their adversary Thoas into a half-emancipated Caliban dazzled by Iphigenia, who gags disgustedly when he hears of the refinements of human sacrifice practised by her ancestors of the House of Atreus.

Roland Schafer, of Dusseldorf, mounted Schiller's Kabale und Liebe, a domestic prose tragedy which the comparatively poor are ruined by the undoubtedly rich. The contrast between ranks is fairly simplistically pointed up by housing the former on a cramped bit of forestage and the latter on the vast expanses behind, but the production is informed by a remarkable poetic delicacy. The hero and heroine die of poisoned love and poisoned lemonade; in the first scene both the love and the lemonade flow freely and the long-range forecast is very touching.

These are all old plays. New ones were sparsely represented, though Trilogy des Wiedersehens by Botho Strauss turned up in two manifestations (it's been done all over); I saw Stein's Schaubuhne version which offered the best ensemble playing of the festival. The play, set at the opening of a 'capitalist realist' art exhibition, would be a natural for Hampstead, since it's crammed not only with the discreet charm of the bourgeoisie (and Stein's production is quite Bunuelian), but with its wit and its hang-ups as well. The Schaubuhne is a large adaptable room; the play was performed, with consummate naturalism, in one corner of it.

I had a tonic week, and a reassuring one: reassuring since I detected little of the self-congratulation and coterie ingratiation that might be expected to dog a theatre in thrall to directorial concepts; directors in thrall to designers; concepts and designers in thrall to huge budgets. Subsidies make ours look shameful, but nobody seems to have been corrupted by them. I have my doubts as to whether the British theatre could muster 10 comparable productions for a similar annual event, but the only way to find out is to try.
