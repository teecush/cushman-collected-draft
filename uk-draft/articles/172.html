Riotous appetites

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on plays from Chicago and plays at the National

TITLE of the week, or rather the fortnight, was Sexual Perversity in Chicago (Regent). I mentioned this to a friend, pointing out that just about every play on my list seemed to concern sexual perversity (as opposed, note, to perversion) somewhere. The same goes, she said unimpressed, for most plays.

She's right of course, though there are exceptions: one of them being Duck Variations, the curtain-raiser on the same bill. It is another product of Chicago, Chicago, that toddling town, and of its - indeed America's - white hope playwright David Mamet. Two old men sit on a park-bench and talk, with considerably more humour than men on park-benches usually exhibit in plays, or indeed in parks. Part of the playwright's purpose may be to satirise all portentous dialogues. His characters gaze at a pond, situated somewhere in the front stalls, and discuss ducks.

Mr Mamet never claims, even implicitly, that ducks symbolise the human condition; he merely demonstrates the ease with which any line of conversation may be made to terminate in webbed feet. The forcible incarceration of barnyard ducks is given judicious consideration ("you can't put 'em on their honour"), and just occasionally someone springs an unanswerable ("I mean, what is worth living if not life?"). Albert Takazauckas's direction rightly cherishes Mr Mamet's words, which are spoken good by Bernard Spear and all right by Gordon Sterne.

There is a New York flavour about the production and it persists, with less justification, into Sexual Perversity, etc, which is less a comedy of language than of particularly thrusting urban manners. Chicago is carnivore city, and these actors are vegetarian. One of them, Stephen Hoye, is entitled; he plays the younger, rawer (and ironically more successful) of two questing males.

Courteously and rather shyly making a date with his lady after getting her to bed, he is gently hilarious. But as his friend and mentor, a self-proclaimed lecher condemned to continence, Kenneth Nelson gives a smooth account of a roughly assertive role, lacking the macho look of accustomed success; his rebuff by an unco-operative pick-up in a singles bar (one of the best of the play's array of short, sharp scenes) is accordingly less funny and furious than it should be.

The evening sizzles with promise. Sparkling set-pieces counterpoint the stabbing monosyllables of insecurity; partners work self-consciously to please and then to destroy each other, and friends do the same.

Do You Love Me? (Roundhouse) covers the same ground in dance and doggerel; the very title tells the same story, in more whimsical terms. This is the Actors' Company's second theatricalisation of R. D. Laing, and this time winsomeness, though inventively orchestrated by the director, Edward Petherbridge, takes all. Still, the company seem strong and, as they cycle, pirouette and banter around a white art-gallery in which they themselves are the exhibits, they can occasionally make us remember things in our own loves we'd like to forget.

So, even more fleetingly, can the characters in Terry James's Confession Fever (King's Head), a truth-game with some witty lines but too heavily contrived for the slender facts it reveals. Maureen O'Brien again proves herself one of our most stylish sufferers.

Sexual perversity on the grandest scale is the subject of Wycherley's The Country Wife (Olivier). It calls to mind a few of King Lear's pungent reflections on respectable womanhood: "Behold yon simpering dame. That minces virtue and does shake the head to hear of pleasure's name." The fitchew nor the soiled horse goes to't, with a more riotous appetite.

It may not greatly tax Elizabeth Spriggs to play another simpering dame, but as Lady Fidget, circumspectly salacious, she duly capsizes the house when rebuking a companion on whose tongue pleasure's name has trespassed: "for shame, sister; whither shall we ramble?" Others in place are Polly Adams as the virtuous and sensible Alithea (though this most charming of actresses might, as an experiment, deliver the odd line ungraced by a radiant smile), Robin Bailey most efficient as the masculine Fidget, and Susan Littler in the title role: an unmatched part for a classical debutante - and one in which Miss Littler distinguishes herself with her reeling delight after her first bouquet of extra-marital kisses.

One of the charms of this play is that all its characters get what they deserve. The country wife may be consigned to her tyrant husband, but she has learned to outsmart him. Her seducer Horner may, through his stratagem of pretended impotence, have gained access to all the women he desires; the joke on him is that he despises them too much to enjoy them. (The real prize is off to stale woods and pastures old.) He becomes, as his name implies, a cuckolding machine; what a passion goes he for. (In this production gowned and turbaned, he dresses the part.)

Albert Finney's Horner, having gained the audience's confidence, abuses it; for all his vigour he generalises, missing both Horner's misanthropy and his implicit discomfiture; he neither judges nor is judged. The sadistically possessive Pinchwife (who threatens to go for his wife's face with a penknife) is similarly reduced by Richard Johnson to mere bluffness; while Ben Kingsley makes a lisping, honking caricature of Sparkish. Peter Hall's production is a considered homage to Restoration theatre but with little feeling of Restoration, or any other, life. The actors talk more readily to the audience than to each other, brandishing a convention rather than honouring it.

The National has been firing all three of its guns. The Lyttelton houses a Christmas show, Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, based with remarkable fidelity on the old poem. Penguin Classic version, I would not have expected an uncompromising exposition of the medieval doctrine of chastity, tricked out with many acrobatics but no jokes, to hold an audience of six-to-twelves, but they seemed gripped. I thought it went on a bit, but I thought that of the original, too. And the symbolism of the Green Knight (nature, etc) seemed more convincing when standing a solid eight foot than ever it did in Middle English.

The Cottesloe's hospitality to touring groups, which reached a recent nadir with Babel's Tower, presented by Moving Being, perked up again with Paines Plough in two plays by David Pownall. One, Richard III Part Two, juxtaposes the maligned Crookback with a nervous George Orwell, wondering what the world will make of 1984: the two are linked, unnecessarily, by Betrayal, a board-game about Richard III, marketed in 1984.

Motocar is a far more powerful assessment of relative values; Rhodesia is finally on the brink of majority rule, and a black runs imaginatively amok in a white-run mental hospital, forcing the staff to participate in his own re-creation of oppression. The play, too, is overwrought: full of ingenious, even compelling images, but strangely lacking in urgency.

The Plough are not my favourite group, but they are thoughtful, stylish and unique; their imminent dispersal from lack of funds is to be regretted and, if possible, prevented. Though having said that I will add that were I keeper of contracts on the South Bank, until I had the signature of Joe Marcell, who gives sleek and mocking performances in both plays, firmly imprinted on a dotted line, I would not allow him to quit the building.
