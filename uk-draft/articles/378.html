Soliloquies in Louisville

ROBERT CUSHMAN reports from America on a festival of new plays.

THERE are currently two plays on Broadway, and a third one off, that were first staged in the annual New Play Festival at the Actors' Theatre of Louisville, Kentucky. That makes Louisville the main provider of material for intelligent middle-brow drama in New York and subsequently the rest of the country.

I doubt whether this year's bash, which culminated last weekend in a monster weekend of plays attended by producers, scouts and critics from America, Europe and even China, will yield much to enrich the world repertoire; you could hardly call the work experimental, except perhaps in the sheer scale of the organisation and in the proliferation of plays lasting one act or less. Lunchtime theatre is a British phenomenon unknown in the States, but here were pieces ready made for it, available with every meal. In three days I saw 27 plays.

That may be an unfair count; since 18 of them were short monologues, combined to make three separate entertainments. There is no question at Louisville of the soliloquy being a dead convention; the Actors' Theatre may be doing more than television to kill the art of conversation; and when the third programme arrived most auditors were sighing for a good old extravagant two-hander.

Not that the Louisville solos were ill-written; they provided basic proof that the average American playwright writes tangier sentences than his British equivalent. They offered well-seized opportunities to a strong bunch of actors, who were however playing to an audience of professional egotists, prepared to tolerate monologues only when delivered by themselves.

The fashion apparently grew from the work of a mysteriously reclusive resident playwright named Jane Martin. Nobody knew, or anyway was letting on, quite who Miss Martin is (or, as some sceptics would have it, are), but I side with those who hold that she must be an actress. Of the 11 numbers she contributed, comprising a programme called Talking With, one depicted a leading lady, in her dressing-room pondering her audience ("how can they live in a town without one decent Chinese restaurant?"), another a desperate auditioner, and both of these wit triumphed over whimsy; in too many of the others it lost out. Miss Martin's overall title suggested to me a rule, not infallible, for one-person plays. They are more effective when the speaker is talking with an actual unseen someone than with himself or herself. Solitary musing too often turns to self-pity. Even the touring actress at one point told us, "I am not an entertainment." Tough, maybe.

An actor called Robert Schenkkan wrote and performed a chilling, deadpan, mock-lecture about post-holocaust survival. This was almost the only play in the festival you could accuse of having political content; monologues in general are turned inexorably inwards like most American drama, only more so. A writer in New York's Village Voice recently arraigned British playwrights from Peter Shaffer on the right to Trevor Griffiths and Howard Barker on the left for tidying up experience. I know what he means, but the American alternative seems, as they might put it in Kentucky, right sloppy.

A couple of years ago Louisville plays afforded the visitor some insight into different levels of American society: from the alienated working class Mid-West to the alienated affluent of California. This year we have been taken, as if by Greyhound time-machine, to witness charity and suspicion in Minnesota 1915, cosiness and compassion in Arkansas 1951, and madness and mayhem in Nebraska now; but the settings, and even the dates, seemed immaterial; all that mattered was what Tennessee Williams once called "personal lyricism," sweet or sour.

This year's likeliest candidate for Broadway (everybody who writes about the festival ends up sounding like a stringer for Variety, weighing up commercial chances) was Oldtimers Game, a baseball comedy that lapsed into sentimentality, but had an attractively jaunty air to it. It belongs to the salty, bustling American genre epitomised for all time by The Front Page, and represented at Louisville two years ago by a piece called They're Coming To Make It Brighter. The author of that one, Kent Broadhurst, this time moved down in scale, but up in quality, with The Eye of the Beholder, a witty, logic-chopping, role-reversing playlet about two artists and their model. It was the best play of the festival.

The big guns, though, were two adaptations of novels, both familiar to us as John Ford movies. The Informer gradually recovered from an impenetrable start, the fruit of obscure plotting and dubious accents, without ever persuading us that another play about Dublin in the Troubles is what the theatre needs now. It had a fine production by the theatre's director, Jon Jory; a wizard with large casts. His touch was much missed in The Grapes of Wrath; Steinbeck's story still grips shamelessly, at least when disasters start mounting up, but the stifling nobility of his persecuted migrants was in no way mitigated by presentation and acting that made these Oklahoma workers look like the Beverly Hillbillies.

This, though, was an exceptional lapse. Acting standards were very high, with individual players recurring rapidly and gratifyingly throughout the weekend. Susan Kingsley, who moved from a funny old geriatric to a graphically frightened young wife, is a major actress, scarcely less good than Anne Pitoniak, who presented a formidable number of crabby old ladies without ever getting her wires crossed.
