Rock around the Bard

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN

THE word is that the musical of Two Gentlemen of Verona (Phoenix) pays no attention to the themes of its original. This is unfair. The play is more narrowly concerned with love in its purest (which is to say its silliest) state than any other Shakespeare, and the musical has its own gloss on this. It comes at us loud, funky and often: "You can't love another without loving yourself."

That's almost good Shakespeare, though by the time he got to, say, Twelfth Night, he might have wanted to substitute "without knowing yourself." Self-love is what Malvolio was said to be sick of, and this show has caught the plague.

It suffers from the Godspell blight; it will do anything to be likeable. It is colourful and teeming with action: that much to its credit. But its idea of modernising Shakespeare is just to make the sex as explicit as possible. (Those poor, sonneteering Elizabethans with their Platonism and their Courtly Love, what did they know?)

So Silvia becomes a nymphomaniac. Proteus does not merely desert Julia; he gets her pregnant, though not without making provision for her future. He has at least ensured that she will get a laugh when, disguised as a boy, she reaches the line about women changing their shapes.

If it isn't sex, it's protest. There is, though it looks a little wan now, a Vietnam angle with the Duke of Milan conceived as (you should pardon the expression) a stylish President Nixon.

In New York, the show presumably had authentic American performers. By the time it got to the Phoenix, only two such natives were left in the cast. When B. J. Arnau and Samuel E. Wright combine on a number called "Night Letter," the whole house wakes up and realises, with a shock, that it is enjoying itself. Miss Arnau is, from her first entrance, a majestic Silvia; to her let us garlands bring. Mr Wright takes longer to establish himself but develops into a sweet comic Valentine with the lungs and the humour to tear a soul ballad properly apart.

Proteus (Ray C. Davis) displays similar verve when confronted by a calypso. He is a toothy performer, a quality which sorts well with calypsos, but not with much else. The supporting players must have looked at the script and run each for his peculiar brand of cover. We get a funny old Italian. We get a Scots wench with maxi-boobs, apt to break silence by saying, "We were just comparing you to a summer's day." (That's bringing Shakespeare to the people.) We get Benny Lee, attempting to impose Anglo-Jewish rhythms on Shakespeare's funniest stand-up comic. We get, at the curtain call, dozens of people I didn't remember seeing before.

Doing his own thing most furiously, but most forgivably, of all is Derek Griffiths, whose talent is to contort face, body and voice, gloriously and often simultaneously. One of nature's stars, he is condemned in his present role to be only (and this in a cast which includes a dog) an unrivalled scene stealer.

My view of the show has possibly been jaundiced by the blatant plugging it received on an appalling television documentary last Sunday. Throughout this, I noticed, the most celebrated member of the creative team--the composer, Galt MacDermot--kept very quiet.

Is there a real Mr MacDermot? His present score is fecund and ingratiatingly bouncy, but it has no character and very little body. It uses pastiche as a painless device for keeping the music coming and the party going. It implicitly obeys the two fashionable rules for rock musicals: the music forsakes rock as often as possible to parody other forms and the lyrics are painful.

There is matter in Tom Gallacher's Schellenbrack (King's Head) to touch any critic. One of our breed, name of Cope, momentously confesses to have changed his opinions. "Then how," comes the reply, "do I know you're Cope?"

That crusher falls from the lips of Schellenbrack himself, an historian with his own identity problem. He may be a Nobel Prizewinner and he may be mad. Mr Gallacher specialises in ambiguous portraits of genius and he has never before been so well served by his actor. The part requires a powerful physical impact to be filtered through a mercurial temperament and Wolfe Morris supplies both.

The King's Head is an Islington pub, but the play kept transporting me to the boozers round Broadcasting House. On its breath I smelled the liquorous intellectuality of the old Third Programme. Mr Gallacher's studies--written later, but produced earlier at this theatre--of an Ibsen-intoxicated actor and a Joyce-intoxicated Joyce had summoned similar associations. The current piece is a literary detective story--a favourite radio genre--but Mr Gallacher is no storyteller. Schellenbrack benefits from witty dialogue and some delectable insights, but he is a glittering portrait in search of a frame.
