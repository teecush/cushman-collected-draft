Theatre Review

Friends, Romans, the lot

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

Peter Gill's production of Julius Caesar (Riverside Studios) has the best Shakespearian crowd scenes in recent memory. There is nothing apologetic about them; no pretence that three or four actors can -- if symbolically grouped, murkily lit and encouraged to stomp or chant in unison -- do the work of a dozen.

Mr Gill has about 20 actors to play with and he sends them on in swiftly varying formations: as soldiers -- standing, turning and even executing a balletic battle that is over before you have time to question it -- or as citizens. In the intervening scenes they play individual officers or conspirators, two or three apiece. But the doubling is never either obtrusive or confusing.

All the men are dressed in the same grey uniform: tunics, socks and long winter underwear. The effect is not prepossessing, but it is immensely useful -- an enshrouding anonymity from which each player can emerge and to which he can return. While on, he has to trust to the script and his own skills to establish him; and though the level of success is variable no-one actually fails. The play is kept taut.

The scenes of full orchestration are thrilling. Brutus and Antony make their orations from precarious plinths actually borne aloft by the mob they are addressing: a foot or a word wrong and they will slip and, presumably, be torn to pieces. Or be stoned to death: as happens, in the ensuing savagely staged scene, to Cinna the poet. As the lights go down on his remains, the first act ends with an off-stage shout of 'Caesar', a cry repeated at the close when the only figure visible, upright this time, is that of the glacial Octavius.

The effect -- cynically cyclic -- is the nearest Mr Gill comes to explicit political comment: kill a dictator and gain an emperor. Robert Flemyng's Caesar, though, lets you see why he was killed. Mr Flemyng must have a good 30 years on anyone else in the cast, but his career has not included much Shakespeare. So he is liable at first to appear both outmoded and unpractised: caged in mannerisms. But his isolation emphasises Caesar's own -- physically infirm, but inhumanly brave because afraid to admit to being anything else. Mr Gill has staged the murder superbly -- a series of sadistic lunges topped by the victim's suicidal advance on Brutus's sword, while the heavens belch red smoke -- and in dying with a Latin tag on his lips, Mr Flemyng strikes his finest attitude.

John Shrapnel's Brutus is another, even richer, study in egotism. He argues himself fascinatingly into killing Caesar for fear of what he might do, quizzically spelling out the arguments and then rushing, self-hypnotised, into a murderous conclusion. Brutus himself speaks of his state as 'a hideous dream' and this one never really wakes up.

Michael Byrne's Cassius, apart from a fine early spurt of venom, comes over as a solid Englishman out of Simon Gray; and John Price's Antony is little more than vigorous. The finest colouring in the production comes, unexpectedly, from Portia: a brief role into which Lindsay Duncan packs an infinity of poise, anguish and affection. Even in silence she rivets respect. Other performances are adequate if vocally limited. As stagecraft this is Riverside's finest offering to date.

It would be hypocritical to pretend that any critic approached Barnardo (Royalty) -- billed as a family musical -- expecting it to be anything but appalling. There must, what with Oliver and Annie, be a severe shortage of singing and dancing orphans by now; and the choice of theme and hero hardly seemed to promise astringency.

London's East End is the title of the first number. It seems a nice enough place, and not even the discovery of a few starving children can stop the chorus singing about it. But perhaps it's more menacing than it seems. One notices that none of the inhabitants dares to make a move on his own; and the exercise they do take, though certainly energetic, is severely limited: they rarely get beyond flinging their legs at the audience and their arms at one another.

This affliction strikes the population of the neighbourhood very young. Even when removed to the first Barnardo home -- and sleeping, incidentally, three in a bed -- they cannot refrain from sitting up in unison, folding their arms, rocking from side to side, putting their hands behind their heads and swaying. These are boys; the girls (they arrive in the second act, rather arbitrarily I thought, but maybe the Equal Opportunities Commission operates at unexpectedly low levels) are saucier; they turn repeatedly upstage and jut their behinds out. One even breaks ranks.

Their number is called 'Girls'; the boys' bedtime routine is entitled 'Snuggle Up.' I hate to think what effect these early experiences will have on their future development, let alone that of the British musical. One of the lads is Jewish and has a solo 'Oy Vey.' Lord Shaftesbury is needed for the story and, providentially, otherwise how would we work in 'The Midnight Waltz,' he is throwing a ball. (Not having a ball; only Victorian Cockneys use language like that.)

Barnardo wins a wife, with absolutely no trouble. In Act Two he loses her, because their son dies of pneumonia. She blames him for this, possibly because instead of putting him to bed (which might have occasioned a meaningful reprise of 'Snuggle Up'), he held the barefoot boy in his arms while singing at him, for what seemed like hours, a ballad ('My Son') about all the things he might have achieved had he grown up.

Ernest Maxin is credited as author, director and composer, and his work is certainly consistent. He has fashioned a collector's item, right down to the obligatory distinguished actor (John Arnatt) lifting voice and linking arms in praise of 'Lovely Hot Pies' (he's playing Shaftesbury, by the way) and the permanent crackle on the PA system. I wouldn't have missed it for the world.

Sam Shepard's Seduced (Theatre Upstairs) is, I suppose, a more distinguished piece of work, but it suffers from the same inability to do more with its central figure than just put him there. He is, more or less, Howard Hughes, discovered lying in a dentist's chair with fingernails a mile long (Image I). This is his Endgame, and he has a surly attendant to push the point. Two women arrive, and attempt to lure him back to life with many words and a striptease. (Image II.) The play ends with the stage bathed in the protagonist's blood. (Image III.) The Shepard plays I have enjoyed have been moved along as briskly as possible; this one sinks in the verbiage, despite striking performances all round.

Theatermanufaktur, a roving troupe from West Berlin, have brought to the Old Vic Murieta, which seems to be a free adaptation of a parable of Chilean revolution by Pablo Neruda. It was almost comforting to note that German actors seemed hardly more comfortable playing Latin peasants than our own, but the visual impact of Otto Zonschitz's production is considerable. The verbal was difficult to judge, owing to a faulty translation system (now apparently rectified). Even so, I wondered whether the liberal use of a narrator was not draining out the drama.
