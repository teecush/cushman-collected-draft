Fats on the stage

Theatre

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

A FRIEND of mine, a jazz pianist, walked out of the New York production of Ain't Misbehavin' (Her Majesty's). In doing so he missed out on a considerable quantity of fun, but his purist's response to this much-acclaimed Fats Waller compilation deserves some attention.

Waller's reputation, unshakable since the thirties (he died in 1943), is quadruple: he was pianist, composer, singer and clown. Of these four talents, the first was most obviously forfeit in the theatre. Luther Henderson, the piano man here, functions merely as a skilful and sympathetic accompanist: he is also the MD, and his men swing when they get the chance. A lyric in the show--a new and good one by the director, Richard Maltby Jr.--sports the wry line: "They like jazz but in small doses." Small doses are all that the theatre can assimilate; and my friend--who like most good jazzmen has in his time adapted dozens of show-tunes to his own purposes--should not logically have complained at the traffic being reversed.

It is more surprising that the show has ironed out Waller's verbal humour: the tolerant, disbelieving asides with which he sprinkled his vocals. The crazily elegant catch-phrases are reverently preserved, but instead of gently murmuring "Mercy!" or "One never knows, do one?" these performers declaim them. Trying to find a sizeable extrovert equivalent for Waller's intimate addresses, they assault the audience, especially during the first 15 minutes, with exaggerated coyness.

But the show takes its stand on Waller as singer and composer; every number we hear was written or recorded by him. (One of the surprises of Fats's career is how little overlap there was between these two categories.) He was not a great vocalist; and the two songs--"When Somebody Thinks You're Wonderful" and "My Very Good Friend the Milkman"--with which he came nearest to making a style out of rhythmic affability, are omitted here. So there is no question of imitation.

The cast are free to make the songs their own, their only impediment being the rather hollow soul-based seventies style to which the ladies in particular tend to resort in moments of stress and which can knock the individuality out of any voice or any vehicle.

They are a dazzling quintet, though, these performers. Andree de Shields is a devil-dancer whose voice drips feline disdain; his sinuous account of the reefer song ("If You're a Viper") raises the Harlem dandy to a new, and distinctly sinister, power; here the thirties and the seventies really do illuminate each other. Where he is all curves, his colleague Evan Bell is all bumps and angles: tall, broad and vulnerable, but still capable of turning "Your Feet's Too Big" into a bruised aria of insult.

Talking of insult, the two men also bring the house down with "Fat and Greasy" and pay acid tribute to "The Ladies Who Sing With the Band" by way of bringing on the girls. These are Annie Joe Edwards, a hugely cherishable mama; Charlaine Woodard, a baby tigress with a streak of self-mockery; and Jozella Reed, whose demure rendition of "Squeeze Me" is the first thing to set the evening alight.

That song proves one of the gems among Waller's own compositions, along with "I've Got a Feeling I'm Falling," "Keepin' Out of Mischief Now" (which amounts to a delicate reworking of the title tune) and, obviously, "Honeysuckle Rose." They make an appealingly and unaffectedly sexy bunch. But the show makes no musical point that could not as well have been gained by choosing at large among the songs of the period.

In the end, it is words rather than music that determine a programme; and Fats was not a lyricist. The songs tell us nothing much about him, and those lyrics that tell us about his environment or even his style of playing turn out to be Mr Maltby's own, fitted to Waller instrumentals. Even this much sense of period, allied to the snap and logic of the staging, is enough to leave Bubbling Brown Sugar struggling in the mud; and the show takes one risk that its predecessor would never have countenanced. The cast unite in an exquisite lament called "Black and Blue" that must have seemed charmingly ingenuous to its first white audience in 1929 and embarrassing to most people since. That black performers can sing it now--with passion but without apparent bitterness, placing it in its history without denying it--seems to me a sign of progress.

There's another Broadway hit across the street. The Crucifer of Blood (Haymarket) is another example of the American theatre's obsession with British Victoriana, most creatively exemplified in New York at the moment by Sweeney Todd; and The Elephant Man. The Crucifer belongs more at the Dracula end of things and is a new contribution to the Sherlock Holmes apocrypha.

Paul Giovanni, the author-director, makes affectionate fun of his hero without materially injuring him, though his plotting can be hurtful. Holmes has (a) to let a man be killed almost under his nose, (b) to deduce things we have already been told, which makes us unfairly impatient with him, and (c) I haven't the heart for (c) but the last five minutes are a real let-down. Up till then Mr Giovanni keeps his action and his hero moving inventively and spectacularly enough for us to be pleasured. Keith Michell's Holmes is sound, despite the falsest of noses and a nasty tendency to breathlessness in the throes of exposition, Denis Lill is a thoroughly worthy Watson, John Cater an admirable inspector, Susan Hampshire a provocatively demure heroine; while Edward Petherbridge takes us from a wasted youth in the Indian Mutiny through guilt-ridden old age in darkest Maidenhead to final peace in Limehouse's opium dens.

Willy Russell's One for the Road (Nottingham Playhouse) is about life on a housing estate and the difficulties of escaping from it. Early patronising laughter (at the mention of Beethoven Close and Mahler Crescent) freezes as a discontented young man quails before a professional harpy (his best friend's wife) and a keen amateur (his own), makes a bid for freedom and finds that his tormentors would be only too happy to join him.

Mr Russell switches moods rather too mechanically, but he knows where he is at any given moment. He has written a gentle account of a tough situation, nowhere more unnerving than in his hero's realisation of pleasures he is still permitted. They seem to boil down to adultery. Alun Armstrong gives a sweet performance in the lead.

Beowulf, a contribution to Puppet Theatre '79, was booked for the hospitality of the National Theatre, who had in the event to farm it out to St George's, Tufnell Park. Its creator, Christopher Leith, seems on the evidence to have been fired by the Japanese Bunraku theatre into attempting a puppet-play of comparable obscurity.

Finding a suitable theme in the Anglo-Saxon, he loaded it down with oriental music, while neglecting the Bunraku's sense of scale. He gave his puppets no environment, making them difficult to look at. His Fireworm had an elephant's trunk and wore a ballet-skirt, which was interesting in a giggly way. There were two readers, the lady more oppressively elocutionary than the gentleman. Boredom arrived early and stayed late.
