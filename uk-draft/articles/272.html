Richard Rodgers

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the great songwriter who died last week aged 77.

THE Richard Rodgers catalogue of standard songs extends from 1925 ('Manhattan') to 1951 ('Hello Young Lovers'). He wrote some songs before that period and many after, but that neat quarter-century encloses the work for which he will be remembered: the dazzling series of songs with lyricist Lorenz Hart, and four rock-solid collaborations with Oscar Hammerstein ('Oklahoma!', 'Carousel,' 'South Pacific,' 'The King and I').

Rodgers and Hart produced the best songs, Rodgers and Hammerstein the best shows. Hammerstein was librettist as well as lyricist, and an excellent architect; the added weight in Rodgers's music seemed to derive less from the lyrics than from the concept of the show: the integrated musical with all the songs dependent on the story (though in their last shows, notoriously in 'The Sound of Music' - their engagingly charitable outlook subsided into blandness).

In the Twenties, and especially in the Thirties, Rodgers and Hart turned Manhattan, as they had promised, into an isle of joy. Theirs was the style of the isle; hard-driving, witty, self-mocking, just occasionally passionate. Hart, who was engaged in drinking himself to death, was a very uneven writer, but his best work was superb and brought out the best in Rodgers. 'Thou Swell,' for example, is a joke song and a love song at once.

Rodgers, by contrast, was a survivor. Better than any of his great contemporaries (Kern, Berlin, Gershwin, Porter) he could write music with situation and character, though he had been in business for 25 years before his partnership with Hammerstein gave him the chance. Despite his major break in style, there was one mode at which Rodgers was always a master: he was America's greatest composer of waltzes. No theatrical writer of this century can have given more pleasure to more people.
