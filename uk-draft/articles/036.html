THREE of this week's plays are revivals; the fourth might just as well be. Indeed, Jean Anouilh's You Were So Sweet When You Were Little, though technically a British premiere, might almost count as a double revival. Based on and incorporating occasional chunks of Aeschylus's Libation Bearers, it also bears a startlingly close resemblance to M. Anouilh's Antigone of 30 years ago.

Once again there is a grubbily intransigent Greek heroine: Electra this time, spurring on her brother Orestes to murder their mother, Clytemnestra, and her lover, Aegisthus, in retribution for the death of Electra's father, Agamemnon, who had himself been guilty of the blood of his daughter Iphigeneia. (The family that slays together stays together.) Now Antigone's aims, you may remember, were more limited (her relatives were mostly dead already, she merely wanted to bury one of them), but it was clear that for her one pretext for revolt would be as good as any other. At the time of Antigone's premiere in Occupied France everyone, except presumably the Germans, applauded her, but in the years that have passed the arguments of her opponent, Creon, have come to seem increasingly reasonable, and his condemnation of her futile, selfish heroism increasingly persuasive.

The new play is perhaps an attempt to put the record straight: to show that Anouilh was rooting for Creon all along. Electra dwells lovingly on the martyrdom she has imposed upon herself, while the nicest character is Aegisthus, not only doomed but henpecked, and coated in puppy-fat. Anouilh is cunning enough, having aroused our sympathy for him, to turn it against us. When Orestes taunts him for the very stolidity of his long-suffering virtues ("Why not draw us a map of your varicose veins?") we wince, wondering if we have been had.

As you can see, and would expect, this is a well-written but redundant play. Nothing but historical accident makes it seem much inferior to Antigone; the change in emphasis reflects the times as much as the text. Once, when Anouilh's Chorus proclaimed that tragedy was a restful genre, we took it for an oblique commendation of Antigone, a tragic heroine; now it seems an ironic indictment of Electra, luxuriating in the same status. In general, I find Anouilh's mythical reworkings his least satisfactory plays; this business of debunking Aeschylus and Sophocles has been going on for millennia and none has done it so well as Euripides, who really had a stake in what he was attacking. The current production opens the Theatre at New End in Hampstead. I saw it at a preview, when it was rough but not ready. Angela Pleasence is good, obvious casting for Electra.

When I spoke last week of the misogyny variously evinced by Lindsay Kemp and Paul Raymond I should have added that they have plenty of exemplars in the classier spoken drama. M. Anouilh hints that things might have been all right, with the House of Atreus had Orestes and Aegisthus been able to get together out of reach of those awful Furies, women. A golden time-suspending drink scene works to much the same effect in the second offering from the Peter Hall regime at the National, J. B. Priestley's Eden End. Note the title; we have already had The Tempest, and are promised Heartbreak House and John Gabriel Borkman, further studies in finality, before the year is out. Mr Hall may be moving towards a concept of Terminal Theatre.

Eden End is the end of innocence; in October 1912 a North Country doctor, his son and two daughters come briefly together for what we, through hindsight and a certain amount of conscientious prodding from Mr Priestley, know will be the last time. The prodding is the play's most irritating feature; we get occasional hopeful speeches about how much better life is going to be in the 1930s, which happens to be when Mr Priestley was writing the play. He is thus able to attain by conscious effort the sort of effect which time and the Russian Revolution achieved for Chekhov.

But along with much that is takingly Chekhovian, the play offers a few things that are truly Chekhovian: the end -- the very end, the last few seconds -- and that drunk scene, here enacted by Paul Gregory and Michael Jayston. There is a lot of Mr Gregory -- length-wise, at any rate -- and it gets itself draped in helpless, intriguing shapes on chairs and sofas, while the face above it stares, gaunt and admiring, at Mr Jayston, whose inebriated oratory deserves the compliment, though elsewhere his portrait of a middle-aged boy actor is rather too restrained and solid. In one respect the play can be ranked with The Seagull; with no scenes in or near theatres, but with much talk of touring and of the Sunday train to London ("it's a brute"), it creates and paints a wholly convincing picture of the actor's life.

Laurence Olivier's production, which, again, I saw at a preview, is occasionally stiff, but more often rich and expansive. (The Mermaid's An Inspector Calls was brusque by comparison.) On this showing Mr Priestley writes the kind of flat dialogue from which the right actors can sculpt mountains or excavate hollows: Leslie Sands and Geoffrey Palmer are triumphantly the right actors here. Joan Plowright brings to the central role a captivating laconic wit and an undiminished gift for expressing momentary happiness; but she is also liable to swirl around in an undefined emotional wash.

Arnold Wesker's Roots has been accorded a moderate but enjoyable revival by the Young Vic, with Tamara Ustinov, a very perky Beatie Bryant. It is, in both senses, a funny play. It has acquired a reputation for buoyant optimism, but what lingers from it is a picture of family life which, if not quite as mordant as M. Anouilh's, is a good deal blacker than Mr Priestley's.

We all know that The Way of the World is a great text; the Actors' Company production at Wimbledon, with an Edwardian setting and immeasurably improved, since the Edinburgh Festival, reveals it as a great play, racy, cutting, dynamic, and very nearly comprehensible. "O man, man," howls Paola Dionisotti's Marwood in an ecstasy of jealous fury, and then relaxes into what is for her a melting mood (i.e. one of lingering self-contempt) to add "woman, woman!" Robin Ellis's Fainall is a more natural cynic, but in him too sexual nihilism is spurred on by self-hatred. What Millamant and Mirabell have to offer -- to each other and to us -- is self-respect: Edward Petherbridge, without losing any of his critical sense, has come far enough out of his shell to make a believable lover as well as a stylish schemer, and though Caroline Blakiston has been set (by herself, her director or the Millamant legend) the impossible task of acting radiance she succeeds surprisingly often; only she surrenders far too easily.

John Woodvine's ebullient Sir Wilful, formerly an isolated comic force, is now part of a triple alliance, flanked as he is by Margery Mason's agreeably vulnerable Lady Wishfort and by John Tordoff whose mannerisms (chiefly a falsetto sigh at the end of every line) remain the same from play to play, but are here used to expose young Witwoud's patronising pretensions, spiced by an insecurity that almost becomes endearing.
