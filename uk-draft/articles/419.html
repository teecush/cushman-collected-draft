Living in the dark

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN in London and Glasgow

THERE are two plays going on in Crystal Clear (Wyndhams), which in a short show is remarkable and - since they don't get in one another's way - very good value. One is an informative account of the experience of blindness. Of all the afflictions recently examined on the stage, this is probably the one most suited to exploration in the theatre; the one most easily related to what the audience itself experiences.

There are three characters: Thomasina, who is blind; Jane, who can see; and Richard, a diabetic who loses his sight in the course of the action. The most electric moment comes after a scene between Thomasina and Richard; he is now blind, so obviously the light is off. Enter Jane, who switches it on. The audience, who have also grown accustomed to the gloom but still find it frustrating, blink.

Earlier we have watched a one-eyed Richard, trying to seduce Thomasina, bring her back to his flat and show her round. That may be an odd choice of phrase, in the circumstances, but it's how he behaves; he even hides the underwear that, being a born slob, he has left lying about. "What are your first impressions?" he asks. "It feels," she says, "very spacious."

The piece was evolved by its three actors, under the direction of Phil Young. They have uncovered extraordinarily fruitful territory; the opportunities for heartrending comedy are almost limitless. With remarkable tact they pass on to other ground. Richard and Jane are at the fag-end of a relationship. She wants commitment from him: failing that, confrontation. He will do anything - including, it seems, go blind - to avoid confrontation. What Richard wants is to be dependent, on his own terms: a neurosis which, allied here to great joky charm, lifts the play partially clear of the current rut of disability drama.

Does that sound grudging? The play is thoroughly honest but obstinately small, and the two qualities are interdependent. A row, here, is just a row; so baldly that Jane (Diana Barrett), meant to be caring but tactless, comes out bloody-minded. Anthony Allen's Richard is finely balanced between the attractive and the unforgiveable, and Philomena McDonagh gives Thomasina an aspect of serenity that the play finally shatters.

The shattering, though, is where the production falls shortest: when the two blind lovers lose their defences, we get two actors shouting at each other. But the piece pulls itself together, ending, with universally applicable bleakness, as a picture of love trying to shut out the dark. Images like that seem impossible to avoid. The play vibrates with them. Jane says to Richard, "I'm fed up with a relationship where we never see each other." Intentionally or not, it rings.

I don't know why Michael Wilcox wrote Lent (Lyric Studio, Hammersmith), but I'm glad he did. He tells of a boy being 13 in 1956; as Mr Wilcox was himself, and as I was. Greedily I lapped up the references: Journey into Space, the Goons, The Cruel Sea (cadet edition), the Jennings books, Ainsworth's antiquated Old St Paul's borrowed from the school library and cherished for its horrific bits. Only the local cinema bothered me, showing The Crimson Pirate and Captain Horatio Hornblower; but maybe they were revivals, shrewdly programmed for school holidays.

Our orphaned hero spends these holidays at school; he has to, since his grandmother owns it, or at least holds it in trust for him. This is one of a series of puns supporting the temporal title. The boy starts by "borrowing" a starting pistol. "Don't be foolish, Paul," says his headmaster, temporarily misleading us as to the kind of play we're getting into, "hand over the gun."

The headmaster is a narrow man, but he is also a disappointed one, and Dennis Edwards makes him comprehensible and almost sympathetic: ditto Jean Anderson as his peremptory wife, who lets murder stories go to her head. Grandma, whom they simultaneously fear and despise, gives Patience Collier her best, and best taken, opportunity for years: dignified and probably wise. An assistant master, Paul's confidant, is given an enchantingly easygoing performance by Wensley Pithey: beset, like all the adults, by a long consciousness of failure, but transmuting it into autumnal avuncularity.

The play, indeed, has some of the shock-effect of 84 Charing Cross Road; everybody in it is decent. This even applies to Paul, played without embarrassment (high praise for a grown-up) by Jonathan Kent. I can't see that it is about anything more than the delayed consequences of having been born in 1943. I think that's a superb subject, but I'm biased.

The Custom of the Country (Citizens', Glasgow) written in 1619 by Fletcher and Massinger, is the most improbable play I have ever seen. The custom is a droit du seigneur; to escape it, two heroes and a heroine rush off to Lisbon, where they are separated, and meet with many strange and sexy adventures.

One fellow becomes the prey of a rich nymphomaniac; his ruttish brother is employed as a stud in a brothel for ladies. Both get exhausted. There are also duels and potions. After five acts have elapsed the play stops.

This kind of purely narrative, purely titillating drama stopped being written when the theatre ceased to be a mass medium. It passed, of course, to the cinema; accordingly Robert David Macdonald, directing, and Michael Levine, designing, have set it in Hollywood, or at least have peopled it with movie archetypes. After an outrageously drab prologue we pass to a land full of pink furniture, lit by studio lamps.

Guiomar (Jill Spurrier), a predatory matron, is Gloria Swanson in Sunset Boulevard; Duarte (Rupert Farley), her braggart son, is Mickey Mouse, complete with tail; Zabulon (Ron Donachie), the sinister Jew, is Sydney Greenstreet; and Hippolyta (Fidelis Morgan) sounds like Mae West, but looks like Jean Harlow; whichever, her entrance, acknowledging applause and clutching an Oscar, had me laughing very loud. The melodrama can be played full out, and the occasional genuine emotion can quite unexpectedly register.

The less lovable side of the Citizens' is also on show. Heavy cuts become, by the end, cavalier and confusing; and whatever became of the funniest line, a ruffian's declaration that he is always available for a murder, but he's out of practice at beating? More serious is a pervasive misogyny that delights in leaving all the women frustrated, and plays the plight of the knackered gigolo (Lorcan Cranitch) not for laughs against him, but for horror of the predatory female sex. Still, where else?

Clare Davidson's production of Miss Julie (Duke of York's) is much the most lucid I have seen, particularly in its marking off of the social and sexual components in the confrontation of lady and servant. Stephen Rea as Jean lets us see every calculation, every spasm of lust, every pang of fear. Cheryl Campbell, tresses flaming, is audacious to a fault, sounding as if she has wound herself up in the wings, and doesn't dare let go. Occasionally she touches ground and is great, but she is in danger of acting herself into a corner.
