The cut-throat barber

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on the National's Figaro.

LOUIS XVI was dismayed by Beaumarchais's The Marriage of Figaro, but aristocratic society in general laughed all the way to the scaffold: not such a long ride, since the play preceded the Revolution by only five years.

In one defiant line of Figaro's, Lytton Strachey, of whom more later, detected the voice of the guillotine. But this, the play's most oratorical moment, was presented, not in the dark and sparkling light of frivolity, but as the natural extension of intrigue and vapid lovemaking among the antiquated remains of feudal privileges and manners. The intrigue was the frivolity.

Some inconsistency there, but it faithfully reflects the play, which is more notable for comic energy than for unity of tone, or even of action. It is constructed like an old-time movie serial; there is a predestined goal--the marriage of Figaro and Suzanne--and a series of intrigues which run, not in parallel, but in series. Just when you think the plot is exhausted, it starts all over again.

Jonathan Miller's Old Vic Theatre production at the Old Vic does not so much solve these problems as override them. At times I have found his work over-solemn, as if he were trying to illuminate while dimming the lights. This, however, is one of his more joyous offerings, fun for both him and us.

Mr Miller's style occasionally recalls that of the late Tyrone Guthrie. He does not possess Guthrie's consummate showmanship, but he permits his small-part players a similar licence. The practical result--as in The School for Scandal--is a household full of eccentric domestics. One minor role--a justice's clerk--is played perfectly straight; lacking even a facial tic the actor looks startlingly naked, and one feels like ordering him back to his dressing-room.

Wily, experienced players profit most under this dispensation. The most successful of the "secondary characters" (Beaumarchais so described them in his stage directions, before the term was converted to the delicate treatment accorded them here) is the justice himself. In the person of Howard Goorney, he crawls across the stage bent double; an exit begun as the curtain falls on Act One nears completion as it rises on Act Two.

The principal characters have their eccentricities as well. The crucial opposition, as realised here, is the duel between Figaro and the Count: hard-worked wit against hereditary privilege. It is underlined in the casting; Derek Godfrey, an accomplished and experienced classical comedian, gives of his best as the Count, a scheming, ageing seducer excessively liable to accident and frustration. On this occasion, he admits with ruffled dignity, "I was wrong." I forget to which of his mishaps he refers; there are so many. Most characteristically he finds himself caught up in a peasant dance while attempting to pass a note to his intended; trying, for the sake of appearances, to enter into the spirit of the thing, he raises his hands gaily above his head and unconsciously adopts the formal posture of a trained dancer.

Matched against him is Gawn Grainger, who has done sturdy and versatile supporting work at the National, as Figaro. All he lacks is the ultimate turn of speed in soliloquy. Figaro, as everybody knows, entered theatrical history as a barber. With its eyes firmly on the past, Mr Miller has had him revert to his old trade. As his razor plays deftly around his master's chin, Mr Grainger scores for both role and for the company he is representing. It is a neat yet broadened world of catch-throat competition. The production, like the text, tends to sprawl; in the last act Gemma Jones as the Countess and Nicola Pagett as Suzanne are called on to engage in mutual impersonations, which may be a sly comment on The School for Scandal, or it could be carelessness. But the heart of the play--at least in this staging--beats strongly enough.

Johnny Mercer is one of the great men of our time; a new revue built around some of his own lyrics (from You Must Have Been a Beautiful Baby to Moon River and beyond) has cheered my week considerably. His contributions to The Good Companions (Her Majesty's) did less for me (as they will, I suspect, for him); his best work is steeped in Americana (rural-bucolic or urban-hip) and, though the Englishness of his current subject has not actually defeated him, it has cost him audible effort.

Indeed the J. B. Priestley novel has proved an unwieldy source for all concerned. The first act is all flashback, the second mostly wrap-up; the Good Companions themselves--a touring concert party--are squeezed out of the action, and as a backstage musical the show only fitfully takes off. Even the love-interest is belatedly introduced and perfunctorily dispatched. Judi Dench's charm and conviction, as always, are largely wasted (she is given ballads beyond her range instead of the belters she handled so well in Cabaret). John Mills, shyly and fetchingly, takes up where he left off in the film.

Still, Mercer can hardly help writing singable words (he would have done better with a different subject), and some of the numbers have their amiable moments. The production relies heavily on the all-purpose jollity which traditionally fuels the English musical.

The view from Dublin pier

E. A. WHITEHEAD'S The Sea Anchor (Theatre Upstairs) is a searing, sombre little play about the misery of sexual relationships, within and without marriage, in a society where there are no rules other than male/female archetypes.

The characters are four working-class Liverpudlians in Dublin for a dirty weekend: Les, apparently coarse and brutal; Sylvia, his naive, defenceless girlfriend; Andy, a romantic, gentle and self-deceiving; and Jean, affectedly cynical and inwardly hysterical. Out there is a fifth, Nick, Jean's lover, trying to cross from Liverpool to Dublin in a dinghy, not waving but drowning. Nick's hapless voyage, viewed by the quartet during the course of an afternoon, evening and the following morning as they wait for him on a pier, becomes the pivot for the way the characters see themselves and each other. It is an act of suicidal bravado for Nick, torn apart by the jealous devotion of the wife on one shore and the mistress on the other.

Both men see it as such: Les with contempt, Andy with admiration. For the women it is yet another unintelligible gesture of contempt from a man.

It is also a clever dramatic device which allows the quartet to assemble and reassemble on the pier in varying groups, illustrating the different sides of their sexual feelings and ideas. When the two men are together the talk is comradely crudeness; the two women alone are wary, united by a common understanding, but separated by sexual jealousy. Alone with Sylvia, Les is tender; in a group he treats her with brutality. When Les is about, Andy assumes some of his mocking misogyny; with the women he is generous, anxious to please and placate. Les and Jean, both strong-willed and firmly stuck in the prejudices of their sex, despise each other.

This is an intelligent, tight, and meticulously structured play. Jonathan Hales's direction gives it the maximum naturalism with the maximum economy, and John Tindale's lighting casts a bleak view from Dublin pier.
