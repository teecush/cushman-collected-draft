Dazzling Dispute

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a French production of Marivaux.

Theatre critics are a notoriously ungracious, not to say ungrateful, crowd: not only can you not take them anywhere, you can't bring them anything either, certainly not a nicely wrapped company from abroad. Last week, noisily grinding their serpents' teeth, they outdid themselves.

Of the productions--no huge number--that I have seen outside Britain in the last three and a half years, two stand out. One was Pacific Overtures, the American musical over which I rhapsodised earlier this year and which my countrymen now seem unlikely to witness in any form; nobody has the money to put it on the stage and a television version has been rejected by the BBC. The other was Patrice Chéreau's production of Marivaux's La Dispute, which, being the work of a subsidised company--the Théâtre National Populaire--reached us, at the Lyttelton, only to be rejected by everybody else.

What these two shows most noticeably have in common--apart from intelligence, imagination and achieved ambition--is visual splendour. La Dispute is literally a dazzler. Much of it, under the best stage moon I have ever seen, is chiaroscuro, but the shadows are relieved by extraordinary flashes of light, chiefly refracted from mirrors: hard, cruel and, as will appear, eminently suitable.

Marivaux wrote a short piece about a human experiment. Two boys and two girls are each one brought up in isolation, then, when adolescence strikes, turned loose (the production suggests a colossal game of blind-man's buff) to meet one another. The object of the trial is to discover, by recreating the conditions of Eden, which of the sexes was the first to be unfaithful in love.

Girl One meets Boy One; both fall. Girl Two meets Boy Two; ditto. Girl One meets Girl Two; each is jealous of the other. Boy One meets Boy Two; they make friends. Girl One meets Boy Two; both fall again, though neither has any intention of giving up the previous love. Each wants as much as he or she can get. So dishonours are even; it might be claimed that the boys are the more amiable, but they are also the more stupid. Even their initial chumminess is suspect, since each considers himself impregnably secure in the affection of the most beautiful creature in the world, and so can afford to be kind.

What really possesses each member of the quartet is narcissism. The girl Eglé, of whom we see the most, is enraptured at the start by her own reflection in a stream, then by her own portrait, finally by the looking glass. Love, in a state of nature, is defined as meeting someone who will take you at your own estimation; an easy matter until the opportunity for comparison arises, when it becomes impossible.

Chéreau has literally fleshed out the text; where Marivaux's lovers engage with their hands and lips, his actors encounter with their whole bodies. But this is an extension of the play, not a distortion, and invaluable in making almost imperceptible the border between love-play and violence. Laurence Bourdil's Eglé is a kitten who might grow into a tigress. The force and freedom of her performance and her companions' would be remarkable in any company; in a team of French classicists it is a marvel.

The children have two black guardians, one of them given, in this dispensation, to singing the blues voluptuously well. (Voluptuous is indeed the word for the whole production.) Controlling the action, for the benefit of an inquisitive girl friend, is an anonymous Prince. Their presence prompted one reviewer, though more appreciative than some, to castigate Chéreau for ignoring Marivaux's pre-revolutionary social criticism. This is odd: the eighteenth-century French establishment had much to answer for, but I doubt if it went in on any considerable scale for the kind of emotional vivisection the Prince performs on the four youngsters, and I don't believe that Marivaux intended us to think that it did. It seems rather frivolous for the French revolution.

Actually Chéreau has gone further than his author in criticising the Prince and his company, providing a prologue which convicts them of the same ruthless self-regard as permeates the main action. This too is done with mirrors and with fierce acting; we are then transferred across a smoky abyss to a poisoned paradise, which the direction, Richard Peduzzi's setting--half architectural, half arboreal--and André Diot's lighting make palpable to the eye, to the ear, almost to the palate.

There is varied and ingenious design at the Marlowe, Canterbury, provided by Terry Brown to meet the demand of John Spurling's new play, While Rome Burns. While dissociating themselves on an unlocated island from England and the world, a community of well-heeled game-players come to grief: all save one, and she, in a haunting ending, wishes that she had. The engineer of their destruction is called Edgar Allan; the action is obscurely traceable to his namesake Poe's Mask of the Red Death.

It is obscure in other respects as well; though David Carson's production is resourceful and often stylishly acted, older or more idiosyncratic players might imprint the large but faintly drawn cast more firmly on our minds. But there is much wit in the dialogue and surprise in the action and, come to think of it, vice-versa; though the framework (there are distinct suggestions of a Chinese box) has turned out less sophisticated than may have been intended. It still ranks high among the growing band of doom-watch plays, aware of just how many laughs make a shudder.
