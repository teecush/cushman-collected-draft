Lenin's Testament

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on Robert Bolt's new play and Jacobi's Hamlet.

TOM STOPPARD has pulled the rug out from under any writer who attempts a play about Lenin. One's fond memories of Travesties are revived with unexpected sharpness in the very first speech of Robert Bolt's State of Revolution (Lyttelton), which happens to be delivered by Stephen Moore, a Stoppard actor par excellence. Addressing a Young Communist assembly in the role of Lunacharsky, Commissar for Education, he speaks of Capri, which "as some of you may know is a playground of the international bourgeoisie in its more light-hearted, less obviously offensive aspects." It looks as if we may be in for a merry evening of jargon exposed.

The opening scene puts paid to that. Set in Capri in a villa belonging to Gorky (not offensive, but not very light-hearted either), it lays out the play's battle-lines. It is for the moment Lenin versus the rest, Lunacharsky having produced a thesis which is acclaimed by most of his colleagues, but condemned by Lenin as "unashamed idealism."

Dzerzhinsky, goaded by Lenin, protests that "nobody here is afraid of action." Comes the revolution, he is to be held to that; he is put in charge of the Cheka, which he controls with merciless purity. Stalin, not present on Capri, is referred to as someone "who does what needs doing"; an opinion in which Mr Bolt's Lenin persists until he decides, too late, that what Stalin is doing is not needed after all, or at least that it is not what he himself wants. Having suffered a stroke he can afford to permit himself a few scruples.

Actually this Lenin has scruples all the way through. Mr Bolt's Lenin is, in his own terms, similar to Mr Stoppard's. The Stoppard Lenin was a man with the unusual ability to put ruthless theory into ruthless practice. He also liked music which symbolised the pull of conscience, or, if you prefer, of tender-mindedness. It was a schematic picture which Mr Stoppard's comic vision (summed up in Krupskaya's line "something went wrong . . . I forget what") could just about accommodate.

Mr Bolt's vision is, I suppose, tragic; at any rate it centres on a major figure falling into traps which he himself has set. The play was originally titled Lenin's Testament; perhaps it was felt that State of Revolution had more zing. But the first idea was the right one. Mr Bolt's glimpses of Russia at large are perfunctory. Infighting among the Bolshevik elite is handled with far more relish; the onset of the New Economic Policy sets off a finely orchestrated bitching-game for eight players.

Michael Kitchen's Trotsky emerges as the most gifted participant, running ironic rings round Terence Rigby's workhorse Stalin. Mr Rigby's performance is remarkable; he, more than anyone else on stage, settles into history before our eyes. At each appearance, it seems, his moustache acquires an extra bristle; painfully he learns to smile and each smile is less penetrable than the last. Every time he raises his hand to vote the gesture gets closer to a clenched-fist salute. He has, besides, a blockish manner of speech, a slow sealing of every word, that brings the conventional figure of the unimaginative tyrant to new life.

One is tempted to wish that Mr Bolt had put Stalin, Trotsky or both in the centre of the play, but he would probably have been less successful with them if he had. The biographical approach to history, for a playwright of less than genius, is a perilous one; the man in the middle always comes out unsatisfactory and Mr Bolt merely gives us the Stoppard Lenin at greater length. He spends the play being tugged: by Stalin in one direction, by his gentler colleagues - a conscientious collection with Gorky as visiting good uncle - in the other. Even while instigating action he remains a pawn. A pawn though of drama, not of history; Mr Bolt's characters spend much time debating whether man makes history or vice versa, and philosophically he probably has doubts. Theatrically he is a cult-of-personality subscriber with no dues outstanding.

Hence, perhaps, his choice of Lunacharsky, with his suspicious devotion to bourgeois artistic values, as raisonneur. To cast as Lenin so sympathetic an actor as Michael Bryant seems, whatever your politics, a bit soft, though it does guarantee an intelligent and technically flawless performance. Ralph Koltai's set of mobile slats frames a handsome production by Christopher Morahan, which does justice to the play's narrative virtues; it tells, after all, a very interesting story. The play has been given instant publication by Heinemann Educational Books; a format in which it seems perfectly at home. Mr Bolt's preface shows him to be one of the most intelligent men now writing plays; I wish his dialogue had a similar spark.

Toby Robertson's production of Hamlet (Old Vic) is the kind in which the players arrive for their Elsinore date whooping and tumbling and in which conversations come to guilty halts when soldiers cross the stage. But the Danish royal line comes over as remarkably intelligent.

Like most royals they say things you have heard before, but they make them sound fresh. Timothy West's Claudius is the best performance this super-competent actor has recently given: a narrow-eyed brute who has to force himself to be polite to Hamlet, gets giggles both at the Player-King and Ophelia's first couple of fits, and kneels to prayer with no belief whatever. His love for Barbara Jefford's Gertrude he has unfortunately to compress, owing to cuts, into an outstretched hand after the closet scene which she conscientiously rejects. She too seems often on the verge of losing her temper with her son: better for him, surely, than the indulgence he usually receives.

Derek Jacobi also suffers under the blue pencil; he has lost the "how all occasions" soliloquy, which only happens to be the turningpoint of his character and the play. I object less on Mr Jacobi's account than on my own; I liked listening to him. He is a prince who briefly attains the full passion of the Renaissance avenger ("now could I drink hot blood") and then stops, scared at himself. Earlier he tries to force himself into frenzy with the aid of the players' property sword. Abusing "this too, too solid flesh," he is furious, not mournful; "my father's spirit . . . all is not well" is reassurance. And so on; this is a superbly inflected, very likeable performance, courteous or embittered by turns: line by line surprising but, in the addition, a little short of startling.
