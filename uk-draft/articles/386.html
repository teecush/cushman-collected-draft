Peer Gynt goes roundabout

ROBERT CUSHMAN in Stratford and London

IBSEN'S Peer Gynt (Other Place, Stratford) may be the supreme example in drama of a tragic theme treated comically. The play is about identity and vocation. Peer grows up as a Norwegian peasant, nourished on fairy-tales and projecting himself into them. After an imaginary fling at reindeer-riding and a real one at bride-rape, he falls among trolls, who are ruled by a ghastly gospel of self-sufficiency: "troll, to thyself be enough" run the traditional translations.

But Peer's own governing principle is never to commit himself to anything. He gets away from the trolls, physically if not mentally, and runs straight into something called the Great Boyg: something whose motto is "go roundabout" and who bequeaths Peer a lifelong habit of evasion.

Sometimes this comes in handy. It enables him, with the help of more yarn-spinning, to bestow on his mother one of the greatest death-scenes ever written. It also helps him get rich: as a slave-trader, typically salving his conscience by exporting missionaries as well.

All through his prosperous middle-age he prates of "being himself"; in Cairo, wandering into an asylum where the lunatics have truly taken over, he finds people who really are themselves suicidally self-obsessed.

In the last act he returns, an old man, to Norway for a brilliant replay of the events of his youth. Finally he faces death in the shape of a Button-Moulder offering neither salvation nor damnation -- Peer has been too half-hearted to deserve either -- but mere melting-down in a ladle. Peer protests that he demands better -- or worse -- but typically he excuses his sins even while proclaiming them. In fact there has only been one real thing in his life: his love for the pious girl Solveig. He once abandoned her through guilt (another case of "going roundabout"); now he returns, this time without excuses. He may yet escape the ladle.

The play is truly a comedy. There is a happy ending, even if provisional; Peer, albeit in peculiar circumstances, gets the girl. There is also unceasing humour. There may be the odd joke in Ibsen's later plays (and there are unceasing echoes of Peer, the fake idealist), but it is almost impossible to believe that the same man wrote them. This piece is buoyant, almost irresponsible, running to gags about a German echo with a Berlin accent and the unlikelihood of the hero's dying halfway through the last act. The ideal translation might be commissioned from Tom Stoppard as wit and Tony Harrison as poet.

The original is written not just in verse, but in rhyme, and I would guess that much of its impact derives from the tension between formal shape and colloquial content. David Rudkin's new version is in prose; so at least the director, Ron Daniels, informed me; there are many times when it sounds like verse.

Peer Gynt wasn't actually written to be performed, and a strange flatness sometimes betrays this. Ibsen claimed that Peer and his mother were fully-rounded characters, and so they are; their relationship is one of the richest ever dramatised. But the people never leap out at you as Shakespeare's or Chekhov's do; they obviously stand for something, even if you are not sure what. Ibsen's satire plays less exuberantly than it reads; it only comes to full theatrical life in the last act, which comes closest to morality play, but which raises the conventions to within sight of King Lear.

So at least it seems in this production, whose necessary starkness is perfectly attuned to Peer's last wanderings, but which makes his picaresque middle-age seem perfunctory. His youth is midway successful. The production is lively and intelligent and the love-scene between Peer and Solveig is breath-taking.

There are good physical reasons for the play's infrequency of production, but I have never understood why actors don't clamour to play a role that combines the attractions of Hamlet, Falstaff and Lear. The curve of Derek Jacobi's performance is that of the production: wily and beguiling at the beginning, rushed in the middle, moving and disturbing at the end.

A small supporting cast ceaselessly multiplies itself. Sinead Cusack has never been more unmistakably a star than in her doubling of Peer's major entanglements. John Carlisle does a majestic funeral orator, Derek Godfrey is an urbane messenger of death, and Robert O'Mahoney offers a delightful sketch of a sheepish bridegroom.

Meanwhile Mr Daniels's production of A Midsummer Night's Dream has come to the Barbican, with all the delights of its Victorian-theatre setting preserved, indeed positively enhanced by the modernity surrounding it. The play begins downbeat, with Mike Gwilym's Theseus remarkably low-spirited at the idea of his coming marriage. It takes the entire evening for him to cheer up; in the interim we have seen him, in his other persona as Oberon, transformed from jealous avenger to benevolent spirit.

The play's final promise of peace has rarely carried more weight; nor has Titania's revulsion when released from her spell ever been more strongly conveyed than it is here by Juliet Stevenson. The lovers have relaxed into routine, alternating caterwaulings, but the mechanicals are more real than ever. Geoffrey Hutchings's Bottom is obviously a very reliable man to whom to take your weaving, and his frightened singing when Titania first lights on him is a joy. So too is Anthony O'Donnell's Snug.

You remain in the same world when visiting Jean-Claude Grumberg's Dreyfus (Hampstead). In a Polish village in 1930 an amateur group are rehearsing a play about France's foulest hour of anti-Semitism, under a writer-director (Tom Wilkinson) whose idealism suffers as many blows from his cast as did Peter Quince's.

His senior actor (Gary Waldhorn) is a veritable Bottom for his conspicuous ambitions, his low cunning; his minor parts are Jewish as their non-professional conditions allow; given judicious editing and strong musical projection, it becomes a production with its own natural nobility.
