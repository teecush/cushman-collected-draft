Faraway places

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on a Pinter triple bill

The Cottesloe offers Other Places, an assortment of brief pieces by Harold Pinter. The first, already seen as a platform performance and heard on the radio, though not by me, is called Family Voices.

It is an exchange of letters, though we may doubt if they ever were exchanged. Ever reached the mailbox. A mother writes to, or of, her son who has left home and whom she misses. She becomes more and more distraught as the play proceeds, increasingly possessive. He tells of his experiences in a lodging-house whose proprietress, also possessive, is called Mrs Withers. (Note to scholars: in the decisive television production of Mr Pinter's The Birthday Party the smothering landlady was played by Margery Withers.)

Another resident is an older man who tries to seduce the son. Mother, coincidentally, fears--or says she fears--that he is being used as a male prostitute. Actually it is his burgeoning heterosexuality that seems to disturb her. Meanwhile his adopted household assert their claim over the boy by giving him a nickname. They call him Bobo. (The tyrant in Alan Ayckbourn's intriguing Way Upstream uses a similar tactic.)

On second thoughts, I believe the letters were sent, and that the correspondents merely carry on as if they hadn't received them.

There is a third voice, belonging to father. He is apparently dead. Mother says memorably that he died 'in lamentation and oath' and blames it on the boy. He says he's looking for his dad, and blames her for the disappearance. Anyway, from the grave or wherever he is, he speaks. A speaker at a theatre conference in Tel Aviv this year described Mr Pinter as a writer presenting Jewish themes (mainly to do with suffocating families) in Gentile settings. Family Voices can be read in this way.

Victoria Station, by contrast, takes him back to his roots, or at least to his revue sketches. A minicab controller tries to send a driver on a job, but the driver seems in a daze. Finally he vouchsafes that he has fallen in love with his current fare. The controller, at first bloodthirstily furious, succumbs to envious rapture. In my experience, conversation on cabbies' airwaves is rarely so expansive, being ritually punctuated by calls of 'roger' or just 'rog,' but Mr Pinter substitutes his own conventions.

The closer, A Kind of Alaska, is a departure for Mr Pinter as Footfalls was for Samuel Beckett. Once again we are presented with a character in a world of her own, but we are given medical evidence for her condition documented in the programme. She is a victim of sleeping sickness, rescued after 29 years by the invention of L-dopa. We watch her awakening. She displays unlikely fluency, pinpointing all the memories that the playwright needs in order to evoke her lost world, but he plays cunningly with the limits of her awareness. Her last words are 'I think I have the matter in proportion,' but she has actually misplaced one vital part of the information she has been given.

As interesting--more interesting, though kept on too tight a rein--are the reactions of her doctor and her sister: 'It is we who have suffered.' They are married. The sister calls herself a 'widow,' since the doctor has given his life to his patient. Isn't it unlikely that she would wax insistently metaphorical at such a time? Is it a metaphor? Isn't there enough in the play about the borderline of life and death already? And if it's mystification, who needs it?

The reality of the situation brings out unusual compassion in Mr Pinter. Judi Dench's performance, an adolescent's consciousness in a woman's body, is magnificent (and her first efforts to walk a brilliant pairing-down of clinical reality); she really is total theatre. Paul Rogers as the doctor is as fine, though his cab-commandant is too stolid to keep the second play alive. (It starts funny but when the laughs are withdrawn nothing replaces them.) Nigel Havers, Anna Massey and Martin Jarvis divide the other roles, expertly.

Peter Hall's direction is supremely tasteful, everything exact, nothing overdone and certainly nothing overlit. I resented it. The Hall-Pinter treatment puts the audience in a false position, awed by the intransigence of it all.

At the opposite extreme of expansiveness the Glasgow Citizens' Theatre has espoused weekly rep. (I thought critics were the only people left doing weekly rep.) O'Casey is playing now to be succeeded by de Sade, Goethe (you heard) and Brecht. They commenced with Massinger's The Roman Actor, a Jacobean piece about a decadent Roman emperor (a gift for them, and played with quivering effectiveness by Robert Gwilym) and his entourage of parasites, whores and players: the latter the only admirable people around and given, paradoxically but naturally, the least mannered performances; Ciaran Hinds made of the title role a very upright and well-spoken juvenile. The empress who tries to seduce him was flamingly done by Elsa O'Toole; one of the many witty touches in Philip Prowse's production is her treatment of despised former queens, put to work with mops. Supposedly virtuous senators were contemptuously treated, one with a speech impediment ('It is in vain to stwive against the towwent'). They died horribly, with power-drills twisted in their guts, drawing ketchup in vast quantities: an ingenious realisation of Massinger's do-your-own-thing stage direction which is 'they torment 'em.'
