Bird of mystery

THEATRE

ROBERT CUSHMAN on 'The Seagull.'

YOU can generally have a good if tepid time at a Chekhov production by concentrating on the characters and not worrying about shapes or meanings. The Seagull (Greenwich) is an exception; it is colder in temper than his later plays and more obviously invites analysis. That bird, as Nina exasperatedly says, seems to be meant as some kind of symbol.

Lying all on its own, well forward on the wide apron, it looks exposed and a little ludicrous. Some yards away Trigorin is holding forth to Nina on the tribulations of the novelist. We listen as raptly as she does, but occasionally our eyes stray to that poor forlorn symbolic property. We - with my own puzzlement I associate the director Jonathan Miller and, I suspect, Chekhov himself - are not quite sure what to make of it.

We will just have to sidestep the seagull and omit it from our pattern. For there is a pattern, though it is slow to unravel. Only when the last act begins, moving us on two years in time, does it finally become apparent that this is a play about survival. All the characters but one are survivors, and they pay in various ways for the privilege. The one exception - the suicide, Konstantin - pays of course more heavily than anyone.

We realise he is doomed from the moment he announces that he has burned the manuscript of his unsuccessful play. Having seen it virtually jeered off the stage by his own mother, he has perhaps some excuse, but it is the act of a man who will always leave his wounds open; most importantly, it is the act of an amateur. All the other men in this play are professionals. Even useless old Sorin has managed to hold down a Civil Service post for years.

The two super-pros are the novelist Trigorin - who would never destroy or forget any piece of material until he had made use of it - and the doctor Dorn. For both, the price of futile marriages. Has Women's Lib discovered The Seagull yet?

Mr Miller's production is less complete than its companion-piece, Ghosts, but then that is a simpler play, more satisfactory, but less satisfying. It is nevertheless a rich experience. Peter Eyre comes nearest to duplicating his Ghosts performance, but his pallor, as of a boy never young, in fact suits Konstantin better than it did Oswald whose "joy of life" had to be taken rather on trust. He is, here, a natural pedant, dreadfully earnest, his faculty of self-criticism so highly developed as to lead quite logically to suicide. His happiest moment is his entry, lugging the dead seagull and radiating the satisfaction of a poet who has found his metaphor.

Strangely enough, considering the family bonds that are meant to link this season, his relationship with his mother is one of the least well realised aspects of the production. (The two players embark on their big confrontation with palpable self-consciousness, as if determined to do justice to the theme.) Irene Worth's Arkadina is a ruthless study of a ruthless woman, but - perhaps inevitably, given Miss Worth's undisguisable intelligence - it has to be a calculating ruthlessness. She gives little evidence of feeling, so her prolonged weeping, when she thinks Konstantin has shot himself, seems out of key.

Robert Stephens is again superb; his acting has taken on a new muscularity which, paradoxically, enables him to project all Trigorin's flabbiness while preserving the steel core that keeps him working and so existing. There are other fine performances: from Anthony Nicholls, the essence of impotent kindliness as Dorn; from Philip Lowrie as Medviedenko; and from George Howe, who is truly too old for Sorin, but whose huddled, wheel-chaired figure makes a powerful image to launch (and partially summarise) the last act.

Maureen O'Brien's Nina is fresh, but a little blank, yielding in interest to her shadow-sister Masha, endowed with Nicola Paget's hardest edge and marrying Medviedenko as much out of spite as desperation. Antony Brown deals nicely with Shamyarev's theatrical memories, but fails to establish that his sycophancy towards the great actress (a necessary aspect of Chekhov's presentation of the all-enveloping theatrical shallowness that helps to kill Konstantin) is the obverse of his tyrannical rudeness: Shamyarev is a boor as well as a bore.

It is, I think, understandable that Mr Miller's season of Hamlet variations has not found room for Adrian Mitchell's Mind Your Head (Shaw), which transforms the Prince to the driver of a No 24 bus (Hampstead to Pimlico), his mother to a clippie, and his villainous uncle Claude to a ticket-collector who turns out to be Hitler, a figure of whom, it is fair to say, Mr Mitchell thoroughly disapproves. His other attitudes are hardly less predictable and the show (a whimsical mini-musical) is often plain silly, and occasionally - as in a parodied TV quiz - much worse. But somehow it survives; we don't very often see plays set on double-decker buses and Peter James's production fills this one with nice energetic people like Nicky Henson and Lynda Marchal. Anyway I live on the 24 bus route and I cannot be too discourteous to the first play ever to pass my door.

David Edgar's Operation Iskra (Bush) works effectively as political melodrama, but its psychological structure is shaky. Since the play is fuelled by the dissensions among its revolutionary characters, it is unfortunate that they can hardly be told apart. The men in this Paradise Foundry production (well directed by Chris Parr) are more convincing politicos than the women, who come on strong like well-intentioned actresses.

The Grand Magic Circus, led by Jerome Savary of the uniquely mournful voice and visage, are back at the Roundhouse with From Moses to Mao, with better music than we get at the Shaw, a comic-strip world history which is fun to be at even when it ceases to be fun to watch.
