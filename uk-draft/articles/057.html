The end of a flying angel

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

Perhaps it is more dramatic for women to be teases; that at any rate is how our dramatists seem to see them. Girls in plays only say No as preludes to saying Yes: this must be what is known as wish fulfilment. Last week, in David Mercer's television piece, Find Me, a young lady rejected Anthony Hopkins with such vehemence that one could only count the seconds before the camera found them in bed together. Sandra, the girl in Dennis Potter's Only Make Believe (Oxford Playhouse), holds out longer; she is working as a temp for Christopher, a playwright whom a burned hand has rendered incapable of typing his own scripts. His wife has left him, and he is taking it badly; his injury is self-inflicted, a gesture of annoyance and frustration.

For an act and a half Christopher pours out his woes, his needs, and even considerable stretches of his new television play to the nervously attendant Sandra; finally, she allows him to have her, and regrets it immediately after. Though Only Make Believe itself began life on television, the Oxford Playhouse puzzlingly claims that it is Mr Potter's first theatre play. (Why does Son of Man, which made a similar journey from screen to stage, not qualify?) In at least one respect I imagine that it worked better in the original medium; every time Christopher comes on to create, the stage darkens, half the set disappears, and with a creak, a groan and a trundle we are plunged into his imagined world.

The play within the play concerns a young man who claims to be an angel and dies in this belief, having attempted to fly out of a bedroom window, the bed being one in which a resentful housewife has attempted to give incontrovertible proof of his humanity by seducing him. Christopher's title for it is Angels Are So Few; which is also the name of one of Mr Potter's own previous works, from which the episodes we see have presumably been extracted. Nobody could accuse Mr Potter of profligacy and on most (not all) of the evidence given here quite the best thing he could have done with this earlier play was to camouflage it by writing another play round it.

In so doing, however, he has fallen into a trap. The scenes Christopher dictates and the ones in which he himself figures are written in precisely the same style; one we agree to accept as realistic, because it is plainly not aiming at stylisation and because we have been ground into expecting it of all literate mainstream playwrights. We accept it because not to do so would be too much trouble and would leave us with virtually nothing we could say we enjoyed. So we go along with the jokes that are too consciously prepared and the epigrams that never quite attain to wit and the intrusive imagery at moments of stress.

Sometimes, however, we rebel; and on this occasion Mr Potter has given us cause. He is not out to make portentous Pirandellian points about illusion and reality; the play's structure makes no pretence that Stage Left, where the angel gets unwillingly bedded, is as real as Stage Right, where Sandra meets the same fate. It offers us merely a good running gag about the words that fall so laboriously from their author's lips and so effortlessly from those of their actors. But the joke is spoiled because Christopher, when speaking for himself, is as conventionally glib as his characters. Or as most characters in most plays; I would not get at Mr Potter particularly if the situation he has conjured up did not demand something more believable.

Granted the style, he uses it rather well; Christopher has an opening monologue which crackles and a post-coital diatribe which propels the play out of its rut. It also propels John Fraser out of his; having offered an anonymous, generalised performance for most of the evening, he suddenly begins to bite. Brenda Cavendish has some good humorous moments, and some even better sullen ones, as Sandra; but both her authors (Christopher and Dennis) tend to patronise her and she seems inclined to go along with them. The best performance is given by Andre van Gyseghem as a doctor persuaded by the exigencies of one-act drama into bestowing a home call on an ambulant patient with nothing more mortal than a bandaged hand to recommend him.

It is the prerogative of children at pantomimes and other such things to despise the hero and love the clowns. Ken Campbell's Old King Cole (Shaw) pre-empts this reaction; Prince Charming is put firmly down and Buttons gets Cinderella. I have, of course, changed the names in the interests of clarity; on second thoughts, the lucky lover is less a Buttons than a junior broker's man. His main attraction is a capacious overcoat, stocked like a supermarket (with a particularly large confectionery counter) and presumably borrowed from Harpo Marx. Mr Campbell has a good memory for gags, and an unerring nose for audience participation. I would have liked a story as well, but the rest of the audience seemed happy to do without. The very bright five-year-old who accompanied me wants to go again, but someone else will have to take him.
