Exporting Amadeus

ROBERT CUSHMAN reports on a recent visit to Broadway.

A dozen years ago, in an invaluable book called 'The Season,' William Goldman analysed the Broadway phenomenon of the Snob Hit.

'The Snob Hit,' wrote Goldman, 'is rooted in two false beliefs: (1) theatre is "good" for you and (2) British is better. The first accounts for so many Snob Hits being "lofty" in either subject matter or treatment, or both.'

A decline in the British export drive, coupled with rampant protectionism on the part of American Equity, has meant a falling-off in the supply. But the current season has produced a prize specimen. I refer of course to Amadeus, written by Peter Shaffer, staged--with all its National Theatre trappings intact--by Peter Hall, provided with three British actors (Ian McKellen, Tim Curry, Jane Seymour) in its leading roles, and treating of two characters as lofty as any snob could desire: Mozart and God.

I am beginning to sound patronising, one of the two sins besetting critics who write about Shaffer (the other is being senselessly ecstatic). Contemplating this account of the hatred of the self-proclaimed mediocrity Salieri for the genius Mozart, reviewers in both London and New York have proclaimed the thing impossible on the grounds that mediocrity would be incapable of recognising genius anyway and therefore would not be jealous.

The critics have enjoyed themselves defending Mozart (they appreciate him, of course, so there's no question of their being mediocre) against Shaffer's supposed portrayal of him as a foul-mouthed buffoon. Actually--and Tim Curry's effervescent performance makes this even clearer than in London--Shaffer's Mozart, consistently right in his judgments of his own work and everybody else's, is bright but fatally tactless. I don't know what Mozart was really like, but Shaffer's version convinces for the duration of the play. As a character he wears better than Salieri.

On the assumption that it could equally well apply to any great artist, I can accept as a metaphor the idea of Mozart as God's instrument. I could even, while listening to Paul Scofield's trumpeting on the London first night of Salieri's deity-defying speech, accept it as a dramatic fact; but not thereafter.

The play's major flaw is not intellectual, but structural; it has no second act. Salieri tells us that his quarrel is not with Mozart, but with God. God, however, has not been established; so we get a succession of scenes from Mozart's last years--scenes which, by the play's own rules, are irrelevant. It doesn't matter how great, even whether, Salieri did love him; his jealousy, once established, is never explored; it might be interesting, but the play has no time for anything so vulgar.

The much-bruited rewrites undertaken for New York and to be incorporated later this year in London make no essential difference. The greatest gain is the loss of Salieri's sepulchral manservants; one lay figure the better. A sequence on 'The Magic Flute,' and its antagonising of the Freemasons, adds to the anecdote, and also, of course, to the parade of greatest hits. A final encounter between Mozart and Salieri, made conclusive of the latter's masquerade as messenger of death, can hardly crown a relationship that never existed.

The New York audience, the night I went, gave the play a standing ovation. A cynical friend maintains that Broadway audiences always do this, to justify to themselves the mountainous cost of the evening out. My experience was different; I saw houses rise only to Ian McKellen and to Jim Dale in Barnum--another visiting star in a virtuoso role.

Neither as it happens is being seen at his best. McKellen is not yet at the stage where he can float through a role, using the fact of stardom for water-wings. Salieri offers him one emotion, plus elementary quick-changes from youth to old age. He wolfs that down in about 10 minutes and goes hungry for the rest of the evening.

Visually, Peter Hall's production is handsomer than his London version, though he has mislaid the running conceit of the play as opera. The antiphonal messengers called the Venticelli now sound--as they were originally described by Martin Esslin, who should know--like bad radio drama.

'Amadeus' apart, the straight play most approved by New York critics and audiences is Paul Osborn's Morning's at Seven, a quirky small-town comedy first staged (unsuccessfully) in 1939. It shows four sisters growing old vengefully.

The precise tone, one of delicate brutality, is difficult to establish, and it has eluded this production by Vivian Matalon, one-time director of Hampstead Theatre Club, before that an actor specialising in playing Americans and now in process of becoming one. The production is graced, all too consciously, by two senior movie stars, Maureen O'Sullivan and Teresa Wright; and the play turns into a homey soporific.

I had fears that Hugh Leonard's A Life, which I caught at the end of a brief run, would be another; it does include some facile juxtapositions of past and present, but its central character--a dry and dying civil servant--is written, and was played by Roy Dotrice (in a good play at last), with invigorating tartness.

Even more surprising was Mark Medoff's Children of a Lesser God, known locally as 'the deaf play'; I had avoided it on previous visits, scarred by earlier encounters with the mixture of earnestness and sentimentality Broadway reserves for the fashionable themes of sickness, disability and death. This piece, however, is honestly emotional: a love story--in fact. Phyllis Frelich, who is herself deaf, plays an embittered rebellious girl who marries her speech-teacher, a knock-out performance by David Ackroyd, who has to speak for both of them.

Clinical drama can be its own form of escapism, and it remains true that for plays that look out at America you have to go, selectively, off Broadway. Ted Tally, whose Captain Scott play, 'Terra Nova,' was seen at Chichester last year, tries contemporary satire in Coming Attractions, which takes a single obvious idea--murder can make a man a celebrity--and gives it a thorough pounding. There are good jokes (a judge asks, 'Is this a first offence? I mean, has he ever killed 30 people before?') and, against the odds, a chilling ending when the hero goes to the chair.

There are also songs, which prompts a consideration of small musicals. Off Broadway they can run eternally; I'm Getting My Act Together and Taking It On the Road (due in London soon) has been around for years, though not as many as its faded feminist pointmaking may suggest.

A marvellous miniature show called Tintypes, a musical history of melting-pot America pre-1914, went Broadway and died. Off-off Broadway, an hour's worth of Stephen Sondheim songs, written for and cut from other shows, were strung together under the title Marry Me a Little to depict, with very little forcing, the fears and fantasies of two impoverished young Brooklynites.

Some shows can expand and live. On the coldest Christmas Day for 100 years, with both the wind and the theatres going full blast, I saw A Day in Hollywood, a Night in the Ukraine, its singalong first-half transformed to advantage, its Marxist second feeling the lack of a feasible Groucho and Harpo, but still indestructibly funny. A hit on Broadway, as it never was in the West End, the show is perhaps the oddest offering yet laid on the shrine of nostalgia.

Most current musicals subscribe in some way. The latest revival is Brigadoon, surviving mainly on Frederick Loewe's score, a succession of ballads that outclasses his later music for 'My Fair Lady.' 'Barnum' is nothing, though Jim Dale, singing while crossing a tight-rope (two new accomplishments for him), inspires patriotic as well as hospitable affection. The spectacular 42nd Street, 22 girls tapping as the curtain rises, is impossible to resist or--a day later--to remember.

'42nd Street,' though, is already yesterday's sensation. The new one is, of all things, The Pirates of Penzance: a smash last year in Central Park and now warming up the inhuman Uris Theatre. Linda Ronstadt, singing beautifully, though not acting at all, may have been the initial draw, but the real driving force is Kevin Kline, an actor extraordinarily good-looking for a comedian or extraordinarily funny for a leading man, and wildly acrobatic for either; Burt Lancaster's Crimson Pirate with vocals. His contortions, and his good humour, seem to have infected the entire cast, chorus especially.

Wilford Leach's production is eclectic, with George Rose revivifying traditional Savoy routines while a synthesiser takes over the string parts in the pit. The original may be, in all senses, amplified, but it is never (well, not very often) contradicted. Snobs go--or stay--home.
