Our old friend Enoch

Theatre

ROBERT CUSHMAN on new plays by Howard Barker, Nigel Williams and Philip Martin.

HOWARD BARKER'S new one, The Loud Boy's Life (Warehouse), takes us back six or seven years to the days when no fringe playlet was complete without an impersonation of Enoch Powell.

In this production the familiar turn is done by Hugh Fraser, who captures admirably the fateful braying tones and the pedantic periods, as of a thinking man's brimstone preacher. As it happens Mr Fraser is not the evening's Powell figure, but only a madman who thinks he is, having found the Napoleon beat to be overpopulated.

The man he imitates is Ezra Fricker, played by Clive Merrison, who nobly refrains from obtrusive mannerisms. He develops an anthropoid hunch of the shoulders and a thick, oily manner of speech, but nothing you could hang a writ on; if his performance remains uncharacteristically lifeless, it could be because he is attempting to play from the inside a role that has not been provided with any.

We first see Ezra as the guest of honour at a society of industrialists, lawyers, novelists and other reactionaries, who want him to take over the country on their behalf, he being "the loud boy," the only Tory with popular support. He refuses, largely it seems because it's past his bedtime.

Flashback to the war: Ezra is just out of college and into uniform, picnicking in Greenwich Park with a party of Fascist sympathisers, watching the docks burn and failing to be seduced by a friend's wife. The fighting done, he finds she no longer wants him and, what's more, has become a Communist. He vows vengeance, in the form of political success.

Flash-forward to his one chance of achieving it. The country is in crisis, the people shouting for Fricker. A top-secret, top-Cabinet meeting decides by a majority of three to two not to offer him the leadership. (A lady who resembles Mrs Thatcher is particularly anti.) He hears the news while opening a fete in the gardens of a mental hospital, and declines to go back to London and try force. Last scene of all: his ashes lie in state in St Paul's and people pay tribute, or not, according to their predilections.

Ezra's refusal of arbitrary power -- in the name of legality, parliamentary democracy and catching the last bus home -- seems to me entirely to his credit, but Mr Barker's tone, which is scornful, does not persuade me that this is the point of the play. The trouble is, he can't work up a dramatically justified hatred either.

Every reviewer in London has pointed out the gaping flaw, but even every reviewer in London can be right. To wit: The action rests on Ezra's supposed popularity, and we are never shown a reason for it. Mr Barker may retort that we already know, but the play lasts nearly three hours and that is a long time not to be told things. Mr Powell's appeal rested less on racism than on the belief that he had the guts to say unpalatable things. That belief may well have been mistaken, but it must have stemmed from something. Ezra, who speaks in meaningless generalities when he speaks at all, gives no clue. His wife hates him, by the way, and much is made, yet again, of the discovery that conservatives have sexual hang-ups.

The irritant is that the play contains some of Mr Barker's best writing. The play of wills, the icy contempt, of the secret caucus, is brilliant -- as is the indifference of the caretaker who lets them in and out. (There, for a moment, is political analysis dramatised.) The club dinner goes on and on, but it includes a glistening Donald Sumpter as an author squirming on the table, mimicking a housewife on heat. A female infiltrator points out the dreadful misogyny of all this; we are unlikely not to have noticed for ourselves, but at least her interruption, like the whole scene, is well orchestrated.

Ezra defying fate by aiming darts at the ace of spades is another strong moment. Howard Davies's production, though more than a bit portentous, makes the most of all of them. The various ladies are well chiselled by Jill Baker, Cheryl Hall and Mateylok Gibbs. Mr Barker, though, is throttling himself. Perhaps he was drawn to Ezra by their common inability to believe that an enemy could be worth understanding.

Nigel Williams's Trial Run (Young Vic) is also rather suicidal. Two Asians, victims of official mistreatment, hold two salesgirls, a student and a Securicor man prisoner in the basement of Woolworths. We are all guilty of looking the other way from, for example, the Southall disturbances, at which one captor was run in for assaulting a policeman; and the two intruders put the security guard, in himself a totally inoffensive teenager, on mock trial for our crimes.

There is enough generalised truth in this, and enough specific nonsense, to keep us interested; there is also, with guns being waved around and the police laying siege, built-in suspense. But the trial hardly ever gets going; one jailer is too furious and the other, mocking his own racial stereotyping ("oh golly gosh"), too fond of the sound of his own voice. His tirades get us resenting him, and then worrying about it. That's a complex effect, sure enough, but the writing is sketchy, and on this exposed stage weaknesses of text, acting and direction compound one another. One character is supposed to spend the play in terror of his life, but he never looks very bothered.

Bottom of the heap is Philip Martin's Thee and Me (Lyttelton). It is the year 2040, and natural disaster, maybe compounded by human recklessness, has reduced Britain to a dustbowl. It has also set the human race back, theatrically speaking, by a couple of centuries. A race of patriarchs walks the earth, guarding its water supply and its family, dispensing with its definite articles, and scheming to marry off its daughters to horse-traders called Jeremiah Stiggins. The characters live in giant disused water-pipes, which at least enables Sue Plummer to design a spectacular set, flanked by a heap of petrified motorcars.

A more recognisable civilisation, referred to as "state," flourishes to the north and representatives of both orders are felled in the end. The audience can hardly feel like choosing between them. Maybe there was an element of mockery in the original which attracted the director, Michael Rudman, but if so his production has failed to bring it out.

A bug kept me from Rose (Duke of York's), which from all accounts was my misfortune. It will be reviewed next week.
