Making you blub

THEATRE

by ROBERT CUSHMAN

TERENCE RATTIGAN'S In Praise of Love was once a double bill and, like most, not very popular. As is common practice (cf. 'The Bells of Hell') its more successful half was torn from its mate and forced to live alone, first on Broadway and now at Bromley's new Churchill Theatre (christened perhaps in memory of the Blitz; it is certainly as subterranean as an air-raid shelter, though more comfortable).

The new play has been permitted to retain the family name, which in fact suits it better than did its former title, 'After Lydia.' It is a piece in which theme is more important than character. Plot comes in last though what little there is is deftly handled; the exposition relayed to a patient best friend (American as such sticks always are in posh plays now, perhaps because Englishmen can no longer afford to travel long enough to get out of touch) needs a determined swallow, but there is nothing actually painful about it. Though the play must have been padded to fill out an evening, the swelling is not easy to detect, and the tension remains.

Lydia is an Estonian refugee, married just after the war to a London literary critic, and now dying of leukaemia. She refuses to tell him on the grounds that he, as a public-school Englishman, is quite unfit to cope, either practically or emotionally, with the prospect of losing her. He in fact knows the score, thinks she doesn't, and is trying to hide it from her by intensifying his natural boorishness.

Lydia has altogether to do a good deal of suffering and Sylvia Syms sometimes sinks beneath it. She bobs up again, though, in the scenes that obviously lie closest to the dramatist's heart, denouncing the society in which she finds herself ("English bad form lies not in showing emotions but in having any emotions at all") and brushing aside ideological differences as irrelevant to human feelings.

In its beleaguered old age West End drama is becoming very didactic; Rattigan's heroine and his title make up a statement of belief. A statement, too, which he has the craft to justify. Lydia's husband says of good writers that "they all make you blub somewhere" and proceeds to make the point on his own creator's behalf. He has begun, inevitably, really to love his wife when about to lose her, and Alfred Marks releases the banked-up hesitant tears to fine effect. He, too, is miscast (he might pass for a self-made bookman, but never for a Wykehamist and Oxonian), but in the end it hardly matters. If this piece and 'Cause Celebre' are indeed to be Rattigan's last, they stand in the best tradition of final plays: summaries and experiments.

At the other extreme Nigel Baldwin comes on very strong with Sudlow's Dawn. With this play Upstairs and 'Once a Catholic' below, the Royal Court is accommodating two attention-grabbing playwrights; both are in residence, which suggests sharp thinking on the part of some committee or other.

Mr Baldwin's title is ambiguous; Sudlow is a mild young mental case, rescued from rock-bottom cafe employment to attend on a young lady of the suburbs (father a councillor) who is also, theoretically at least, a revolutionary and sees him as a cause. This agent of Sudlow's awakening is actually called Dawn; you will see that she is, strictly speaking, the titular figure, and she does indeed get most of Mr Baldwin's attention. She spouts and paints slogans, rows with her parents, rails at her carefully chosen working-class boyfriend for not living up to her preconceptions, idly seduces Sudlow, gets VD from him and goes to jail. (There are intermediate stages I've omitted.) Coming out, she goes to work in a strip-club, finds she can't take it and goes home, though not before her tender-mindedness has been exposed by a fellow-artiste.

The acting around here is very thin, but then so is the writing. Mr Baldwin goes on telling us things about Dawn which his first scene - the cafe pick-up - has made brilliantly clear. Meanwhile he skimps on Sudlow: a pity since Karl Johnson's frayed nervous puzzlement is riveting. ("I hate bringing people bad news," he says, announcing a scarcity of bacon sandwiches.)

If Mr Baldwin's point is that the dispossessed keep getting elbowed out and about by their self-appointed saviours he is party to the offence himself. (Actually he is unfair to Dawn; some of her activities sound useful, but they are given only passing mention.) Still, he never romanticises and, until the last scenes, you can't stop listening to him.

Part of Michael Hastings's thesis in For the West is that Amin plays up to his newspaper image: a point the President proved abundantly and fittingly last week by shooting actors in Uganda while Mr Hastings's view of him (mad and murderous, yes, but partly our creation and largely our responsibility) took the stage of the Cottesloe. History, in these circumstances, made drama pale, though, given Mr Hastings's emphasis, it was possible to applaud the faultless authenticity of the two British colonial types (Basil Henson upstanding and Roger Milner cowering, both with dignity) who made up half the cast.

Mr Hastings takes Amin seriously. Brecht in Schweyk in the Second World War (Warehouse) was less responsible; his Hitler is a strutting marionette (Nickolas Grace's eyeball-rolling is spectacular) anxious for the love of the little man.

When Schweyk, who is that person, meets Adolf he defies him, though the heroism of this action is limited (a) by the scene which is Russia with the Germans on the run and (b) by the mood which is allegorical. Early on, in occupied Czechoslovakia, he is concerned to keep his nose clean while still being moderately subversive. With these contradictory aims it is hardly surprising he gets in trouble, thereby impairing his mythological status as a survivor.

What Brecht was at is unclear, except to serve up individually funny scenes to help the war effort; in this he succeeds. Michael Williams's Schweyk is much influenced, to his benefit and our pleasure, by north-country comics; there is a bit of Eric Morecambe in him and a lot of Ted Ray.
