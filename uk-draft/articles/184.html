Down With Clowns

Robert Cushman · The Observer · Review · 1978-03-05

WHEN A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum was in trouble in Washington, its fortunes were saved by the addition of a new opening number, a sort of declaration of intent:

Nothing with kings,
Nothing with crowns,
Bring on the lovers, liars and clowns.

The new musical Kings and Clowns (Phoenix), as you may gather from its title, sets out to shatter such rigid demarcations but makes, by gross default, a powerful case for artistic apartheid. After 400 years Sir Philip Sidney, who strongly objected to having clowns thrust in by the heels among tragical matters, has been vindicated.

The aim of Leslie Bricusse's show seems to be to demonstrate that every king is actually a clown, and Henry VIII an ordinary bloke with six wives or a kingdom just like you or I. One has, though, to be tentative in judging its intentions; this show too ran into trouble on the road, less happily resolved. It arrives in town, for one thing, with no one credited for its book.

Once upon a time someone known anagrammatically as Tom Rimer is believed to have been involved; those with a head for this sort of thing have declared him to be Lara Murtin, and I would like to believe him responsible for a powerful speech in which Henry declares his need for a woman who will love him through all his physical infirmities, which are graphically described. This, in its grotesquely credible self-pity, must be what the show was designed to be about. That, however, was probably a long time ago; what is evident now is a desire to get through the evening somehow, anyhow.

I seem to remember that Peter Hall was once slated to direct, and Bernard Delfont to present, a musical called "Great Harry." I know that years ago Sandy Wilson wrote a show on the same subject. More recently on Broadway Richard Rodgers had a King Hal musical called "Rex," short-lived and soporific.

This catalogue of doom suggests that there may be something wrong with this king as a subject. You can have a go at Tudor history and get bogged down, or you can pretend that all Henry ever did was get married. This is the way of Kings and Clowns and it is very monotonous, the same situation repeated half a dozen times, with a scoreboard at the side of the stage to ram the point home and stop us hoping for anything else to happen.

Well, they try. The past and future wives are kept around, a chorus of blithe spirits, to comment on whichever happens to be the present incumbent. Halfway through Act Two, with Catherine Parr at last installed, we are whisked alarmingly back to number one. The ladies have been well cast and each goes through her assigned routine with spunk and, I imagine, the desperate hope of something better next time.

Dilys Watling, as Anne Boleyn, a red-feathered vamp, sort of strips and Anna Quayle, one of the worst-served talents on our stage, delivers Anne of Cleves's bad jokes with such ferocious aplomb that it seems churlish to wish her some good ones. Frank Finlay manages to keep his head above these unaccustomed waters, trouping spryly and tunefully enough to earn a booking with Morecambe and Wise next Christmas. (Have they done Henry VIII yet?) Ray C. Davis barnstorms bravely as the king's jester and, I suppose, his alter ego.

That last suggestion, I know, sounds pretentious, but it is one of the show's troubles that it is not brave enough to be wholly mindless. The man is a fool-and-bring-on-the-girls idea harks back to Mr Bricusse's big success with Anthony Newley, "Stop the World--I Want to Get Off." That was a dreadful show, an idiot's idea of satire; but it had a line and kept to it and some took it for art.

Traces remain here; John Napier, bringing with him some left-over horses' heads from "Equus," has designed a set that seems meant to be sparse and spectacular and is just tacky. (The musical really is an area where looks can kill.) If the choreography is the worst single ingredient of the evening, the direction (by Mel Shapiro) can hardly have provided much incentive; the opening has the cast playing boomp-a-daisy on a strip of stage too small for them--which at least gives the audience fair warning.

As for Mr Bricusse, he perhaps is the real Tom Rimer--he depends on jingle rather than sense to hold his lyrics together, so can hardly afford such clinkers as "morality" and "fidelity." His music varies from the tinkle to the portentous, a combination believed to have great efficacy in the winning of Oscars, and known in pop circles as middle of the road; the show, bankrolled and masterminded by the American Hilliard Elkins, is just middle of the ocean, a damp, dangerous place to be.

In the first scene of Robert Holman's Outside the Whale (Bush) a short passage from George Orwell's "Down and Out in Paris and London" is read to us; it sounds concerned but faintly patronising. Later a clerk who has nicked a few copies from the Gollancz office prepares to sell them in order to survive; that, it is hinted, is the only social good the book can do.

This, and a few noises on the grapevine, suggest that the play is an attack on Orwell. He may be, in the words of his most famous essay, "inside the whale" ("very comfortable, cosy, homelike"--and oblivious), but there are others who cannot afford the luxury. Mr Holman, however, is a gentle as well as an allusive writer and his central scenes, which show Orwell actually on the road with a tramp, are far from lethal. Obviously the two come from different worlds; certainly they cannot communicate, maybe (but so what?) Orwell is working out his own hang-ups, but the real interest lies in the humour and tenderness of the writing--particularly in a prize scene where the tramp, wrapping himself in scowls and in newspaper, takes a much dreaded bath.

Chris Parr's direction is superb here, as are the performances of Godfrey Jackman and Mark Penfold. The time-scheme is fragmented and the play itself fragmentary, but there are other scenes which show the same mastery of muted domestic discord (though protracted here) as Mr Holman's "German Skerries." He is an oddball writer, and valuable.

Julian Mitchell's Half Life has arrived in the West End, still witty, still indeterminate, unless you can take one Don's disappointment as a metaphor for the failure of a civilisation. (Some can, apparently.) John Gielgud is now in command, though still too kind for his role; Lockwood West has arrived as his manservant, Richard Pearson has put down roots; otherwise the supporting acting has gone off.
